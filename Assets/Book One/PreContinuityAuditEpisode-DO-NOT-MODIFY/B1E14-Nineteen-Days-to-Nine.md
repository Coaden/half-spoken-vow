# Episode Fourteen: Nineteen Days to Nine

Episode Fourteen: Nineteen Days to Nine

Yolanda slid every safe-room form back across the desk unsigned.

Behind her, the moon calendar hung on a nail with the three required nights boxed in red. The first box was eleven days away.

"I checked twice," Yolanda said. "Then I checked the old book because the old book sometimes has an answer the new one got too embarrassed to print. It doesn't."

June kept her hands on the stack. "Application for emergency placement."

"Requires a membership number."

"Guest housing request."

"A guest has to be sponsored by a member in good standing, and guest housing specifically excludes the three nights."

"Medical waiver."

"Waives the fee. It doesn't create a room."

"Temporary initiation."

Yolanda's face changed. "There is no temporary initiation."

"I know. I wanted you to say it."

The office at the Hall was warm from a radiator nobody could turn down. Beyond the door came folding chairs scraping the wooden floor and the coffee urn making its tired metal noise. Presence everywhere. Bodies that could smell June's fear and knew exactly why she was asking.

Yolanda lowered her voice anyway.

"I can put you in my basement the night before. I can sit with you until it starts. I cannot keep you there once it does. My grandchildren sleep upstairs."

"I'm not asking you to."

"I know. That's why I offered."

June squared the forms. "Read the denial into the minutes."

"June."

"The Local has three hundred and eleven members, a housing roster, and safe rooms because every one of those members loses control on a public schedule. I am asking for the record to state what happens to a person of member blood who isn't on the roster."

"Marta won't like it."

"Marta told me to take standing where I could find it."

Yolanda drew the minute book toward her. She turned to a clean line and read while she wrote.

"On application by June Havlik, uninitiated and not entered on the roster, the recording secretary finds no charter process for assignment of a Local Nine safe room. Emergency placement requires membership. Guest placement excludes the change nights. No waiver creates membership or assigns a room."

She stopped.

"Keep going," June said.

"There is nothing else."

"That's the point. State that no official route exists."

Yolanda looked at her for a long moment. In this room she could not pretend the omission was administrative. June's blood was a fact between them.

She wrote the last sentence and read it aloud.

"No official route exists for the uninitiated."

The absence became a record.

It did not become a room.

June left with eleven days, four rejected forms, and nine hundred and forty dollars in the rent envelope inside her bag. The money had been eleven hundred before bus fare, archive copies, and three meals she had bought instead of admitting she had missed them.

In the main room, a man she did not know was taking his name off a paper list taped beside the coffee urn.

"You giving up your slot?" somebody asked him.

"Switching with my brother."

He smelled afraid before he said it.

June stopped at the urn and read the list. Room assignments. Each line had a member number, three dates, and a signature. One entry had been crossed out and rewritten twice.

"Is a slot transferable?" she asked Yolanda when the secretary came out behind her.

"Within a household, sometimes. The room doesn't care whose name is on the door. The Local does."

"So physically, yes."

"June, don't."

"I asked a physical question."

The man by the list had heard. He met June's eyes, then looked at her bag.

An hour later she found him at the hardware store on Butler Street, loading bags of ice melt beneath a display of plastic snow shovels. He had named the location because his cousin worked at the Hall and did not work here.

Before money, June made him describe the room. Concrete walls. Steel door. Floor drain. Water line. An attendant in the corridor who checked the lock twice. His voice steadied over the materials. The room was infrastructure built because three nights had to be survived on schedule.

"What are you using instead?" she asked.

"My brother has space."

Fear rose. The lie could not hold between them, and he knew she smelled it.

"I'm buying the room," June said. "I'm not buying your explanation."

"I got three nights," he said. "Cotter Street, room six. You want all three?"

"The change is three nights."

"Some people chance the first."

"Do you?"

"We're not talking about me."

He was a member. He could smell that she knew he was lying, and she could smell the reason was fear rather than greed. It made the negotiation worse, not easier.

"How much?" June asked.

"Eight hundred."

That left one hundred and forty dollars for rent, which was another way of saying it did not leave rent.

"Five hundred."

"Seven fifty."

"Six, and you sign a receipt naming all three dates and room six."

"Cash."

"Cash with a receipt."

"Six seventy-five."

June thought of the locked drawer in the back of her head where the numbers lived. Rent. Lost December work. Eleven days. A body she had never been taught to house.

"Six fifty," she said. "You read the receipt aloud in front of the clerk."

"Why?"

"Because if you sell it twice, I want a human witness who can identify you."

He almost smiled. "You're Marta's niece."

"I'm nobody on the roster. That's why we're here."

They did it at the service counter between key cutting and propane exchange. June wrote the terms in plain language. The man gave his member number, room six, and all three dates. The clerk witnessed his signature because June bought a roll of duct tape she did not need and asked him to.

The man counted the cash twice.

"You understand they won't honor this if they catch it."

"Will the key work?"

"Yes."

"Will the room hold?"

"Yes."

"Then they can decide what to call it afterward."

He read the receipt aloud. June recorded him. The clerk said he had witnessed the payment and looked as if retail had finally delivered on its worst promise.

The man handed June a brass key stamped with a six.

The practical relief was immediate and ugly. For the first time since Pratt mentioned the moon, she had a door built to hold what her body would do.

She also had six hundred and fifty dollars less.

Kenneth Boyle was waiting outside beside his truck.

He wore a welding jacket with a burn along one sleeve. Cold had reddened his hands. He did not ask whether she had made the purchase.

"Show me the receipt."

"No."

"Then tell me the name."

"Why?"

"Because there are six people desperate enough to sell a room this month and four of them are dangerous."

June opened her bag and let him read without releasing the paper.

Kenneth got to the signature and shut his eyes.

"How much?"

"Six fifty."

"Rent money."

"My money."

"I didn't say it wasn't."

"Yolanda put the absence in the minutes. There is no route. I found one."

"You found a man with his back against a wall."

"So did he."

Kenneth looked toward the store doors. The man had not come out.

"He asked me first," June said.

"That doesn't make it clean."

"I didn't say clean. I said I have somewhere that won't open when I hit the door."

"A safe-room place isn't property. He can't sell it."

"Then the Local can return my money and assign me one properly."

"You know they won't."

"That's why the receipt names the thing they say can't be sold. Either they honor it or explain why the room is empty while I have no placement."

Kenneth's silence said he could smell that she believed it. Belief did not put the seller somewhere safe.

"You could file the receipt with Yolanda," he said.

"And trigger discipline before I use the room."

"Or force the Local to solve the conflict."

"In eleven days?"

Kenneth did not pretend. "Probably not."

June unfolded the receipt and read the consideration aloud. Six hundred and fifty dollars for three dates. The seller had represented that he possessed another safe arrangement. His body had contradicted that representation before the ink dried.

"I knew he was lying," she said.

"Yes."

"I bought it anyway."

"Yes."

"Would you have stopped me?"

"If you'd asked me before paying."

"I didn't."

"That's becoming the expensive part."

June put the receipt away. Kenneth was not accusing her of theft. He was refusing the cleaner story in which a valid receipt made both parties equally free.

"Find out where he plans to go," she said. "Don't threaten him. Don't tell the Hall yet."

"And if the answer is nowhere?"

The brass key weighed almost nothing in her bag.

"Then I decide with the whole price visible."

Kenneth asked whether she wanted him to stand witness if that meant returning the key. June said yes before independence could disguise itself as principle again.

"Name what you're agreeing to," he said.

"You find his actual plan. You tell me. If he has nowhere safe, you witness the return and the fact that I waive repayment."

"And your room?"

"Still unresolved."

Kenneth repeated the terms. They had named no supernatural debt. The promise mattered anyway. He would do the work, and June had allowed another person into the decision before the deadline made choice impossible.

"Give him the slot back," he said.

"And go where?"

"I'll find something."

"In eleven days? You had thirty-one years to find a process for people like me."

He accepted that without flinching, which did not make it kinder.

"The receipt works," June said. "The key works. I have a room."

"That man has three children," Kenneth said. "He has never missed a protected cycle in twenty years."
