# Episode Twelve: Instrument

Episode Twelve: Instrument

Constance had numbered every sentence Elias spoke before he reached the room.

The transcript lay open on the long table at Fourth Avenue. Twelve pages, certified by Aloysius Pratt, with Constance's narrow hand in the margin. One through six beside the vow. Seven beside his statement that he was not reading. Eight beside the first answer he had given June Havlik after it was done.

*I know.*

That one had been underlined.

"Sit with me, my dear," Constance said.

She occupied the chair at the head of the table and had arranged the room as though the meeting were a courtesy. Tea in a silver pot. Two cups. Pratt at the far end with three folders and a legal pad. The blinds were still closed although sunset had passed an hour ago.

Elias sat.

"Would you like tea?"

"No, thank you."

"Mr. Pratt, will you read the present position?"

Pratt opened the first folder. He had removed his pocket square for the evening and seemed diminished without it.

"Clause one was interrupted before execution by a valid first half spoken before the accepted witness. The clause remained suspended until the thirtieth day. Miss Havlik has since filed a satisfaction under the period definition of residence. I accepted and recorded it."

"Correctly?" Constance asked.

"Entirely correctly."

"There is pleasure in your voice."

"It was good work."

"Then we must not begrudge you the pleasure. Continue."

Pratt looked down again. "The first half exposes the speaker until answered or formally abandoned. Trustee Marrow's ordinary protections are suspended. Any party to the Articles with standing may compel his performance, bring a claim against his office, or name him into an obligation the Trust would then be required to answer. Rival trusts with derivative standing may petition to do the same."

"How many?"

"Three have colorable claims. Seven more could manufacture one with competent counsel."

"And have they noticed?"

"Two requested certified copies of the transcript this morning."

Elias watched Constance make a small mark beside sentence four. She did not need the half-glasses low on her nose. She had adopted them before Pratt's grandfather was born because men answered an older woman's glasses more candidly than they answered her face.

"Read the final consequence," she said.

Pratt turned a page. "A completed attachment would enter both registers, create permanent standing, and prevent either party from forming another attachment. It would also disqualify Trustee Marrow from any future office requiring an oath. As the attachment remains incomplete, none of those consequences has vested."

"And the Trust's interest?"

Pratt hesitated.

"Read what I dictated."

He read. "The Marrow Trust has determined that a completed attachment is necessary to preserve continuity beyond the present century. No substitute instrument has been identified. The Havlik attachment is the only presently recorded attachment capable of satisfying that requirement."

The words entered the room in Pratt's careful daytime voice and stayed there.

Elias had known the Trust was contracting. Properties sold one at a time. Offices left unfilled. Petitions answered through increasingly remote human hands. He had not known Constance had reduced the condition to one sentence.

"Necessary," he said.

"Yes."

"You did not say preferable."

"I did not."

Pratt moved his pen but did not write.

Constance poured tea into one cup. "You have served this Trust for one hundred and twenty years. I think you have earned the courtesy of the whole fact. We cannot carry our holdings into the next century in their present form. Human trustees die too quickly. Appointed successors have begun refusing the office. The other houses are purchasing our obligations at a discount and waiting. A completed attachment would place one record beyond succession. It would give us continuity neither death nor appointment could interrupt."

"At Miss Havlik's expense."

"At yours also."

"That does not improve it."

"No," Constance said. "It does not."

Pratt looked between them with the strained neutrality of counsel who had just learned the client was mortal in a way not covered by probate.

Elias put his right hand on the transcript. The old mill scar crossed three lines of his own testimony.

"You drafted the Articles to prevent this exact use."

"I drafted the Articles to prevent a slaughter."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the answer." She lifted her cup. "I wrote every original line. I accepted every later construction. I remember the reason for each clause and the name of every person whose conduct made it seem necessary. I did not imagine that necessity would remain while the people changed around it. That is my failure of imagination, not evidence of bad faith."

"Miss Havlik will find the amendment trail."

"I hope she does."

Pratt's pen stopped.

"Mrs. Marrow," he said, "the Trust's position has been that the operative instrument is continuous from eighteen seventy-one."

"It is."

"The word continuous may be asked to carry more than one meaning."

"Then you will prepare all of them."

Pratt wrote that down with considerably less pleasure than he had shown over residence.

Constance turned the transcript so sentence seven faced Elias. "You stated that you were not reading."

"I was not."

"You spoke before Father Delisle."

"Yes."

"You knew it would suspend the clause and expose you."

"Yes."

"You knew the attachment was the first viable one in nineteen years."

"I knew it was the first recorded one."

"A distinction Mr. Pratt would admire."

Pratt did not look up.

Constance touched the underlined answer. "Why did you do it?"

The room became very quiet.

She could require the answer in writing. His office was hers to examine and his reports were Trust property. She could dictate the question, have Pratt serve it, and compel a response before midnight. Exposure made other parties dangerous. Office made Constance sufficient.

Elias said, "You have the authority to demand a report."

"I do."

"You have not demanded one."

"I have not."

"Why?"

"Because you chose not to read."

It was the only kindness she could have offered him and the one he least wanted.

Constance removed her glasses. "For a century, whenever this Trust required an act that should have injured your conscience, you read it. You read accurately. You did not embellish. You did not refuse. Last Monday you closed the folder and spoke a sentence of your own. I would like to know why. I will not turn that first act of private judgment into a compulsory report tonight."

"Tonight."

"Exact as ever."

She nodded to Pratt, who opened the second folder.

"There is another matter," he said. "Miss Havlik has met with Tess Renn."

Elias did not move.

"How do you know?"

"Ms. Renn requested three certified amendment records from county archives forty minutes after the meeting ended. She used Miss Havlik's standing in the request."

"Did Miss Havlik authorize that?"

"The request says she agreed to examine the case. It specifically disclaims any promise to act."

Constance's mouth altered by less than a smile. "She has learned quickly."

"Tess Renn's case concerns clause two," Elias said.

"Among other things."

"I executed that disposition."

"I remember."

He remembered too. Lenora Renn's objection, correctly framed and four days late. The precise failure of acceptance. The room at Sewickley. Tess outside it, striking the locked door with the flat of her hand while Elias read the construction that made the lock irrelevant.

He had preserved every word in a private record because the official one called the clause satisfied and said nothing about the sound from the corridor.

Constance slid the third folder toward him.

"Rival petitions," she said. "Three drafts. None filed. Each party wants a different performance from you. We may oppose them, settle them, or permit one to proceed when its use becomes advantageous."

"You intend to spend my exposure."

"I intend not to waste it. There is a difference."

"Not to the instrument."

"No. Instruments are wonderfully free of moral vocabulary. That is why people like us employ them."

Pratt closed his legal pad. The sound was small and final.

Constance dictated for the minutes. "The senior trustee declines, at this time, to compel disclosure of Trustee Marrow's motive. His exposure is acknowledged as a present liability and reserved as an instrument for future use. Counsel will monitor all petitions. No action will be taken against June Havlik's freedom to answer, refuse, or remain silent."

Pratt read it back exactly. Constance approved it. Elias did not.

"Add a condition," he said. "No petition will be settled by requiring performance from me that acts upon Miss Havlik without her knowledge."

Pratt looked to Constance.

"Exposure does not give you authority to demand that," she said.

"It is a condition of my cooperation."

"Your cooperation may not be necessary."

"Then the condition costs the Trust nothing."

Constance regarded him over her cup. He had learned that construction from her: make refusal more expensive to state than acceptance.

"Put it in the minutes, Mr. Pratt."

Pratt wrote and read it back.

"You made her knowledge a requirement," Constance said. "Not her consent."

"Her refusal requires no procedure. Knowledge gives her the chance to make it."

"Do you imagine rival counsel will honor the distinction?"

"No. I imagine you will."

Affection showed plainly in her face. It did not reassure him.

"You are becoming inconvenient, my dear."

"I have been reliably informed that exposure does that."

Pratt read the added condition into the minutes a second time. Constance required Elias to confirm it represented his instruction. He did. A private desire to protect June had become a written limit the Trust would have to work around.

That did not make it safe. It made the danger legible, which was the only protection his office still understood.

She gathered the transcript into order, tapping the pages once against the table.

"Would you like to know what I shall have to do to you, my dear?"

"I would rather not."

"Yes," Constance said. "That is probably kindest."
