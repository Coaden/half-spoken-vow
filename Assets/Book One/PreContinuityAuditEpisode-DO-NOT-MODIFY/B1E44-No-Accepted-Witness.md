# Episode Forty-Four: No Accepted Witness

Episode Forty-Four: No Accepted Witness

June put the Hall roster, Trust register, and parish list on one table. The only name common to all three was Delisle's.

It had taken six hours and three rooms to make that sentence true.

At the parish house, Delisle certified his succession list and his permanent refusal. At Fourth Avenue, Pratt printed every continuing office the Trust could recognize as independent. At the Hall, Marta opened the roster only after June stated that inspection created no membership claim.

Now the documents occupied the records-room table under separate paperweights, as if one list might contaminate another by touch.

Marta read the member criteria first. An accepted witness had to be present, capable of smelling borne kinship, and recognized by open vote.

"Capable personally?" June asked. "Or capable through member witnesses?"

"Personally under the current construction."

"Show me where."

Marta read the vote that adopted the construction after a bride's family disputed whether a human witness could recognize fear. The Hall had required the witness's own body to bear the relevant facts.

"Can the meeting reverse it?"

"Yes, after notice and a full cycle."

"Emergency vote?"

"Can waive notice. Cannot manufacture the cycle of witnessed presence."

Pratt read the Trust criteria. The witness had to hold a continuing office, preserve a written register, and be independent of both parties.

"Can a human hold the office?" June asked.

"Yes."

"Can the office be created today?"

"Yes. Independence cannot. A newly created office funded or appointed by either party fails the test."

"What if both appoint jointly?"

"Then the holder depends on both parties rather than neither."

Every shortcut preserved the function that created it.

Delisle read the parish succession. His office met both tests by the custodial agreement.

"And your personal refusal does not terminate recognition?"

"No. I remain accepted and unwilling."

"Can an unwilling witness be compelled?"

Pratt answered first. "Not to certify a vow. Compelled witnessing would defeat the witness condition itself."

Marta agreed. A room could require presence for a vote. It could not make a body honestly bear consent.

June recorded the agreement. Nobody would solve the missing witness by spending Delisle.

"Candidates," June said.

They tested every name. Yolanda possessed presence but was an officer of the Local. Pratt preserved writing but represented the Trust. Nadia kept an independent ledger but served a Trust house. Kearse held rival standing and failed independence.

June made them test categories before individuals. Retired Local officers still carried membership and failed independence. Former Trust staff lacked continuing office. Human judges held office and registers but could not smell borne kinship or receive a lineage vote within nine days. Clergy from neighboring parishes lacked the accepted custodial succession.

"A child of both lines?" June asked.

Marta said kinship could satisfy presence. Pratt said birth supplied no written office. Appointing one now failed independence.

"A bonded pair?"

No completed attachment presently held an eligible office. The rarity that made June valuable also removed the obvious bridge.

"A cutter?"

Vera preserved a commission register and could read bodily records, but her work was a crime against both institutional records. Neither would accept her without first undoing the rule that defined independence.

Each candidate appeared on two lists at most.

June drew three circles on a legal pad. Hall presence. Trust office. Independence. Names crowded every overlap except the center, where Delisle stood alone and crossed himself out.

"What about a witness accepted for this act only?" she asked.

Marta said temporary recognition was still recognition and required the full cycle. Pratt said a temporary office ended with the act and therefore was not continuing.

The two systems had designed their tests to prevent a convenient witness. They had also prevented an emergency one.

"Appoint somebody," June said.

Marta shook her head. "Standing cannot be appointed into a body."

Pratt said, "The Trust can appoint an office, but the Hall would still need to recognize the holder in presence."

"Then recognize one."

"Recognition is not saying a name at a meeting," Marta said. "The candidate must stand through a cycle where members bear who they are, what pressures act on them, and whether their claimed independence survives."

"The moon cycle ends before the deadline."

"The following meeting does not."

"Call it at dawn."

"After three nights, half the roster cannot safely attend and the vote would be challengeable for excluding incapacitated members."

The public calendar that protected members from secret urgency now made June's emergency impossible to rush.

"Not before the deadline," Marta said. "Initiation requires a cycle."

June turned to Delisle. "Delegate the parish office."

"My refusal is personal and my custodianship is not delegable during life."

"Can your office recognize an assistant?"

"For preservation, yes. Not as successor or vow witness."

"Can the diocese appoint a co-pastor?"

"Ordinary appointment would not transfer custodial acceptance."

"Can you become medically incapable?"

Delisle stared at her.

"I am testing the rule, not threatening you."

"Incapacity transfers emergency custody only after certification by two physicians and the diocese. It does not make the acting custodian an accepted vow witness."

June entered that route as failed and hated that desperation had made her ask it aloud.

"Resign."

"The successor would receive the office after diocesan appointment. Months, not days."

"Fastest lawful estimate?"

Delisle called the diocesan office on speaker without naming the supernatural duty. Emergency appointment to a vacant parish could occur in weeks. Full canonical transfer might take longer. No process fit nine days.

The human clerk offered to email a checklist. June thanked her and accepted it because failed routes still needed proof.

The deadline did not care about administrative realism.

Neither did desire. June knew where Elias stood in the Fourth Avenue basement by the pull beneath her sternum. Completion had been a choice she might make later, after lawful defeat, after Milo's release, after motive. Delisle's conscience had converted later into never unless his successor qualified.

She refused to let grief over that private loss distract from the immediate one. Milo had nine days.

Tess sat at the end of the table, silent until every route failed.

She had arrived during the diocesan call and asked no question. Her case files remained closed in front of her. Unlike everyone else, she did not look defeated as June crossed the last candidate from the center circle.

June noticed and postponed the observation until the joint conclusion was entered. Suspicion should not interrupt proof.

"So say it," June told Pratt.

He read the conclusion into the joint record. No accepted witness presently existed other than Delisle. Delisle refused permanently. A valid completion could not occur before the term expired.

"And the consequence?"

Marta answered. "Automatic forfeiture."

"Define custody," June said.

Pratt read the Trust construction. Milo could be ordered to remain at a named property, surrendered temporarily to the holder of the executing claim, and required to perform the deed named under clause four. Ordinary bodily harm remained illegal. Supernatural enforcement would make departure a breach against his standing.

"Can he refuse?"

"He can refuse the deed and take its named consequence," Pratt said. "He cannot make forfeiture cease by refusal."

"Can I substitute myself?"

"No. Forfeiture distinguishes bride and surety intentionally."

Milo's surety would convert to custody. June's standing under the sequence would be struck. Her refusal remained absolute, but everything around it would execute.

"If my standing is struck, can I still challenge?"

"Not under the Articles," Pratt said.

Tess moved for the first time, one finger resting on her closed file.

June heard the omission. "Under something else?"

Nobody answered quickly enough.

Tess laughed once.

June turned. "What is funny?"

"Nothing. This is the first correct opening in eleven years."

"Correct for whom?"

"For a court. Dead brides are history. Released brides lack current injury. Before forfeiture, you have prospective harm institutions call hypothetical. A perfected forfeiture creates a living party, a completed injury, and an action the Trust cannot dismiss as unripe."

"Milo is not a jurisdictional event."

"No. He is a plaintiff if he agrees. You remain the bride whose standing was struck. Together you carry both sides of the injury."

"Milo gets taken."

"And a living bride suffers a perfected forfeiture. That creates the injury my case lacked standing to challenge."

"You triggered clause three."

"No. I prepared for somebody to."

"How?"

Tess opened the first file. Draft petitions already bore blank spaces for forfeiture date, custodial property, and executing notice. Exhibits were tabbed through the previous evening. She had built the case to become fileable within minutes of loss.

"You knew the term was nine."

"I knew the parish schedule could differ. I did not know the number until Delisle read it."

"Did you know he was the holder?"

"I knew a neutral holder existed and that Kearse was looking for it."

"Did you help him?"

"No."

June could not smell Tess's answer. She wrote it down for later verification instead of deciding from anger.

"You wanted this."

"I wanted a living challenge. I did not want Milo harmed. Those facts can coexist."

"You budgeted his harm into your forms."

"I budgeted the harm the Articles were already designed to create. Preparation is not causation."

"It can become permission to stop looking for rescue."

Tess's silence conceded more than an argument would have.

"I wanted a challenge the Trust could not dismiss as dead, hypothetical, or late."

"You told me the case might kill me. You didn't say it needed my cousin in custody."

"You had not agreed to read that part."

"You used my consent boundary to hide your strategy."

"I offered the files in stages because you said you would not accept every risk at once."

"And you chose which risk counted."

"Yes."

Tess did not pretend procedure had absolved her. She had honored the literal boundary and used it to preserve a plan June might have refused.

Tess did not soften. "This is the best news either of us has had in eleven years."

June understood then that Tess had been waiting for a forfeiture, not a rescue.
