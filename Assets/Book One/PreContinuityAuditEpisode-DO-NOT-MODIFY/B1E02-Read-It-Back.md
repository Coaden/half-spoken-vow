# Episode Two: Read It Back

Episode Two: Read It Back

Marta got to him in the stairwell before June did.

June heard it from two flights up, coming down with her case in one hand and her coat still over her arm, and the concrete did what concrete does, which was hand her every word.

"You have made three hundred and eleven people less safe," Marta said, "in a room with a witness in it, and you did it in about nine seconds."

"I am aware."

"Are you. Because from where I sat it looked like a man who had not thought past lunch."

"I had thought past lunch."

June stopped on the landing. Below her the two of them stood a careful arm's length apart under a fire door, the heavy woman in the windbreaker and the man in the suit, and neither of them was raising their voice, which somehow made it worse.

"Say the rest of it, then," Marta said.

"There is no rest of it. The clause is suspended."

"For thirty days."

"For thirty days."

"And in the meantime you are standing there with nothing on you." Marta's voice did something June had not heard from her yet. It went flat and a little tired. "You understand what you are now. Anybody with a claim can put a hand on you. Anybody with standing can make you do a thing you would not do, and you will do it, and you will be able to watch yourself doing it."

"Yes."

"Say the words, then. Out loud, so I've heard them."

"I am exposed." He said it the way a man reads his own diagnosis off a chart. "Until the vow is answered or formally abandoned, I hold no protections of any kind. Any party with standing may compel me. Any party with a claim may bring it, and there is nothing for me to stand behind."

"Compel you to what."

"Anything they can phrase."

"And she is fine."

"She is entirely free."

"That is not what I asked."

There was a pause, and June understood that she had been standing on a landing eavesdropping for long enough that walking down now was a decision, so she made it, and her heels announced her the whole way.

Marta looked up at her without one flicker of surprise. "Miss Havlik has a transcript to certify," she said, "so I will get out of the way."

She went. The fire door shut behind her with a sound like a full stop.

June set her case down.

"You didn't answer her," she said.

"I answered both of her questions."

"You answered the first one." She had spent twelve years listening to people do exactly this, and it was almost restful to hear it done well. "The second one you took apart and gave back the easy half."

Something moved across his face that might have been amusement in a person who used his face for that.

"Why," June said. "Plainly. In one sentence, in words that would survive a read-back."

"Because clause one should not have been read this morning."

She waited. He did not add to it. He stood there in the stairwell with his hands at his sides and let the sentence be complete, and after a moment she realized it was complete, that it was the entire answer, that it was true in every particular and told her nothing she could use.

"I didn't ask you to do that," she said.

"No."

"I want you to know I didn't ask."

"I know that," he said. "I would not have asked either."

And that was worse. She had come down those stairs with about four things loaded and ready and he had agreed with the first one before she could fire it, and there was nowhere to put the rest.

Her chest was doing the thing again. Standing four feet from him on a landing that smelled like floor wax and old smoke, low and physical and entirely her own, and she thought, with the clean detachment she used for other people's disasters: I am going to have to find out what that is.

"One more," June said. "Procedural. Is there a form?"

"For what?"

"For undoing it. There's a form for everything. Somebody drafted this thing in eighteen seventy-one and they were thorough enough to put nine clauses in it, so somewhere there's a way to take a half-spoken vow back off the table."

He was quiet for long enough that she knew the answer was yes before he gave it.

"There is," he said. "It is called abandonment. It is available to the speaker at any time before completion."

"Then abandon it."

"No."

"That's not a reason."

"It was not offered as one." He picked up his folder from the rail where he had set it. "Ask me the price sometime when you have not had a morning like this one, and I will tell you exactly. I would rather you were sitting down."

"I have to certify the record," she said.

"Yes."

"Your name is going to be in it."

"That is generally the point of a record," said Elias Marrow.

Pratt was waiting in the conference room with the exhibits squared and his coat already on, and he was so pleased to see her that it took her a second to be angry about it.

"Miss Havlik. I wondered whether you would want a moment."

"I want to close the file."

"Of course." He drew out the chair beside him, which no attorney had done for her in twelve years, and turned her own certification page toward her as though it were something he had made. "You will want to read what you are signing. I always do. People sign things all day without reading them and it is the great tragedy of my profession."

She read it. It was the same language she had signed eleven hundred times. *I certify that the foregoing is a true and accurate transcript of the proceedings.*

"You understand," Pratt said, "that this makes it a record."

"It was already a record."

"It was already spoken. That is not the same thing, and the difference is the whole of my working life." He said it without any weight at all, the way a man says a thing he assumes everyone knows. "Spoken, it happened. Certified, it happened *and* somebody competent stood behind it. After you sign that, what Mr. Marrow said this morning is a fact that can be relied on by people who were not in the room."

June looked at the page.

"And if I don't sign it."

"Then the transcript is uncertified, your agency's contract with this firm ends, and the proceeding is noticed again for a date within the week." He straightened the exhibits. "And Mr. Marrow says the same words to you a second time, in front of the same witness, having had a week to think about whether he wants to."

"That's not better."

"No," Pratt agreed. "It is considerably worse for him, and no different for you. I mention it only because you asked."

She signed it. Her hand did not shake, which she noted, the way she noted everything.

"Thank you," said Pratt, and meant it, and squared the page onto the stack with two fingers. "May I say one more thing, and then I will let you go and get wet."

"Everybody keeps letting me go."

"I have been counsel of record to that instrument for twenty-two years," he said. "I have read all nine clauses more times than I have read anything except the newspaper. And this morning is the first time in twenty-two years that anybody has done something to it that I did not see coming."

"Is that good."

"I have absolutely no idea," Pratt said, delighted. "It is certainly interesting."

It was raining by the time she got to Liberty Avenue, the fine sideways kind that gets in under a collar, and the bus was eleven minutes out.

Cyd rang while she was still counting the minutes on the board.

"Tell me it went fine."

"It went fine."

"Because Pratt's office already called about the expedite and they never call about the expedite, and I have exactly one person who never has a transcript come back, so if there is something on this one I would like to hear it from you and not from a paralegal named Braden."

The rain was going down the back of her neck. Down the block a man was arguing with a parking kiosk, patiently, the way you argue with something that cannot hear you.

She thought about the nine hundred dollars. She thought about the fact that Cyd had four contracts and one of them was Pratt's firm, and that a court reporter who becomes a *subject* stops being a court reporter, and that this was the sort of thing that did not need a scandal to end a career. It only needed a rumor and a scheduling clerk.

"There's nothing on it," June said.

Six words. She heard herself say them the way she heard everything, half a second behind, and she thought: that is the first lie I have told her in nine years, and it went out clean, and she took it.

"Okay," Cyd said. "Okay, good. You sound like a woman standing in the rain."

"I am a woman standing in the rain."

"Get on the bus, June."

"Cyd."

"What."

She had it right there. *A man bound himself to me in a deposition this morning and I typed it.* Nine seconds, and it would be Cyd's problem too, and Cyd would make it Cyd's problem gladly and immediately and without asking a single condition, which was exactly the reason not to say it.

"Nothing," June said. "Bus is here."

She hung up, and the screen stayed lit, and there was a voicemail on it from a number with no name.

She played it standing under the shelter with the phone flat on her palm, because her hands were full and because some part of her already knew she was going to want both of them free.

The voice was a woman's. Warm, brisk, pleasant, the voice of somebody who had done this many times and enjoyed the work.

"Miss Havlik. My name is Vera Odell. I cut attachments for a living, and I already know what you're going to be wanting." A pause, and something that was almost a laugh in it. "My price is never money. Call me when you've stopped being proud."
