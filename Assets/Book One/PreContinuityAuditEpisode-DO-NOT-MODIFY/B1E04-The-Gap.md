# Episode Four: The Gap

Episode Four: The Gap

"Nineteen years," Yolanda said. "It started the winter your mother left, and I have carried it every month since, and I want you to hear it from me before you hear it from —"

The office door opened.

"Ladies," said Marta Havlik, without any heat at all. "Would you both like to come in and do this properly."

It was the politest thing June had ever watched happen to anybody.

Yolanda got up. That was all. She did not argue, she did not finish the sentence, she got up out of the folding chair with her hands flat on the ledger for one second and then she took the ledger with her and went through the office door, and the thing she had been about to say went with her, and June sat there and understood with total clarity that a fact had just been removed from the room while she watched.

She had spent twelve years being the person who caught that. She had never once been the person it was done to.

She picked up her case and followed.

The business agent's office was the size of a walk-in closet and it held a metal desk, four chairs, a filing cabinet with a plant dying on top of it, and forty years of paper. On the wall behind the desk was a photograph of a mill gate with maybe two hundred people in front of it.

Marta sat down. Yolanda did not.

"Sister Bright, thank you, that's all."

Yolanda went. She touched June's shoulder on the way past, once, and it felt like an apology from somebody who had run out of them.

"Sit down, June."

"I'll stand."

"You'll stand for an hour and then your feet will hurt and you'll sit, so sit now and save us the theater."

June sat.

Marta took her glasses off and set them on the blotter and rubbed the bridge of her nose, and for one second she looked exactly like a sixty-four-year-old woman at the end of a bad week, and then she stopped.

"Ask," she said.

"Who closed my mother's line?"

"I did."

June had a second question loaded and it fell over.

"You can ask me the next one," Marta said. "You're allowed. I'm going to tell you now that you won't like the answer to it either, and then you can ask."

"Why."

"That I won't tell you."

"That's not —"

"It's not an answer. No." Marta put her glasses back on. "I'm not going to sit here and pretend I don't remember, or that it was somebody else's decision, or that the paperwork got away from us. I signed it. I read it first and I signed it and I knew exactly what it did. And I am not going to tell you why, and I'm not going to insult you by dressing that up."

And there it was.

June had been braced for a lie. She had come up three flights of stairs building a case for a lie, and she had a whole apparatus ready for one, and the woman had simply declined to provide it. There was nothing to catch her out on. There was nothing to *do*.

"You could have told me at any point in nineteen years," June said.

"Yes."

"You came to my mother's funeral."

"I did."

"You stood next to me."

"I did that too." Marta's hands were flat on the desk. They were terrible hands, June noticed, distantly, professionally — knuckles like walnuts, two fingers that had healed crooked. "You're going to want me to feel worse about it than I do. I want you to understand that's not going to happen either, and it's not because I don't like you."

"Then what is it."

"Three hundred and eleven."

The radiator knocked.

"That's what's in the book," Marta said. "Three hundred and eleven members in good standing. Fourteen of them are over eighty. Forty-one are drawing on the fund right now. There are nine rooms in the basement of this building and there is a waiting list for them, in a city where a person like us cannot rent a place with a basement door that locks from outside without somebody eventually asking why."

"I know what a local is."

"Do you." Marta pulled a folder off the stack without looking and opened it. "Dennis Pilcher, forty-six, roofer, missed four cycles this year because his employer stopped believing him and he was afraid to lose the job. Fourth night he put himself in a storage unit off Butler Street with a padlock on the outside and a cousin who swore he'd come back at dawn." She turned a page. "Cousin came back at dawn. Wasn't a problem. Could've been."

"Why are you telling me this."

"Because you're about to decide I'm a monster, and I'd rather you did it with the numbers in front of you." She closed the folder. "That fund paid Dennis for eleven shifts. That roster is why there was a room for him in May. Both of those things exist because we honor the Articles, and the Articles get honored because somebody signs the paperwork, and for nineteen years the somebody has been me."

"You know what a union is. This isn't a union, it's a lifeboat with a charter, and the charter is contingent." Marta held up one crooked finger. "One condition. We honor the Articles. Eighteen seventy-one, nine clauses, a bride in each generation. That's the whole rent we pay to be left alone in this county, and it has been paid seven times."

"And I'm eight."

"You're eight."

"And you'd spend me."

"I already did," said Marta. "Last Tuesday, in that hall, by a show of hands, and I called the vote myself and I did not abstain."

June sat very still.

She had come here for a form. That was the thing she kept getting stuck on. She had gotten on a bus this morning with a plan that had one step in it, and every hour since had removed a step she had not known was load-bearing, and she was now sitting in a closet in front of a woman who had voted to hand her over and would not pretend otherwise.

She was aware that somewhere under all of it something enormous was arriving, and that she did not currently have time for it. It had a shape and the shape was roughly: *there is nobody coming.* Not the Hall, not her aunt, not the law, not the building full of people who could smell exactly whose daughter she was and had put their hands up anyway.

Nineteen days to the twenty-first. Eleven days after that until a clause resumed. And the only instrument in the county that admitted she existed was the one that had her down as owed.

So she put it in the drawer at the back of her head, and shut it, and opened her case instead.

"All right," she said.

Marta watched her take out a legal pad.

"I want a certified copy of the Articles," June said. "All nine clauses, complete, with the exhibit number from Monday's deposition. I'm making the request to Local Nine as a party to the instrument, and I'm making it in writing."

"You're not a member. You've got no —"

"I'm not asking as a member. I'm asking as a person named in the document, which I am, which is the one thing everybody in this building agrees on." She was writing while she talked, in the blocky hand she used for case captions. "You hold a certified copy. I'm entitled to see the instrument I'm named under. If I'm not, tell me the clause that says so and I'll go away."

Marta did not tell her the clause.

June finished writing, tore the sheet off, and held it out.

"And I'm saying it out loud," she said, "because I'm told that matters here."

That got something. Not a smile. A change around the eyes, small and quick, the look of a woman being handed a tool she recognizes.

"Say it, then."

"June Havlik, requesting a certified copy of the Articles of eighteen seventy-one from Local Nine, this afternoon, in the business agent's office." She raised her voice slightly, toward the door, where a shadow had been standing in the frosted glass for the last ninety seconds. "Witnessed by Sister Bright, who is in the corridor."

The shadow did not move. Then it said, muffled, "Witnessed."

Marta took the sheet.

She read it all the way through, which June had not expected, and then she opened the second drawer of the metal desk and put it in a folder and wrote something on the tab.

"Fourteen days," she said. "That's what the bylaws give me and I'll use eleven of them."

"Why eleven?"

"Because fourteen looks like I'm hiding something and seven looks like I'm frightened of you." Marta wrote a date on the tab. "Eleven looks like a woman following a procedure."

"That's fine."

"It's not fine, it's eleven days you don't have, but it's what the bylaws say." Marta stood, which meant it was over. "Get out of my office. And June —"

She stopped in the doorway.

Marta was looking at the photograph of the mill gate.

"You did that right," she said. "In writing, out loud, with a witness. Nobody taught you that."

"Somebody taught me depositions."

"Same thing." Marta sat back down and pulled the next folder toward her. "Go home."

June was six steps down the corridor when the voice came after her, not raised, but pitched to carry the way a voice gets pitched in a building full of people who cannot be lied to.

"There's no paper in this county with your name on it but theirs," Marta said. "If you want standing, you'll have to go and take it out of them."
