# Episode Twenty-Five: The Cutter

Episode Twenty-Five: The Cutter

Vera Odell locked the door, named the deed she wanted from June, and only then invited her to sit.

"Cyd Toomey closes her agency for one full business day," Vera said. "No transcripts produced, no calls answered, no emergency coverage. Every employee paid. She puts a notice on the door stating the closure is the price of June Havlik's severance."

The rooms above the Bloomfield shop smelled of beeswax and boiled linen. No surgical steel. No restraints. A kitchen table stood beneath a shaded lamp with two chairs placed far enough apart that nobody could touch by accident.

Downstairs, someone dragged a display rack across tile. The ordinary noise made the locked room worse. It felt like an appointment kept after hours, lamps turned low for someone who could not tolerate questions.

Vera had asked June to bring her own water, a list of medications, and the name of a person authorized to retrieve her if the consultation left her unable to travel. June had brought the first two and written *none* for the third.

Vera tapped the blank line. "That is not a medical fact."

"It's the arrangement I have."

"Then no procedure happens tonight even if you accept the price. Consultation only."

June resented the relief. "You said terms came before touch."

"They also come before scheduling. A person willing to cut the only involuntary direction in her body is not automatically competent to take a bus home afterward."

June remained standing. "Why Cyd?"

"Because you can pay money without changing. You cannot ask her to lose a day and remain convinced nobody else bears your freedom."

"Her employees would lose work."

"Paid work. Her clients would lose service. Her reputation would take the mark. The deed belongs to her and must be freely performed. You cannot counterfeit it."

"And if she refuses?"

"No cut."

"Can I perform a different deed?"

"You may request a new quote. I may decline to give one."

"Could I close the agency for her?"

"No. The deed is hers."

"Could she close without posting my name?"

"No."

"Could the notice say personal emergency?"

Vera's smile made the answer unnecessary. "You are very practiced at turning a price into an inconvenience."

"I'm practiced at limiting damage."

"Then hear the damage accurately. Cyd chooses whether to interrupt the work that feeds her and the people she employs. She names why. She lives one day with what that announcement changes. I witness the closure. Only afterward do I touch your record."

Vera was perhaps fifty, with iron-grey hair shaved close on one side and a cheerful voice entirely unsuited to the subject. She put both palms on the table.

"You asked for terms. Terms come before touch. Do you want the commission explained?"

"In full."

Vera opened the commission sheet but left it facing herself. "I will ask questions that would be insulting in any ordinary transaction. Whether anyone sent you. Whether anyone promised to restore what the cut removes. Whether anyone threatened the other party, your housing, your employment, or your family. Whether you are choosing absence because you want it or because another person taught you that your symptoms were permission."

"What answer disqualifies me?"

"A false one. An intoxicated one. An answer repeated in somebody else's language. Sometimes an honest yes."

"You can tell when I lie?"

"I can tell when your body and your words produce incompatible records. That is not the same gift and not an invitation to test me."

"I sever an incomplete attachment from the borne record and annotate the written registers afterward. The severance is permanent. You remember the person, the pull, every feeling you had. You cannot feel the attachment again. The other party is severed at the same moment."

"Does it hurt?"

"Yes."

"Survivable?"

"Ordinarily. I have lost two clients in thirty-one years. Both concealed medical facts. I will examine you before accepting."

"What does the examination cost?"

"Nothing. Examinations are not supernatural obligations. I do not turn a pulse into a debt."

Vera asked permission before coming around the table. When June gave it, she checked June's pupils, pulse, and the color beneath her fingernails. She listened to June's heart through a wooden bell that looked older than the building. At every step she said what she was about to do and waited.

The care did not make the work harmless. It made care impossible to confuse with innocence.

"Can it be reversed?"

"No."

"Can you cut a completed vow?"

Vera's cheer left. "Nobody can. Completion exists in writing, body, and a living witness's memory. There is no instrument that reaches all three. Anyone offering annulment is selling murder with stationery."

June sat.

"Why deeds instead of money?"

"Because cutting is a crime against a record. No court enforces my bill. No trust honors my invoice. No local collects my debt. A deed performed in view is the only payment I can secure."

"So you make somebody do something first."

"I quote it first. They consent or leave. No deed, no cut. No debt carried after."

"Who decides the deed?"

"I do."

"Then your conscience is the price list."

"My risk is the price list. If I alter a record, every institution that depends on records has reason to make an example of me. Money can be hidden, clawed back, inherited, or declared stolen. A deed performed openly cannot be repossessed."

"You could ask for something useful."

"Useful to me would turn the commission into employment. I ask for something that proves the person seeking freedom understands freedom will enter other lives."

Vera placed a tuning fork, a length of red thread, and a blank commission sheet on the table. She touched none of them.

"Demonstrate without cutting me."

"I can demonstrate the edge. Hold your hand above the thread."

June did. The pull toward Elias lived under her sternum, pointing south and west. Vera struck the fork softly. The note entered June's teeth. For one breath the direction vanished.

Then it returned hard enough to bend her forward.

Vera steadied the table, not June.

"Name what happened," Vera said.

June pressed one hand below her sternum. "The direction stopped."

"Did your judgment change?"

"No."

"Did you stop remembering him?"

"No."

"Did you want him less?"

June looked up.

"That is a medical question for this purpose," Vera said. "You may decline it."

"I don't know. It was one breath."

"Good. People who answer too quickly are usually answering a hope."

"That was not a cut. It was interruption. The full work removes the return."

June waited for her breath. "What happens to Elias?"

"The same absence. His exposed status would end because the attachment capable of answer no longer exists. His half-vow would be annotated impossible, not abandoned."

"And the Articles?"

"They continue. Cutting solves the bond, not your naming."

Immediate escape had been too generous a phrase. The pull would end. Elias would be protected. Clause two would still hold Milo, and nine clauses would still know June's name.

"Who buys the severed records?" June asked.

Vera smiled without warmth. "Not part of your commission."

"You sell them."

"Attachments leave holes in two registers. Somebody always accounts for a hole."

"Who?"

"You are not buying that answer."

"Would the answer change whether I consent?"

"If it would, do not consent."

"That's convenient."

"It is precise. I can tell you where your severance will be recorded and what facts will remain. I will not tell you who inspects records after they leave my custody."

"Can't or won't?"

"Won't."

Vera showed June an old severance entry with the names covered. The written register recorded an attachment on one line and an annotation of severance beneath it. Nothing had been erased. On a second sheet, a member witness described the bodily absence afterward: two people who had once turned toward each other no longer did.

"What remains between them?" June asked.

"Whatever they built without the attachment. Affection, resentment, habit, shared rent. The cut removes one fact. People regularly discover they blamed it for facts that belonged to them."

"And if they loved each other?"

"They remember loving. They may still choose every act love required. The involuntary part is gone. Some experience that as freedom. Some as bereavement with the deceased still making breakfast."

June thought of Elias across the sitting room, staying exactly six feet away. A cut would remove the direction under her sternum. It would not unsay his vow, restore Milo, reopen her roster line, or give Cyd back the lost clients.

"How many people regret it?"

"Everyone regrets something. That is not a useful outcome measure."

"How many ask you to reverse it?"

"About half. I tell them no before the work and again afterward."

"What do the other half do?"

"Some leave relieved. Some rebuild the relationship without involuntary direction. Some discover the attachment was the only reason they remained in the same room. I do not grade the lives that follow."

"You keep no outcomes?"

"I keep complications, deaths, attempted reversals, and threats against my practice. Happiness is not a condition I can verify."

June read the blank commission. It stated the deed exactly and required Cyd's separate declaration. No euphemism. No clause allowing substitute performance.

"Why a full day?"

"Because an hour becomes lunch. Half a day becomes a plumbing problem. A full business day with the reason on the door cannot be mistaken for convenience."

"You want a public price."

"I want an irreversible one."

June imagined asking Cyd to close. Eleven thousand seven hundred and sixty dollars had already vanished because of June's name. A closed day would tell every client the danger was real and the agency had chosen it.

It would also require Cyd to answer questions from employees June knew mostly by the food they left in the break-room refrigerator. It would turn June's secret into their canceled appointments, their explanations, their reason to wonder whether the agency could survive another favor.

Vera had found a price June could afford only by pretending other people's work was less real than her fear.

"Can I refuse now and return?"

"Refusal requires nothing. Return requires the same price unless you change."

"Change how?"

"Pride is expensive to cut because it makes other people perform the grief. Desperation is more expensive because desperate people agree too easily."

June stood. "I'm not accepting."

"I heard you."

"You won't perform it for the Trust, the Hall, Elias, or Cyd."

"That requires a formal election recorded by both sides. Today you only refused me, which is sufficient to make me stop."

Vera unlocked the door.

"The offer stays open at that price for as long as you're proud," she said. "It goes up the day you stop."
