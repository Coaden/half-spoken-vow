# Episode Sixteen: Billable

Episode Sixteen: Billable

Cyd put the cancellation on speaker so the whole office could hear the client say June Havlik was the problem.

"Not the agency," the man said through the phone. "Her specifically. We were advised she is a party to an unresolved matter and may become unavailable without notice."

Nine people stopped pretending not to listen.

The agency occupied two rooms above a tax preparer on Grant Street. The front room held six desks, a scheduling board, and a printer old enough to respond to threats. The back room held Cyd, June, Sondra, and the phone. The door between them was open.

Cyd leaned over the speaker. "Advised by whom?"

"I can't disclose that."

"Can't or won't?"

"The decision is final, Miss Toomey."

"Then say what you're cancelling. All of it. My staff are listening and I want the number correct."

Paper moved on the other end.

"Four sessions next week. The standing monthly calendar beginning December first. Expedited copy on the pending commercial matter."

Cyd wrote each item on the pad in front of her.

"That's one hundred and twelve scheduled hours through the end of December," she said. "Plus copy."

"If you assign another reporter, we may reconsider."

"You didn't call to cancel the agency. You called to make me remove one contractor."

"I told you the concern."

"No, you repeated somebody else's concern and declined to name them. Those are different activities."

June watched the fear gather around the desks in the front room. Human fear had no clean scent she could separate, but nine people checking the scheduling board at once carried the meaning well enough.

This was the second client.

The first loss had taken December from June. This one had reached everybody.

Cyd said, "Send the cancellation in writing. Include every assignment you just named."

"We'll send confirmation."

"And spell Havlik correctly."

She ended the call.

Nobody spoke for three seconds. Then the printer began feeding a blank sheet with the solemn timing of a machine attempting to leave the building.

"All right," Cyd said. "Conference. Everybody in. Bring your coffee and whatever grievance you've been composing with your face."

The front room came into the back. Chairs ran out at five, so four people stood. Cyd remained at the desk with the speakerphone between her hands.

June said, "Take me off the board."

"No."

"They said they would reconsider."

"They said may. May is what clients say when they want you to do the ugly part before they decide whether to reward you."

Sondra folded her arms. "What does the board look like if she stays?"

It was the question nobody else wanted to put in the room.

Cyd turned the scheduling board around. Red marks showed cancellations. Blue showed assigned work. Three weeks ago the board had been mostly blue.

"One hundred and twelve hours gone," Cyd said. "At our average billed rate, eleven thousand seven hundred and sixty dollars before copy. The first cancellation was June's calendar and cost her directly. This one was agency work. If nobody else leaves, I can cover payroll through January without touching the tax account."

"And after January?" somebody asked from the doorway.

"After January I develop a drinking problem in a more affordable neighborhood."

The laugh was brief and grateful. Cyd let it finish.

"I don't have an after-January answer yet."

June looked at the board. "You have one. Remove my name."

"Stop volunteering my decisions for me."

"It's my name costing you work."

"Correct. My decision is whether that fact gets to run the company."

Sondra stepped closer to the board. "We still need an assignment rule."

Cyd's eyes narrowed. "Meaning?"

"Meaning June can't keep the best sessions because clients dropping her creates a moral emergency. She also can't be frozen out because somebody anonymous knows how to use a phone. If we absorb this by improvising, every assignment becomes a favor and every favor becomes a fight."

June heard the fairness in it and disliked it more than accusation would have deserved.

"Seniority and availability," Sondra continued. "Same rule as last month. If a client rejects the assigned reporter by name, the work rotates to the next qualified person. The rejected reporter gets the next comparable opening. Put it in writing and apply it to all of us."

"That could cost you work," June said.

"Yes. Fair rules are annoying that way."

Cyd uncapped a marker. "Anyone object?"

Nobody did.

She wrote the rule at the bottom of the board and read it aloud. Seniority. Availability. Named rejection. Comparable replacement. Then she dated it and signed her initials beneath it.

"There," Cyd said. "We have converted panic into office policy. This is why America remains open during business hours."

People went back to their desks one at a time. The fear did not vanish, but it acquired tasks. Calls to make. Sessions to move. A revised calendar to send before lunch.

Sondra stayed.

Cyd pointed the marker at June. "You have the warehouse supervisor tomorrow and the insurance panel Friday. Sondra has the commercial matter if the cancellation holds. Nobody swaps privately. Nobody nobly falls on anything."

"You're taking eleven thousand dollars out of the business to keep my name on a board."

"Eleven thousand seven hundred and sixty before copy. If you're going to be guilty, be accurate."

"Cyd."

"No. You don't get to turn yourself into a line item because it feels cleaner than asking me to choose you."

The words stopped June more effectively than volume would have.

Cyd set down the marker. "I am choosing to keep a contractor who has never returned a bad transcript, who restored a client yesterday, and who is currently lying to me about something large enough to make strangers call my office. Those facts can all exist at once."

"You shouldn't have to pay for the last one."

"I shouldn't have to pay quarterly taxes either, but the world has declined my feedback."

Sondra looked toward the front room. "What are you cutting?"

"My draw for December. The equipment replacement. Holiday bonuses stay."

"Cyd," June said.

"Absolutely not discussing my compensation with you while you still owe me an explanation."

"Then take it out of mine."

"You are a subcontractor. I cannot dock you for a client I chose to lose without becoming exactly the kind of employer I spend money avoiding."

The printer started again. This time it produced the written cancellation.

Cyd took the page and read it standing beside the machine.

"Effective immediately, all pending and future assignments are withdrawn due to concerns regarding reporter June Elizabeth Havlik, née Sabol, and her availability arising from an unresolved personal matter."

June felt the room narrow around the older name.

Her mother's name.

Cyd read the rest. No source. No allegation they could answer. Only a concern polished until it looked reasonable.

"Née Sabol," June said.

"That's what it says."

"That isn't public in my business records."

"It could be found," Sondra said. "Marriage index. Birth record."

"By somebody looking for it."

Cyd turned the page over as though the blank back might identify the caller.

"Does that name mean something?"

June thought of the roster line closed nineteen years ago. Her mother's date. Marta's signature. The gap Yolanda had begun to explain and been stopped from explaining.

"It means this isn't a client hearing gossip," she said. "Somebody connected the agency to my mother."

Cyd's face went still.

"Then we call the police."

"And report what?"

"Harassment. Interference with a business. A deeply irritating voice on the telephone. I will let them select the statute."

"The caller didn't threaten anyone."

"They took twelve thousand dollars out of this office by lying politely. I am not waiting for them to improve their manners."

June looked through the open door at the scheduling board. A police report would create another record with her name, her mother's name, the client list, and a question asking what unresolved matter might make her unavailable. Human law would treat the Articles as nonsense until somebody produced a certified filing that was not nonsense at all.

"Give me until tomorrow," she said.

"You have been giving me tomorrow since the first phone call."

"I need to find out whether the caller is from the Hall, the Trust, or somebody outside both."

"You just named three organizations as though I know what any of them are."

June heard the mistake after it left her mouth.

Cyd heard it too.

"The Trust owns your building," Cyd said. "The Hall is where your aunt works. What is outside both?"

"I don't know yet."

"That is the first useful thing you've told me."

Cyd took the scheduling rule off the board, made a copy, and handed the original to June.

"You keep this. If I get frightened overnight and pretend we never made it, you bring it back and embarrass me with my own handwriting."

"You won't."

"That is friendship talking. Office policy requires a copy."

June folded the page once. It was not standing in any lineage sense. It was better in one respect: Cyd had made it while knowing only the cost and none of the reason, and had left June a way to hold her to it.

"And you know who."

"No."

That answer was true.

Sondra took the cancellation and read the sentence again, slowly, exactly as written. When she reached the maiden name, she stopped.

"I moved six sessions for you this month," she said. "I covered the warehouse witness today. I will keep doing my share under the rule we just made."

"I know."

"No, listen. I will cover work. I will not cover a secret. Not when somebody using it knows our clients and my schedule."

June could hear Cyd breathing beside the printer. Beyond the open door, nine people worked more quietly than people ordinarily worked.

Sondra held out the cancellation.

"What is happening, June? And why did the caller ask for you by your mother's maiden name?"
