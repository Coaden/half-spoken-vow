# Episode Eleven: The Seventh Sister

Episode Eleven: The Seventh Sister

June reached the booth at nine fourteen and heard her own voice say, through a stranger's phone, "Clarification for the record."

The woman sitting with her back to the wall stopped the recording under June's next breath.

"Why does the eighth bride sound surprised?"

June stayed standing. The diner was full enough that every plate arrived with somebody else's conversation attached. The woman had chosen the last booth, beside the kitchen door, where nobody could approach without crossing her line of sight. Close-cropped hair, dark at the roots. Canvas jacket. Work boots. A hearing aid in her left ear.

On the table were two coffees, a phone, and a file thick enough to have ruined several weekends.

"You recorded the Hall."

"Somebody did. I bought it."

"From whom?"

"Not relevant yet. Sit down."

June slid into the opposite seat but kept her coat on. "You asked me here. You can start with your name."

"Tess Renn. My sister was number seven."

There was no softening before it and no pause afterward. Tess opened the file and placed a certified death record between the sugar jar and the napkin holder.

"Lenora Renn," she read. "Born May third, nineteen eighty-six. Died November twelfth, two thousand fifteen. Cause entered as accidental trauma. Clause two was read satisfied at nine twenty-seven that night. Elias Marrow read it. Aloysius Pratt filed it the next morning."

June did not touch the paper.

"Nadia called her Renn."

"Nadia liked her. It did not help."

The waitress appeared with a pad. Tess ordered eggs and toast without looking up. June asked for water.

"Order food," Tess said.

"No."

"Good. You can refuse a small thing. Keep practicing."

June looked at the phone. "Play the rest."

"No."

"Then tell me what you want."

"I have a case. Eleven years of records, witnesses, certified copies, and people who will deny knowing me until I put their own signatures in front of them. It can only be filed by a living bride with standing under the Articles. You are one. I want your promise that you will keep it alive."

"Keep it alive how?"

"Read it. Carry it if I die. File it when the opening comes."

"What opening?"

"Promise first."

June took her recorder from the stretched pocket of her cardigan and set it beside Tess's phone.

"I'm going to record this."

"Do."

"State your name."

"Tess Renn. I want to use June Havlik's standing to prosecute the case I built after my sister's death. The case may get her killed. I am withholding evidence until she agrees to examine it."

The recorder's red light held steady.

"That last sentence isn't what you asked me to promise."

"No. It is what I can get today."

"And if I don't agree?"

"I keep looking for another route. You probably die under a numbered clause. I dislike both outcomes."

The waitress put down the water and went away more quickly than she had arrived.

June said, "You don't know me."

"I know you made Marta Havlik produce the copy in eleven days. I know you beat residence with an eighteen seventy-one definition. I know you spent three nights under the Trust's roof and found the book they do not know exists. I know a client dropped you yesterday. I know you told Cyd Toomey you would explain and then did not."

The glass stopped halfway to June's mouth.

"That wasn't on the recording."

"No."

"Stay away from Cyd."

"I have. She is useful leverage and innocent. Those are two reasons not to touch her and one reason somebody else will."

June put the glass down. "Show me something worth examining. Not your conclusion. Your evidence."

Tess watched her for three seconds, then drew out nine index cards held by a rubber band.

"The Articles did not begin with nine clauses," she said. "They accumulated them."

"Pratt says the sequence has never changed."

"The sequence has not. The drafting has. Amendment, restatement, construction, and enforcement instructions. They call all of it the Articles because institutions enjoy pretending the current version descended from heaven complete."

"Who drafted the changes?"

"The Trust drafted. The Local witnessed and accepted. Every time."

Tess put down the first card.

"Bride one refused the Sewickley house. Residence became clause one. Bride two disappeared before the moon and nobody could compel her family to produce her. Surety became clause two. Bride three kept both sides in court for four years by disputing the duration. A term certain became clause three."

She laid the cards down one at a time. Each carried a woman's name, a date, and a number. She read every one aloud over the clatter of cutlery.

"Bride four had a child outside the agreement. Issue became clause four. Bride five moved property beyond the county. Disposition became clause five. Bride six transferred what remained through a human trust. Clause six answered that."

"And seven?"

Tess touched the card with her sister's name.

"Lenora found a defect in surety. She argued the person named had never accepted the office. She was right. The Trust filed a construction saying acceptance could be borne by kinship and witnessed presence. Local Nine accepted it in an open meeting. Clause two killed her four days later."

June heard Nadia's voice from before dawn. *She was right and she was alone.*

"You said every clause answers a bride. There were seven brides and nine clauses."

"Two answer women who resisted before the first naming. The paper trail starts in eighteen sixty-nine."

"Before the Articles."

"Before the Articles."

June looked down the row. Nine clauses were no longer a staircase descending in the dark. They were answers. An answer had a question behind it, a drafter, a date, and language that might have changed between versions.

"I want the precedent files."

"Promise."

"No blind promises."

"Then no files."

"You brought me here because you can't file without me. If I walk away, you still can't."

"If you walk away, I lose nothing I had at breakfast."

Tess's eggs arrived. She salted them before the waitress had fully released the plate.

June picked up the card for bride one. "This says residence was added after she refused the house. Clause one required me to reside there. Those are not necessarily the same wording."

"Correct."

"So there is an earlier version."

"Correct."

"And if the Trust relied on later wording while claiming an unbroken eighteen seventy-one instrument, that's a record problem."

"Now you understand why I bought your voice."

June put the card back exactly where it had been. "I will examine your case. I will not promise to file it. I will not carry it without knowing what it contains. I will not let you name an obligation for me in a diner."

Tess cut into an egg. "Say the first sentence again."

"I agree to examine the case. Examination creates no promise to act."

"Good enough."

"It wasn't what you asked for."

"I ask for what I want. I take what advances it."

She pulled a smaller envelope from the middle of the file and slid it across. It contained a list of file numbers and repositories, not the files themselves. County archives. Trust filings. Local Nine minutes. Three private collections. Beside each was a woman's name.

June read the first two entries aloud into her recorder. Tess stopped eating and listened with her head slightly turned, the hearing aid toward June.

"Why record me at the Hall?" June asked.

"I needed to know whether you understood the vow."

"I didn't."

"I know. You asked what it was, not what it meant. Lenora did the same thing the first time they read clause two to her. Exact same construction."

That landed harder than the death record. Not resemblance. Precedent.

June closed the envelope. "What makes the case dangerous to me?"

"It needs an active clause. You let one reach far enough to create a challenge, then stop it before satisfaction."

"Which clause?"

"Not until you read the file. You haven't seen enough to consent to the risk."

June had not expected the boundary from Tess. It made the threat more credible.

"Send the first file to a neutral address. No contact with Cyd. Break either condition and examination ends."

"Agreed."

Tess showed her a withdrawn court petition. An attorney had tested Lenora's objection after her death. The Trust had bought the holder of the attorney's mortgage, called the loan, and offered to correct the default if she withdrew. Human paper had been enough.

"Her wife left because nobody told her what helping me could cost," Tess said.

"And you still ambush people in diners."

"I just showed you the price before asking for more. That's the improvement."

June examined the disposition. "Is she dead?"

"No."

"Then eating alone is a choice."

"Hers."

Tess returned the petition to the file. "I am not offering friendship. I am offering organized evidence and a motive you can see coming."

"At the moment, that's unusually competitive."

Tess gave her one final index card. It carried no bride's name, only two dates and the phrase *construction accepted after death.*

"What is this?"

"The reason my case needs you alive. Dead women become precedent. Living women can object before the institution decides what their silence meant."

June returned the card. "Then keep me alive long enough to disagree with you."

"That is the arrangement."

Tess recorded the whole sentence exactly as carefully as June would have.

June closed the envelope. "You said the last woman who agreed to help you. That wasn't Lenora."

Tess laid down her fork.

For the first time, she did not answer immediately.

"My case is eleven years old," she said. "Only a living bride can file it. You are the first living bride in nineteen years."

"That still doesn't answer me."

"The last woman who agreed to help me is the reason I eat alone."
