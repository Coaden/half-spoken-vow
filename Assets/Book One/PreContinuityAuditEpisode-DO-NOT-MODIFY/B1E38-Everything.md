# Episode Thirty-Eight: Everything

Episode Thirty-Eight: Everything

June put the certified transcript on Cyd's kitchen table and said the lie started on page one.

Cyd looked at the stack. "That is four hundred pages."

"The relevant part is thirty-two."

"Comforting."

She turned off the stove, carried both coffee mugs to the table, and put her phone face down.

"Before page one: are you in immediate danger?"

"Not immediate by the definition in my safety plan."

"Human answer. Is somebody coming through that door tonight?"

"No."

"Are you sick, bleeding, or about to become anything?"

"No."

"Good. Then I get to be angry in order."

June started with the deposition. She read Pratt naming her, Elias speaking the half-vow, Delisle accepting it into the record. She explained the attachment, the Hall, her closed roster line, and why every member could smell the lie in the paper.

Cyd stopped her at attachment. "Direction how?"

June pointed toward Sewickley without checking a compass. "That way. Rough distance. Stronger when we're close, worse when we're apart too long."

"Does he know this address?"

"Not from the attachment alone."

"Can he hear us?"

"No telepathy. No shared emotion. No compulsion from the bond itself."

"Does it make you want him?"

June took too long. "It creates symptoms. Wanting is also true. They aren't the same fact."

Cyd wrote that on the back of an electric bill. "Continue."

Cyd interrupted only to make the impossible facts practical.

"Sunlight kills him?"

"Direct sunlight."

"Can he come into this house?"

"Only if you invite him. You can revoke it."

"Revoked in advance. Continue."

"You cannot revoke an invitation you never gave."

"Excellent. I have successfully not invited him. Put that in whichever ledger keeps him off my porch."

"Human property law also keeps him off your porch."

"I am relieved one legal system here has police I recognize."

June read clause one, the counted nights, Nadia's ledger, Tess's case, and the safe-room denial. She put the brass-key receipt on the table and explained returning the slot.

"You had nowhere for the moon," Cyd said.

"Kenneth and Elias cleared a hillside."

"When?"

"Last night."

"And today you came to work?"

"I lost access before the session."

"That was not my question."

"Yes. I intended to work."

Cyd pressed both hands to the table. "Your body broke itself apart on a hillside, and your recovery plan was billable hours."

"Independent contractors don't have recovery leave."

"You have me."

"I hadn't told you what I needed recovery from."

"Whose decision was that?"

Cyd stood so fast the chair struck the wall.

"You came here after a client meeting and sat in this kitchen and lied to me while you were nine days from turning into a wolf with nowhere to go."

"Yes."

"Do not use the deposition voice."

"I don't have another one for this."

"Find it."

June tried again without the cadence she used in hostile rooms. "I was afraid you would think I was dangerous. I was afraid you'd tell me to leave the agency. I was afraid if I said it here, it would become the only true thing about me."

Cyd's anger did not disappear. It changed shape.

"Better," she said. "Still infuriating. Continue."

June told her about Kearse, the schedule she traded, Elias's compelled answer, and the client access revoked that morning. Cyd paced between the stove and sink, swearing in precise bursts.

She made June distinguish every ordinary consequence from every supernatural one. The termination could be challenged through counsel. The lease remained a lease. Missing income did not vanish because Kearse had used compulsion to identify where pressure would hurt. Milo could still quit a job, visit a doctor, and refuse ordinary requests while surety attached to his standing.

"I need the borders," Cyd said. "If everything becomes magic, I can't tell where a normal subpoena works."

"Everything ordinary still works."

"Not mercy. Jurisdiction."

June almost smiled. "You should have been a lawyer."

"I enjoy lunch."

"So the dead-looking man gave your job to the charming bastard under supernatural duress."

"He gave information. Kearse used it."

"And you gave Kearse the chance."

"Yes."

"Good. I mean not good. I mean thank you for including your part before I had to drag it out of you."

June reached Milo. She explained surety and what default could do to him.

Cyd sat again.

"Does he know?"

"He accepted after every consequence was read."

"That was not my question. Does he know what you're planning?"

"I don't have a plan."

"Then he knows more than you do."

"He knows the consequences he accepted."

"Does he know you considered cutting the attachment?"

"No."

"Does he know completion could release him?"

"Yes."

"And he accepted knowing you could still refuse?"

"Yes."

Cyd leaned back. "Then stop talking about him like he tripped into traffic. He made a terrible informed choice. You can hate it without stealing it."

June had heard the principle from Elias and Kenneth. It sounded different from a human who could smell nothing and still recognized agency.

June finished with the cut she had refused because the deed belonged to Cyd.

The kitchen went silent.

"You decided I wouldn't close the agency," Cyd said.

"Vera said the deed had to be yours."

"Exactly. Mine to refuse too. You took even refusal away by never asking."

"I protected the agency."

"You protected your idea of yourself as the only person allowed to pay."

The sentence found the structure beneath June's choices. She had called it independence while controlling every sacrifice, including those other people were forbidden to offer.

"I decided I wouldn't ask."

"Same arrogance in a nicer coat."

"It would cost you clients."

"They are my clients."

"And nine employees."

"Mine too."

"You would have said yes."

"Probably. That is why you should have asked."

"Would you say yes now?"

Cyd considered rather than rewarding the question. "I don't know. I might decide one closed day is cheap. I might decide your attachment isn't the emergency. I might tell Vera to find a deed that doesn't advertise my employees' workplace. But I would decide."

"The offer is still open."

"Are you asking me?"

June felt the direction under her ribs. "No. I'm telling you the option exists."

"Good. That's information, not a request. We're learning."

June had expected disbelief, fear, perhaps the end of the friendship. Cyd's anger found a narrower injury: June had assigned her a limit without consent while building an entire moral case against everyone who did the same.

"I'm sorry."

"You keep apologizing after choices you made alone. I want one before."

"How does that work?"

"You call me before doing the next catastrophic favor. I get to say whether I can survive it."

"What if telling you creates risk?"

"Name the risk and let me decide whether to hear more."

"What if there's no time?"

"Keep yourself alive. Tell me afterward without pretending the emergency was a general license."

"What if an institution bars you?"

"Tell me there is a barred room and what ordinary consequences leave it. I don't need a folding chair inside every cult basement."

"Local hall."

"Do not spend your correction on that."

Cyd pulled the transcript toward her. "New terms. I handle the agency, client calls, and your lease negotiation. You tell me every ordinary consequence before it arrives. I do not join the Hall, meet a vampire, witness a vow, or become anybody's leverage."

"Trustee."

"I will call him a vampire in my own kitchen."

"Fair."

"Another term. Nobody gets invited here because you need neutral ground. Not the elegant one, your aunt, or the smiling bridge man."

"Kearse isn't—"

"I did not request taxonomy."

"Agreed."

"And if you need somewhere human to sleep, ask. The guest room has a door and no ancient clauses."

"Your house has people I could hurt during the moon."

"Then not during the moon. See how terms work?"

"You stop deciding ignorance protects me."

June looked at the pages she had used to keep Cyd outside. "Agreed."

"Ordinary agreed or magic agreed?"

"Ordinary. Fully real. No supernatural enforcement."

"Good. I prefer the kind where I can call you a jerk without filing a construction."

Cyd took out her phone. In six minutes she moved tomorrow's assignments, arranged a payment plan with June's landlord, and booked a consultation about the client interference.

She did each thing in language nobody at the Hall would call powerful. Sondra could cover the morning if the expedited copy moved. The landlord would accept half rent Friday and the balance after the next invoice, with no fee. An employment lawyer offered twenty minutes before court if Cyd emailed the termination letter and performance records now.

No witness. No seal. No scent. Three ordinary arrangements that changed what June could survive.

June could have done none of it without spending a day she no longer had.

"What did that cost?" June asked.

"Two favors, one schedule headache, and possible consultation fees. None creates a claim against your body."

"I can repay the fees."

"Later, after I decide whether to retain counsel. You are not converting help into an invoice before I hang up."

Cyd opened a new note on her phone. "List the things you still have not told me."

"I told you everything."

"That is never literally true. List the categories."

June thought. "Elias's original motive. My mother's reason for leaving. What she may have taken. Who inspects severed records. Whether attachments are becoming rare."

"You don't know those answers."

"No."

"Good. Unknown is different from withheld. What do you know and still intend to keep private?"

"Things Elias told me voluntarily that do not create ordinary consequences for you."

"Acceptable. I asked for danger, not ownership of your intimacy."

The new terms had a limit in both directions. Cyd was not demanding every secret as the price of staying. She wanted the facts that reached her life before they arrived disguised as emergencies.

Cyd ended the last call. "I will handle the agency, the clients, and the lease. In exchange, you will stop deciding on my behalf what I can survive."
