# Episode Thirty-Six: Three Nights

Episode Thirty-Six: Three Nights

The basement padlock failed before moonrise, and June had six minutes to choose between the hillside and the house she had refused.

The wall anchor came free with a fist-sized piece of mortar attached. Chain struck concrete. Above them, somebody crossed the kitchen floor.

They had inspected the anchor after Kenneth's warning and installed a second plate across sounder brick. It failed anyway. The inward-opening coal-room door had not yet been replaced because the landlord wanted drawings, a deposit, and proof of insurance. June's careful alternative had survived on paper until her body began to change the load.

The protocol lay clipped to a board beside the door. Every basement boundary depended on hardware now loose around Kenneth's boots.

Kenneth said, "We leave now."

June's hands had begun to cramp. Every smell in the basement arrived too sharply: rust, damp plaster, Kenneth's fear, her own blood where a nail had split.

"Not Sewickley," she said.

"Then the hillside."

"That plan was for overflow after the house."

"It's the only plan that doesn't put you under an occupied floor."

Above them, the footsteps stopped. A child's voice asked a question June could not make out. The crib in apartment two rolled six inches and squeaked.

That sound decided what pride had been trying to keep open.

"Houses below it."

"I can clear the path. Elias can hold the lower boundary."

The name pulled through her body like a direction.

June took out the written plan she had prepared after refusing the house. "Call him. Speaker."

Kenneth did.

Elias answered on the first ring.

"I need help," June said.

The sentence cost more than the broken hardware.

"Name it," he said.

Not *where are you*. Not *why did you refuse the court*. He made her request define his authority before urgency could enlarge it.

"Hillside above Polish Hill. Kenneth clears people from the upper path. You hold the lower boundary from at least thirty feet. You do not approach unless Kenneth says a person is in immediate danger. You leave before dawn even if I'm still changed."

"Yes."

"No restraint. No transport. No Trust staff."

"Agreed."

"If I speak after it starts, you do not treat it as consent to anything."

"I will not."

"If I move toward you, you retreat while keeping the lower path blocked."

"Yes."

"If retreat puts a human behind you?"

"I warn Kenneth and turn your movement uphill. I do not use the attachment as a command."

"No blood."

"I have brought none."

"No weapon against me."

"None."

June's fingers locked around the phone. Kenneth took it only after she nodded.

Kenneth repeated every term as witness.

Then he read the emergency exception from June's hillside plan. Immediate danger meant a human inside the boundary, June past the outer gate, uncontrolled bleeding, or fire. Property damage was not immediate danger. June moving toward Elias was not immediate danger unless another person stood in the path. Sounds of distress were not permission to approach.

"Correct," June managed.

"Accepted," Elias said.

For once, the institutions were nowhere in the procedure. Three people used ordinary agreement, a recorder, and the authority June retained over her own body.

They reached the hillside with two minutes left. Kenneth locked the access gate and walked the upper trail shouting that a gas line had ruptured. Human beings accepted infrastructure failure more readily than lineage truth.

June heard doors close along the row below. A dog barked until someone pulled it inside. Kenneth called the city emergency line and reported a suspected odor without inventing a leak he could not smell; the operator promised a crew. That gave them perhaps twenty minutes before helpful strangers became the next danger.

"Utility truck coming," he said when he returned. "We may have to move them around the lower street."

"Do it without naming June," Elias called.

"I know how secrets work, Marrow."

Their irritation steadied her more than comfort would have.

Elias stood below among bare trees, a dark figure beside the retaining wall. He had brought no one.

"Boundary set," he called. "Thirty-four feet."

June made herself verify what she could still see. Kenneth above the steel gate. Elias below the retaining wall. Houses east beyond two hundred yards of trees. A chain-link fence west. No person inside.

"Recorder?"

"Running at the upper gate," Kenneth said.

"Time?"

"Seven fifty-six."

Moonrise was seven fifty-eight. Her body's schedule had no grace period for equipment failure.

June put her coat on the ground because suddenly fabric was unbearable.

The change began.

Her first knuckle displaced with a small wet sound. Pain broke the number she was trying to count. She went to one knee, then both hands, and the ground arrived through senses too large for skin. Her spine contracted and lengthened in successive instructions. Teeth loosened before settling into a mouth that no longer fit human speech.

June tried to say *boundary*. What came out carried no word.

Kenneth answered anyway. "Boundary remains. Nobody enters."

It was not anger and not release. Her body took instructions from a calendar it had never been taught to read. Bone moved. Breath became too large for her chest. The hillside filled with scent: wet leaves, old brick, rats under stone, Kenneth's recent passage, Elias below like cold iron and held fear.

June lost language before she lost memory.

Memory changed shape. It was not sentences but arrangements: the gate that meant stop, the houses carrying sleeping bodies, Kenneth as kin and witness, Elias as direction and prohibition at once. Her humanity did not sit inside the changed body giving orders. It survived as paths worn deeply enough to follow without words.

She remembered the boundaries. She remembered asking.

She ran uphill until the gate stopped her. Metal shook. Kenneth stayed beyond it and described the perimeter in a steady voice.

The gate bowed under her first impact. Kenneth did not grab it. He backed to the distance marked in the plan and kept talking.

"Gate is a boundary. You chose it. Turn left or right."

June struck it again. The latch held. Above Kenneth, a porch light appeared through the trees. He lowered his voice but did not stop.

"Left is open hillside. Right is open hillside. Nobody is asking you to come through."

She turned right.

"Upper path clear. Houses east, two hundred yards. Wall west. Elias below. Nobody inside."

June turned downhill.

The attachment found Elias. Her changed body wanted the shortest distance without possessing any right to it. She struck the earth once, veered, and circled the trees.

Below, Elias moved backward in exact proportion, never fast enough to become pursuit. His coat brushed dead branches. The iron smell of his fear sharpened as distance narrowed, but he did not use fear as evidence June intended harm.

"Twenty-nine feet," he called.

Kenneth answered, "Move south. Clear behind you."

"Moving."

Their words created a corridor her changed body could not read as law but could encounter as space.

Elias did not move closer.

Hours became smell and motion. Once, a man climbed the lower wall despite the warning sign. Elias intercepted him beyond June's boundary, speaking calmly until the man retreated. He never crossed inward.

The utility crew arrived at nine sixteen. Amber lights moved along the lower street. Kenneth met them above the access road and let them test the air. No gas. He blamed a solvent spill in the abandoned lot, an ordinary possibility that brought a warning instead of excavation.

June smelled three humans and wanted the motion they made. Elias shifted downslope, placing his voice between her and theirs without placing his body inside the boundary.

"Lower path occupied," he called. "Turn uphill."

The words were not compulsion. June turned because Kenneth struck the upper fence with a wrench, giving her a louder direction that did not lead through people.

Kenneth checked the gate. "Still holding. Lower path?"

"Clear," Elias answered.

"Time?"

"Five twenty-three."

"Your departure time was five fifteen," Kenneth said.

"The lower path is not clear."

"Plan says you leave before dawn."

"It also says I keep humans outside. There is a pedestrian at the bus stop."

June heard the conflict without language. Elias had reached the edge of two promises and chosen delay. The eastern sky was already changing color.

Kenneth swore and ran for the street. A minute later, he called that the pedestrian had boarded. "Clear. Go now."

Elias moved toward the retaining wall.

The eastern sky had begun to thin.

June was herself in fragments before she was human again. Cold arrived first. Then pain. Then the fact of lying naked behind a fallen tree with dawn six minutes away.

Her coat landed beside her, thrown by Kenneth from beyond the gate.

"Can I come in?" he called.

"Yes."

Kenneth entered carrying a blanket, water, and the emergency bag they had staged above the gate. He kept his eyes on the ground until June had the coat closed.

"Bleeding?"

"Hands. Mouth. Not much."

"Can I look?"

"Not yet."

"All right. Can you stand?"

June tried and failed. Kenneth did not convert failure into permission to lift her.

"Ask again in a minute," she said.

"I will."

Her voice barely worked.

He approached with his face turned away and wrapped the coat around her. "We made it."

June looked downhill. Elias remained at the lower boundary.

"Leave," she said.

He did not answer.

The first direct sunlight reached between two buildings and touched his forearm.

Smoke rose from his sleeve.

Elias moved then, but not before checking the lower path once more. He crossed into shadow behind the retaining wall with his coat burning at the cuff.

"Idiot," Kenneth said, without humor.

June woke fully at dawn with her coat over her and Elias sitting a careful distance away in the last band of shadow, badly burned along one forearm where the sun had reached him.
