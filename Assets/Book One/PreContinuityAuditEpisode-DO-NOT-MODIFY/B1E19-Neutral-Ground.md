# Episode Nineteen: Neutral Ground

Episode Nineteen: Neutral Ground

Halfway across the Sixteenth Street span, Lionel Kearse greeted June by reading her demand to Local Nine back to her from memory.

"Production is requested in writing and named aloud before a witness, so that neither custodian may later dispute receipt."

River wind moved the end of his scarf. He was broad, silver-haired, and dressed like a man who expected every room to contain a chair for him.

"You missed a comma," June said.

"Did I?"

"No. I wanted to know whether you'd pretend."

He laughed with enough pleasure that she nearly liked him.

"Miss Havlik, the Marrows described you as exact. They neglected to say entertaining."

"The Marrows described me to you?"

"Not voluntarily. Walk with me. Standing still on a bridge makes people assume despair."

They moved toward the center. Traffic passed behind the barrier in wet bursts. Below, the Allegheny carried city light in broken strips.

"Why a bridge?" June asked.

"Because neither bank can claim the conversation. Your side believes presence. Mine believes paper. Here we provide both and disappoint everyone equally."

He handed her a single folded page but did not release it.

"I offer an alliance," he said. "Access to my trust's filed objections to the Articles. Notice of any petition touching your standing. A written undertaking not to compel Elias Marrow through you. In return, you disclose the present limits of his exposure and any change in the sequence before public filing."

"You want advance notice."

"I want not to learn institutional events from courthouse clerks who eat lunch at their desks."

"What happens if the Marrow Trust fails?"

Kearse answered without false surprise. "Its properties transfer or liquidate. Human obligations survive where ordinary law recognizes them. Lineage offices close unless another trust assumes them."

"You assume them."

"I petition to."

"Including the Articles."

"If they remain operative."

"Then helping me defeat a clause could reduce what you inherit."

"Or prove the instrument can be administered without killing its named party. A functioning asset is worth more than a scandal attached to four graves."

June leaned against the opposite rail. "You said you prefer me alive. You didn't say free."

"Freedom is not a condition trusts know how to hold. I can promise narrower things."

He named what his house stood to gain if June survived clause two: precedent against Marrow exclusivity, access to dormant claims, and proof the Articles could be challenged by a living party. He did not pretend those gains aligned with Local Nine's charter or Elias's safety.

"If our interests diverge?" June asked.

"I notify you before acting against yours where notice does not destroy my claim."

"That exception destroys the promise."

"It accurately sizes it."

June wrote the exception in larger words. A dangerous promise became slightly less dangerous when its hole was visible.

"Why help me?"

"Because I prefer you alive and the Marrow Trust embarrassed. These interests currently agree. I promise to notify you when they stop."

There it was: the price, named without apology.

June took the page and read it aloud. The undertaking not to compel Elias applied only *through the person or standing of June Havlik.* Kearse remained free to compel him directly.

"This protects me from being used as the route," she said. "Not him from being used."

"Correct."

"And present limits means I have to tell you facts I don't know yet."

"When you learn them."

"No. I will not sell future information blind."

"Then revise it."

He produced a fountain pen. The hospitality was so complete it had included a tool for resisting him.

June made him read every pronoun with its referent supplied. *We* became Kearse's trust, not Kearse personally. *Notice* meant written service, not a phone call. *Change in the sequence* included a suspended clause, a matured clause, or a filed election.

By the end, his one-page offer carried eight handwritten definitions.

"You improve documents by distrusting them," Kearse said.

"You enjoy resistance. That isn't safer."

"No. It is less lonely."

The attachment pulled faintly toward the far bank. Elias was somewhere beyond the buildings, unaware his exposure occupied a line in June's negotiation.

"One exclusion," she said. "Nothing I disclose may be used to force an answer from Elias about my body, dwelling, work, family, or the vow."

Kearse's expression sharpened. "That removes most facts with value."

"It leaves procedure."

"Procedure is where the value usually hides."

"Then you still have a bargain."

He accepted the exclusion subject to the one identified fact. June did not notice that a scheduling location sat outside every category she had named.

June struck *present limits* and wrote: *one scheduling fact, identified before disclosure and accepted by both parties as sufficient consideration.*

Kearse read the change aloud.

"You have turned an intelligence arrangement into one fact."

"You offered an alliance. Those take longer than a bridge."

"Fair. What do you require before supplying it?"

"Proof that the third copy exists and carries standing."

His expression did not change, but the pause did.

"Aloysius Pratt talks when somebody appreciates his card index," June said.

"Mr. Pratt talks when somebody remains still long enough. It is one of his finer qualities."

"Can you prove the holder?"

"I can prove a holder answered."

"That's not the same."

"No."

Kearse leaned against the rail. "Three copies were placed in eighteen seventy-one. The Trust kept the sealed original. Local Nine received a certified counterpart. The third was deposited with a custodian acceptable to both and granted standing to enforce if either signatory failed. The custodian's name was omitted from the index available to ordinary counsel."

"Why?"

"Protection from pressure, according to the filing. A sensible arrangement until everyone who remembered the name died."

"You remember."

"I was not a party then. My predecessor objected to the omission. I have his objection."

"Access to that objection is in your offer?"

"It is."

"Then read the relevant part."

He did. The predecessor had protested that an unnamed holder with dormant enforcement power made notice impossible. The objection had been denied because the holder's answer, when required, would identify the office.

Not the person. The office.

"Have they answered before?" June asked.

"Not in any surviving record."

"So you can't prove they're still there."

"I can prove the copy remains operative. Last year the Trust tried to consolidate the counterparts. The request was refused by an unsigned reply bearing the original custodial seal."

"You have it?"

"A copy. You may inspect it if we agree."

June asked him to describe the seal without showing her. Kearse did: an ordinary parish device pressed over a date, with the custodian identified only by office. Then she unfolded Pratt's copied index card and read its blank aloud.

"These don't prove the same holder."

"No."

"You keep agreeing when I tell you your evidence is incomplete."

"Incomplete evidence is not false evidence. I am courting your judgment, Miss Havlik, not trying to survive cross-examination."

"You're doing both."

"Excellent."

He offered the reply for her to hold while keeping one gloved finger on its edge. The copy carried no name, but it quoted the custodial provision word for word. Whoever answered had either the third counterpart or access to a text missing from both public indices.

June read the quotation aloud. River noise took the softer words, so Kearse repeated them. The holder would answer only a properly matured clause or a joint request by both original parties.

"They won't identify themselves for curiosity," June said.

"Neither will most sensible people."

"Then your letter must have alleged a matured clause."

"It asked what notice would be required if one matured."

"You showed them the sequence was active."

"The Hall's filing already did that."

Another technically true sentence. June began to understand why Kearse enjoyed her.

"Show me the envelope," she said.

Kearse produced a transparent sleeve. The address contained no person, only an office and a parish postal box. The reply had arrived by hand rather than through the mail.

"Who delivered it?"

"A woman who declined to give a name and waited while my secretary signed receipt. Human, as far as anyone could establish."

"Your people searched her."

"They checked for weapons and followed her after she left. She entered a bus, changed twice, and disappeared into a hospital lobby."

"So you pressured the protected custodian immediately after they answered."

"I investigated a messenger."

"That sentence is why the holder stayed unnamed."

Kearse accepted the rebuke. "Likely."

June asked him to include a promise not to trace any future messenger connected to a notice he shared with her. He refused an absolute restriction and accepted one limited to communications concerning June's own standing.

"You keep half of every moral improvement," she said.

"Half is how institutions change without admitting they were wrong."

"I am not an institution."

"Not yet."

The phrase was too interested. June struck it from the conversational record by refusing to answer.

Kearse did not fill the silence. That restraint distinguished him from men who mistook continued speech for consent and made his patience feel like another well-maintained asset.

June read his page again. Kearse was useful because he had spent a century preparing for the Marrows to weaken. He was dangerous for the same reason.

"I will provide one scheduling fact about Elias's exposure after I inspect your objection and the reply. You will provide access and notice. No future duty. No exclusivity. No authority to speak in my name."

"And my undertaking regarding compulsion?"

"Keep it. Narrow as it is."

He signed. June signed only an acknowledgment of receipt, not the bargain itself.

"You do read everything," Kearse said.

"People keep putting my name in things."

At the far end of the span, he stopped beneath a lamp.

"One pleasantry before we part. I have already written to the third holder."

June folded the terms into her coat. "You said the holder was unnamed."

"I addressed the office."

"And?"

Kearse smiled.

"They wrote back."
