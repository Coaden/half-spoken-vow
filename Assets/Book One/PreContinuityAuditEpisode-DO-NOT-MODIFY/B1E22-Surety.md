# Episode Twenty-Two: Surety

Episode Twenty-Two: Surety

Pratt said the word surety, and every member in the Hall turned toward Milo before the sentence was finished.

Milo stood beside the coffee urn. Three hundred bodies identified him as June's line before any page could name him.

June smelled his fear rise.

"Continue," Marta said.

Pratt held the certified notice in both hands. "Upon satisfaction of residence, the bride shall provide surety for faithful performance. Such surety shall be one member in good standing of her borne line—"

"Stop," June said.

Marta occupied the chair at the center table. Elias had not come. Pratt attended as daylight counsel, though night pressed black against the high windows.

"You aren't in the speaking circle," Marta said.

"I'm the named party. If the Trust wants performance, it can hear a clarification."

Pratt lowered the page. "I am willing to hear it."

"Read borne line again."

He did.

The room looked at Milo again. The response passed through them like a vote nobody had called.

"What does the Trust mean by line?" June asked.

"A succession of persons identified by descent or appointment in an accepted record."

"Appointment."

"Yes."

Marta said, "A line isn't appointed."

Pratt turned to her. "Among trustees, kinship is an office."

"Among members, it's a body."

"The clause says borne."

Marta made Pratt define *borne* from the Trust's own glossary. He read: established by descent, issue, or other accepted succession.

"Other accepted succession is appointment," Marta said.

"Among trustees."

"Then your definition smuggles office into blood."

Pratt examined the wording with professional discomfort. "It permits multiple systems of lineage."

"But the clause requires a member," June said. "Membership cannot be appointed by the Trust."

"No."

"And it requires my line, which your register cannot identify without the Hall."

"Correct."

"So the Trust cannot supply the first status and the Hall cannot supply the second."

Marta said, "The Hall supplies membership and kinship. It does not supply surety office."

They made Pratt read the sentence again. This time every institutional dependency was audible. *One member in good standing of her borne line, accepted into the office.* Three separate facts. Membership. Kinship. Acceptance.

The Hall had turned toward Milo after the first two. The clause still needed the third.

"And then asks for an office," June said. "Those are separate tests. The Hall can identify who belongs to my line. The Trust has to identify who can hold surety. You cannot use the same fact twice."

Pratt's delight arrived despite the room. "That is a respectable objection."

"It is not entertainment," Marta said.

"No, but craft remains craft under pressure."

June took Kearse's filed objection from her bag. "A prior ruling says written appointment cannot create borne kinship and borne kinship alone cannot create an office. Read the denial."

Pratt accepted the copy, verified its seal, and read the operative passage aloud. The rival objection had lost, but the ruling admitted the distinction June needed.

"I move for a stay until the Trust states which record creates surety," she said. "Written appointment or borne line."

Pratt asked Marta whether the Hall would recognize a written appointment if Milo signed it outside the room.

"No. A member's obligation has to be named in presence."

"Would the Hall recognize his spoken acceptance without a Trust filing?"

"As a promise. Not as the office your clause enforces."

"Then both are required," June said. "Neither can be inferred from the other."

Milo shifted beside the urn. Fear thickened. June could smell the desire to repair his leak before he opened his mouth.

"Do not make guilt the missing act," she said.

"I didn't say anything."

"Your body did."

The room heard that too. Member privacy did not exist in the way June wished it did. Everyone present knew Milo wanted to volunteer, and knowing still did not authorize them to convert desire into office.

Marta ordered the speaking circle cleared until the stay was decided. Milo stepped outside it. The physical act separated what his body revealed from what the record could accept.

"You don't move here," Marta said.

"Then call it an objection with a clock."

Marta looked around the Hall. Nobody could pretend not to know whose blood answered June's. Their bodies had already made that record. The question was whether that answer could volunteer Milo for an office.

Pratt consulted the clause and Constance's new marginal instruction. "The proposed surety must speak acceptance in his own words. Silence cannot supply it."

June asked to hear the instruction in full. Pratt read it from the certified preparation note. After every stated consequence, the proposed surety must receive a clear opportunity to withdraw. Presence, fear, kinship, and prior volunteer language were insufficient. Acceptance had to follow the last consequence.

"Who added it?" June asked.

"The senior trustee."

"Ask why."

Pratt called Constance on speaker. She answered after the second ring.

"Mrs. Marrow, the named party asks why the personal-acceptance instruction was added."

"Because the prior procedure permitted silence and kinship to perform work only speech may do."

"Is that an admission the Renn execution was invalid?" June asked.

"It is an admission that I would not repeat the procedure. Validity requires the full record and a forum prepared to judge it."

Tess would despise the distinction. June recorded it accurately anyway.

"Will you oppose the stay?"

"No. Forty-eight hours is appropriate."

Constance offered no kindness beyond a correct interval. It was enough to change what happened next.

"Last night?"

"Yes."

"After Tess Renn requested the amendment records."

Pratt folded the note over the clause. "I cannot speak to motive."

"You can speak to sequence."

"The request preceded the instruction."

Tess would call it evidence of guilt. June heard something less convenient: the Trust correcting a procedure before it harmed Milo, while still insisting the clause must run.

She asked Pratt whether the instruction could be withdrawn before the final reading. He said it could not. Once entered into preparation minutes, it governed this execution even if Constance changed her mind. June requested a copy for Tess and another for Milo. Pratt agreed to both, placing evidence of the old harm in the hands of the woman seeking to repeat none of it.

Marta made Pratt state whether the correction admitted the earlier execution was defective. He declined. The Hall reacted in breath and scent; nobody could hide that they remembered Lenora's surety.

"Your side supplied the original kinship finding," Pratt told Marta.

"Our side identified a body," Marta said. "Yours called it an office."

"And both recorded acceptance."

"Then both can answer for getting it wrong."

The collision could not be solved by choosing which record was real. Both were. The injury lived in the space where each institution assumed the other had supplied consent.

June looked up. "That wasn't the Renn procedure."

"No. The instruction was entered last night."

Tess needed that fact. June stored it.

"Then nobody has accepted," she said. "No office exists."

"The clause allows forty-eight hours to provide one after the line is identified," Pratt said. "I can stay the reading for that period."

"Do it."

Marta required the terms spoken for the room. Pratt read them. Forty-eight hours. No surety attached before a personal acceptance. If none accepted, the clause would select the nearest eligible member of the borne line under the standing construction.

Milo.

June turned to him. "Do not say anything."

"I wasn't going to."

Fear contradicted him in front of everyone.

"Milo."

"I heard you."

The stay was entered in the minutes. Yolanda read it back. For one moment June had what she came for: two days in which to prove the bride could stand as her own line or that no person could be made surety without a separate appointment.

June required the stay to prohibit anyone from preparing Milo's acceptance statement. Pratt objected that drafting did not bind him.

"Drafting creates momentum," she said. "A prepared sentence becomes the sentence frightened people think they should say."

Marta agreed. "If he accepts, it must be his wording."

Pratt amended the stay. No form, script, or suggested phrasing. Any final acceptance had to originate in Milo's own speech after the consequences.

Milo said, "I know how to talk."

"You start over every second sentence," June told him.

"That's still mine."

"Exactly. Keep it that way."

Yolanda read the amendment. Milo's imperfect speech gained protection from every institution in the room. A person could sound uncertain and remain free; polished language was not consent.

June requested four copies: herself, Milo, Tess, and any future challenge. Pratt made them before closing the file.

She gave Milo his copy in front of the Hall.

"Read it before you decide anything," she said.

"I was there."

"Presence isn't the same as review."

Marta supported her. "Take it home. Read it alone. If you return, return because the words remained yours after the room was gone."

That was member consent at its hardest: the room could verify a body, but it also knew its own presence exerted pressure. Sending Milo away protected the next answer from the very witnesses who would later accept it.

Milo folded the copy into his jacket. "Forty-eight hours."

"Not a challenge," June said. "Time. Use it."

Pratt folded the clause.

"The session is suspended."

Chairs moved. Breath released. June crossed the speaking circle toward Milo.

He stepped into it before she reached him.

Marta said his name once. A warning.

Milo faced the room. His voice began too high, stopped, and began again.

"I am Milo Havlik, a member in good standing and of June Havlik's borne line. I volunteer to stand as surety under clause two. I say it in front of the Hall so nobody can say she asked me."

The room heard him.

The forty-eight-hour stay remained. So did the volunteered fact inside it.

June reached the edge of the circle.

"Take it back."

Milo looked at her, frightened and proud.

"No," he said. "I volunteer."
