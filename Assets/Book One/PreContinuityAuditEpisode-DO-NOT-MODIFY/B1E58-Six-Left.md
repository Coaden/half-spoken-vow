# Episode Fifty-Eight: Six Left

Episode Fifty-Eight: Six Left

The Trust register said six while the Hall ledger still said nine, and June refused to leave until both agreed.

"Read yours," she told Pratt over speaker.

He read the struck-clause notation. One, two, three removed permanently. Six operative clauses remained.

"Read the operative numbers, not only the total."

Pratt did. Clauses four through nine, each by number and current status. Clause four could still issue notice concerning completed residence. Clause five still governed registration. Clause six preserved the old schedule of institutional appearances. Seven through nine held remedies, succession, and the joint peace. None had vanished merely because the first three had failed.

"Any appeal pending?" June asked.

"Kearse has reserved the right. He has filed nothing. The struck clauses remain struck unless the joint holder reverses Delisle."

"And until then?"

"Six."

Pratt sounded exhausted. June trusted the count more for surviving exhaustion than she would have trusted a ceremonial declaration.

Yolanda opened the Hall ledger. The old count survived in three places: sequence heading, surety cross-reference, and annual report.

The errors had different causes. The sequence heading had been written before the hearing and never amended. The surety cross-reference had been copied from Kenneth's form. The annual report turned nine into a phrase—every clause remaining in full force—that could outlive any arithmetic if nobody made the writer say what every meant.

June set her stenotype machine at the end of the folding table and rolled in fresh paper.

"Correction session opened at six twelve in the evening," she said. "Persons present: Yolanda Bright as secretary and member; June Havlik as permanent party and custodian; Milo Havlik as released member; Cyd Toomey as human business owner. Mr. Pratt attends by speaker telephone as counsel for the Marrow Trust."

"You made me a person present," Cyd said.

"You came through the door. Try to bear it."

"I have always wanted standing in a church basement."

June made her correct each one and read it aloud. Members present repeated the count. Written register and borne witness settled on six.

Yolanda did not erase the old figures. She drew one line through each nine, dated the correction, and wrote the authority beside it. The annual report received a full sentence because a number alone could not repair a sentence.

"Clauses one, two, and three were struck by accepted joint adjudication," she read. "Clauses four through nine remain operative. Local Nine retains its charter and standing under the joint peace."

Milo repeated it. Then Yolanda did. June read back both statements from her notes.

On the speaker, Pratt turned pages. "The Trust register now matches word for word except that we use remains rather than retains for the charter."

"Why?"

"Because the Trust does not concede the charter was ever at risk of automatic loss."

June considered the difference. "Keep it. The mismatch states an actual disagreement. Match the count, not the theology."

Two records could agree on what happened without pretending they agreed on why.

Milo's release came next. The Trust closed custody. The Hall restored his ordinary standing without surety notation. Milo read both entries and signed only the human receipt for his returned property.

The returned property was disappointingly ordinary: his wallet, keys, telephone, belt, and the small folding knife the Hall required him to surrender before a safe-room night. The Trust had itemized every object. Milo checked the cash in the wallet twice.

"Short?" June asked.

"No. I forgot I had twelve dollars. Custody has improved my finances."

"Don't put that in the record," Cyd said.

June did not. She did record that Milo received every listed item and reserved all ordinary claims. When Pratt read the settlement offer, Milo interrupted at full and final.

"Final as to returned property," he said. "Not final as to missed wages, counsel, or being held."

Pratt revised it. Milo signed after June read the correction aloud.

"Do I need your permission to go home?" he asked Yolanda.

"No."

"The Trust's?"

"No," Pratt said.

"June's?"

"Absolutely not," June said.

Milo grinned for the first time since the surety had taken him. He still did not leave. Freedom included deciding to stay while she finished.

Cyd arrived with the agency plan under one arm and June's lease renewal under the other.

She laid out invoices, calendars, and a yellow legal pad covered in numbers. During June's absences, two law firms had moved rush work elsewhere. One would return if the agency could guarantee coverage. The other would not. Cyd had stopped calling that betrayal sometime after she calculated payroll.

"The good news is we remain a business," she said. "The less-good news is we are a smaller business with an intimate relationship to payment plans."

She read the reduced schedule. Payroll covered. Equipment delayed. Sondra retained as contractor while taking direct work under the negotiated referral agreement.

"No unpaid extra coverage," Cyd said. "No heroic weekends. If a client wants overnight turnaround, the invoice contains overnight money. If they object, they discover the thrilling world of waiting."

June found her own name on three blocks next week and nowhere else.

"You cut my hours."

"I scheduled the hours you can perform without becoming a cautionary tale. You may request more like an adult."

"Denied in advance?"

"Evaluated with suspicion."

Sondra's agreement guaranteed payment for completed pages and let her accept outside work without penalty. It also required her to tell Cyd before taking a conflict, a business obligation enforced by invoices and reputation, not supernatural record.

June signed as witness to the agency plan, not guarantor. Cyd had drawn a box around that word.

June read her lease. The Trust owned the building but Pratt had separated management from lineage claims. Six months, ordinary rent, no condition tied to the Articles.

The invitation language was gone. So was a provision allowing the owner or its agents to enter for lineage inspection. What remained was the human lease: advance notice for repairs, emergency access under Pennsylvania law, late fees, utilities, one elderly heating system, and a prohibition against commercial stenotype storage heavy enough to damage the floor.

"That last one is personal," June said.

"The floor is personal," Cyd replied.

Pratt confirmed on speaker that no trustee could use the lease as invitation. June's existing invitation to Elias remained personal, revocable, and independent of tenancy. The management company would schedule human workers in the ordinary way.

"You negotiated this," Cyd said. "Sign it or find another apartment with your imaginary savings."

June signed. Human contract. Human enforcement. Her threshold remained hers.

She paid the first month from the agency account transfer Cyd had finally released to her as wages. The receipt did not discharge a clause, establish allegiance, or mean anything beyond rent paid. Its ordinariness felt luxurious.

Then Yolanda transferred the roster ledger Marta had given June. They entered custody without repairing the nineteen-year gap.

June placed it inside a padded archival case Pratt had sent over without condition. Yolanda checked the sewing, page count, water stain, and the narrow absence beneath June's mother's entry. They each initialed a separate condition sheet.

"Not the ledger," June said when Yolanda reached for it.

"I know. No new marks in the bound record."

"Say why."

"Because custody changes are tracked outside the object until the Local votes on a permanent annotation procedure. We preserve the disputed state."

June let her continue.

"Keep absence visible," June said.

"Entered."

Milo went home with Kenneth. Cyd went back to rescue a Friday calendar. The Hall emptied until only June and Yolanda remained among the records.

Kenneth did not cross the threshold of the meeting room after exile took effect. Milo carried his property out to the sidewalk, where Kenneth waited in the rain with the garage truck. June watched through the open exterior door as they argued about whether the broken kitchen hinge required three screws or four.

It was not absolution. It was transportation home.

Cyd gathered the signed contracts, kissed two fingers, and pressed them to June's shoulder because June's hands were full of ledger.

"Friday at eight," she said. "A deposition about municipal drainage. Wear something that says I have never been named in an agreement from eighteen seventy-one."

"I own nothing that convincing."

"Then wear black."

The door shut behind her. The coffee urn clicked as it cooled. Folding chairs stood in uneven rows awaiting the next meeting under an agent not yet elected.

"There is one more entry," Yolanda said.

She opened the cross-register reconciliation. Attachments recorded, completed, abandoned, severed. One recent severance appeared in both registers without a corresponding name.

The reconciliation had been created for closure. June and Yolanda had started at the oldest entry and accounted for each status that touched the Articles: Tess, the six prior named brides, June, Elias, Milo's surety. The final column was supposed to prove nothing remained loose from the hearing.

Instead, one line had a date, a classification, and an accomplished act with the center missing.

June pulled her machine closer. "Read the full notation."

Yolanda read the Hall version first. An incomplete attachment had been cut and borne as severed. Two members had witnessed the condition afterward. Their names were present. The person whose attachment had been cut was not.

Pratt's emailed Trust extract described the same severance as executed and entered, with an instrument number that led to no instrument in the shared packet.

"Could the person have requested anonymity?"

"A public alias, yes. No principal at all, no. A cut alters standing. Both systems require someone to carry the altered state."

"Did either witness smell tampering?"

"The statement says they witnessed the absence of attachment. It does not say they witnessed the cut or the record being made."

June typed that distinction.

"A clerical omission?"

"Both sides made the same omission. That is not clerical."

"Date?"

"This month."

"Cutter?"

"Not named. The annotation carries the form Vera Odell uses."

Not proof, June reminded herself. Form was habit, and habit could be copied. Vera's cuts were recorded with a particular reversal: the deed first, the commission second, because no court would enforce her price. This entry had the reversal and neither underlying item.

"Do we contact her tonight?" Yolanda asked.

June looked at the completed closure ledger, the lease in her bag, and the six written where nine had survived that morning.

"No. We preserve it, notify both custodians, and ask for the missing instrument by number. We do not turn one unexplained line into an accusation."

It was a smaller action than pursuit. It was also the only action the evidence supported.

June asked for a copy.

Yolanda handed over the register and pointed to the blank line.

"There is one entry I cannot account for," she said. "And it is recent."
