# Episode Fifty-Six: Price Named

Episode Fifty-Six: Price Named

Before anyone left, June asked that every price be named into the same record as the victory.

"The count is six," she said. "Now record who paid for it."

Marta had already lifted the gavel. Constance's car waited beyond the covered exit. Members stood in the aisles with relief rising toward celebration. June's request stopped them before victory could become a story in which the right people had corrected a document and gone home intact.

"Authority?" Marta asked.

"Permanent party administration. The ruling requires every consequential status change entered in both records."

Delisle confirmed it. Marta set the gavel down.

The room wanted adjournment. Chairs had begun to fold. June remained standing until Delisle reopened the parish register.

She made Tess put away the exhibit boxes. This was not additional argument. No one would hide a human price beneath page numbers.

"Order," June said. "Release. Local agency. Membership. Trustee office. Ledger custody. Each separately."

Milo came first. Pratt read the release. Surety closed without residual claim. Custody ended. Milo repeated in his own words that he was free.

"Free from what?" June asked.

"Surety office, Trust custody, performance under clauses two and three, and any derivative claim based on my acceptance."

"What remains?"

"My membership. My ordinary claims arising from custody. My choice to help you if I make it again."

"Does release erase your acceptance?"

"No. It records that the office existed and ended."

History remained without leverage.

"Price paid," June said. "Temporary custody and the recorded risk to his standing."

Milo added the ordinary costs. Two missed shifts. A supervisor who now knew he had been held overnight for a family emergency nobody could explain. Counsel fees the Hall benefit might no longer cover because surety was struck retroactively.

"Who pays those?" June asked.

Constance said the Trust would reimburse documented custody expenses as ordinary settlement, no deed required.

Milo answered, "I will submit receipts. I do not accept reimbursement as waiver."

Pratt wrote the reservation.

Marta stood next.

She removed the business-agent pin from inside her coat and placed it beside the minute book. The pin had no magic. The title spoken at election did.

Local Nine's charter survived because June declined total invalidation. The Hall's bylaws required the business agent who had enforced the defective clauses to surrender office after they were struck.

"Did Marta act in bad faith?" June asked.

The Hall answered no through its designated witnesses.

"Did good faith preserve office after unlawful enforcement?"

No.

Marta had carried three hundred eleven memberships honestly and still administered rules that harmed June and Milo. Sincerity explained the choice. It did not keep the title.

"Marta Havlik loses the business agency," Yolanda read.

"Consequences," June said.

Yolanda listed them. No control of the business calendar, benefit fund, housing roster, charter negotiations, or official vote agenda. Marta retained membership, dues, personal vote, benefits, and kinship. She lost one title, not her body or line.

"Successor?"

"Emergency election after the next full cycle. I keep records as secretary until then but gain no agency."

The Hall would operate without quietly stacking Marta's authority on Yolanda.

Marta confirmed it.

"Do you accept removal?" Delisle asked.

"I accept that the bylaws execute it. I believe preserving the charter was still the correct decision."

Loss did not require ideological conversion.

Kenneth's price followed. His lone dissent, his assistance against the Hall's decision, and his continuing breach of presence required permanent exile.

Kenneth came from the rear wall carrying the sealed inventory June had witnessed at his garage. His workshop tools had already been removed from the Hall. Room key, benefit card, and voting token rested inside the envelope.

"State what exile removes," June said.

"Membership, vote, safe-room assignment, wage claims, burial benefit, workshop access, and standing to speak in Local business," Kenneth answered.

"What does it not remove?"

"Kinship. Ordinary property. My garage. The right to stand on a public sidewalk and tell Marta she's wrong."

Someone near the back laughed once, then stopped when no one punished it.

"I accept," Kenneth said.

"Why?"

"Because the acts are mine. I helped after the room decided not to. I would do it again."

"Do you ask pardon?"

"No."

"Do you ask the record to call exile voluntary?"

"No. I accept the consequence. I did not choose the rule."

The distinction prevented courage from prettifying punishment.

"No reinstatement?" June asked.

"None," Marta answered.

"Can a future Local admit him as a new member?"

"Permanent exile bars reinstatement under this charter. A successor institution could make its own rules."

Kenneth's future had a legal possibility and no promised rescue.

Elias's disqualification was read again. Every future office barred. No oath-bearing title available.

Elias had already left with Constance to inventory his keys. June refused to let absence turn his loss abstract. Delisle read his testimony election, the practical rights removed, and the explicit statement that June owed no answer.

"Price chosen by Elias Marrow," June said. "Not paid by me. Not consideration for the vow."

Both institutions entered the limit.

"These are not decorations beneath a favorable ruling," June said. "They are the cost of reaching it."

"Your price," Kearse said from the rival chair.

June faced him. "Permanent party standing. Six clauses remain capable of notice. No clean denial that the Articles reach me. The vow remains incomplete, and initiation remains possible only while it does."

Kearse called it power.

"Capability can be a price. I am now the person who has to keep the count."

Delisle entered June's consequence beside the others.

The joint record named four continuing prices: Marta, Kenneth, Elias, June. Milo's custody was entered separately as harm already borne, not a price victory asked him to pay. No institution appeared on the list as if an institution felt loss without people.

June required Delisle to read the list back. The first reading made Kenneth's exile sound like an administrative consequence and Elias's disqualification like an elegant legal theory. She stopped him.

"Use the verbs," she said. "Milo was held. Marta loses her office. Kenneth is exiled. Elias can never hold office. I remain subject to notice under six clauses."

Delisle read it that way.

The room shifted as each present-tense verb landed. This was why June distrusted nouns made from injuries. Custody. Removal. Disqualification. Standing. They let a listener admire the architecture without hearing the door lock.

"Again," Marta said.

Delisle read the five lines a second time. Nobody objected. Nobody applauded.

Constance named the Trust's price separately: three clauses gone, succession disrupted, office testimony public, rival appeal invited. Marta named the Hall's: agent removed, a member exiled, charter preserved under a permanent adverse party.

The room heard who paid inside each abstraction.

Marta removed the business-agent key from her ring and gave it to Yolanda. Then she carried the roster ledger to June.

"Custody authority?" June asked.

Marta read the bylaw allowing outgoing agents to transfer a disputed personal record to the person permanently described by it. Yolanda confirmed receipt would not grant June agency or membership.

"Then transfer only the ledger section concerning my line and the linked gap."

"The section cannot be removed without damaging binding."

"Whole book in physical custody, Local ownership retained, access limited to my claim and witnessed inspection."

Yolanda wrote those terms before June touched it.

"This is not the office," June said.

"No. The ledger is custodial property concerning your claim. The gap cannot remain with an outgoing agent who helped conceal it."

"Did you know who made it?"

"No."

"Do you know why?"

Marta's fear and kinship moved beneath the answer. "I know what the gap prevented. I do not know the full reason it was made."

"What did it prevent?"

"Your final exclusion."

The protected mystery advanced one fact and no farther.

"You made the gap."

"No."

The borne record confirmed the denial without revealing the protected hand.

Delisle asked Marta to repeat it for the written record. She did. Trust minutes would hold her spoken ignorance; the Hall carried its truth in the room.

Marta put the ledger into June's arms. It was heavier than its pages required.

June signed a human receipt for the book, then made Yolanda strike the phrase transferred ownership from the draft notation. Custody was not ownership. Possession was not office. The distinctions sounded fussy until someone used the wrong word for nineteen years.

"Inspection requests come through whom?" Yolanda asked.

"You, as secretary. In writing for the Trust. In person before witnesses for the Local. I attend either one. No page is photographed, copied, cut, restored, or resewn without notice to me."

"And if you refuse access?"

"Then either institution can ask Delisle to decide. Permanent party does not mean private owner."

Constance inclined her head. It was the nearest thing to agreement she offered.

The cover smelled of dust, coffee, and hands June shared blood with but had never met. Her line occupied less than one page. The blank beneath her mother's entry was narrow enough to look accidental unless someone knew what absence could preserve.

"What am I supposed to do with it?"

"Keep both versions. Do not repair a gap until you know what the absence protects."

"The official minutes and my dissent?"

"Yes. The roster entry and the unadjudicated blank. The altered parish term and sealed original. Every mismatch that kept someone from making the record clean at your expense."

June had spent her career producing one authoritative transcript. Here, integrity required preserving disagreement.

"Will you tell me?"

"Not yet."

Marta put on her coat. Nobody stopped her. She was still a member, still June's aunt, no longer the person who could speak business for the Local.

"Where will you sit next meeting?" June asked.

"Wherever a chair isn't broken."

"Will you vote?"

"Yes. I expect to disagree with you often."

Their relationship survived office without resolving into affection on demand.

At the door she looked back once.

"The gap is yours to keep now," she said.

She walked out of the Hall for the last time as its agent.
