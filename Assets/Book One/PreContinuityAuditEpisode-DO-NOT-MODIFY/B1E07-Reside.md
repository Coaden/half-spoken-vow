# Episode Seven: Reside

Episode Seven: Reside

The email came in at eight forty-one in the morning while she was setting up in a hotel conference room in Green Tree, and she read it standing, with her audio backup in one hand and the deponent already taking his seat.

*Notice of resumption. Clause the first. The bride shall reside beneath the roof of the Trust within thirty days of naming.*

Nine words that mattered, out of four hundred.

She had two sessions that day and she could not cancel either. She had already worked out what the fee was and what the fee did — nine hundred to the landlord, four hundred and ten to the car so it could pass inspection, and whatever was left toward the thing she had stopped letting herself count.

So she put the phone face down and swore the witness at eight fifty-eight and did her job.

She did it badly.

Not catastrophically. Nobody would have seen it except somebody who knew what she was normally like, and unfortunately the person on the other side of the table at eleven o'clock was Sondra Pell.

"You missed a strike-that," Sondra said in the corridor, without preamble, holding two coffees and offering neither.

"I got it on the backup."

"You got it on the *backup*." Sondra handed her one of the coffees after all. "June. I've been doing this nine years and you're the reason I stopped padding my rates, because you're better than me and I had to get better. You do not miss a strike-that."

"It's been a week."

"I'm aware it's been a week, everybody's aware it's been a week, Braden at Pratt's office is telling people you're a *party* to something." Sondra's face did something complicated. "I'm not asking. I want that on the record, whatever the record is. I'm not asking, and I'm not taking your work, and if you tell me to go away I'll go away."

"Don't go away."

"Fine. Then drink that and go and fix whatever it is, because you've got the afternoon free and I can take your two o'clock."

The county law library on the fourth floor of the City-County Building had six patrons in it and five of them were asleep.

June signed in, and asked the librarian for anything on the construction of nineteenth-century private agreements in this state, and was handed, without comment, a reprint of a treatise published in eighteen sixty-eight.

Then she sat down and did the only thing she had ever been good at.

She started where she always started, which was with what the sentence actually said rather than what everybody had agreed it said.

*Reside.*

The clause said *reside beneath the roof.* Everybody in that deposition room on Monday had heard it and everybody had heard the same thing, which was: move in. Give up the apartment. Sleep in a house owned by a trust in Sewickley with a door she did not control.

But that was what it *meant* now. In two thousand and twenty-six, to a woman with a lease.

It was not necessarily what it had meant to a man drafting in eighteen seventy-one.

She read for three hours.

It was the first three hours since Monday morning that she had felt like herself. That was the strange part and she noticed it while it was happening. Somewhere out there was a house in Sewickley and a red circle on a calendar and a man on a bridge she could compel, and none of it could reach her in a room with a treatise open on a desk, because in here she was not a named party. She was just a woman who was extremely good at finding out what a sentence actually said.

She read about domicile and she read about settlement and she read a forty-page argument about a mill hand from Braddock, and at ten past four in the afternoon, in a footnote to a chapter on poor relief, she found a man from a hundred and sixty years ago explaining, patiently, to somebody long dead, that a person was held to *reside* in a parish where he had lodged a stated number of nights within the year, and that continuous habitation was not required, and had never been required, and that the confusion arose because people used the word loosely in conversation.

June read it twice.

Then she read it out loud, quietly, to an empty room, because she had spent twelve years learning that you do not know whether a sentence holds until you have heard it.

It held.

She checked it three more ways, because a thing that looks this good at four in the afternoon has a habit of falling apart by six. She found the same rule stated in two later editions. She found it applied in a decision about a canal worker in eighteen eighty-one. She found nothing anywhere that said the meaning had been abandoned, only that people had stopped using the word carefully, which is not the same thing and never has been.

She wrote the argument in ninety minutes. She had never drafted anything in her life. But she had transcribed several thousand of them, and it turned out that if you sit behind a machine for twelve years and take down every argument anybody makes, you end up with an ear for the shape of one, the way a person who has copied out music can eventually hear a wrong chord.

Statement of the clause. The word at issue. The period authority. The proposition. The offer.

She wrote it in block capitals first, on the legal pad, the way she wrote case captions. Then she typed it out clean on the library computer and printed it for eleven cents a page and paid at the desk in dimes.

*The named party proposes to satisfy Clause the First by lodging a stated number of nights beneath the roof of the Trust, such nights to be recorded, and gives notice that she does not thereby surrender or abandon her present domicile.*

She read the whole thing aloud twice, standing up, in the stacks, and a man two aisles over woke up and left.

Pratt received it at nine the next morning with his sleeves already rolled.

He read it in front of her, which she had not expected and found unbearable, and he read it slowly, and at one point he took his glasses off and put them back on.

When he got to the end he set it down and put both hands flat on either side of it, like a man steadying a tray.

"Where did you find it," he said.

"Footnote. Chapter on poor relief."

"Nobody reads the chapter on poor relief."

"I read the chapter on poor relief."

"Miss Havlik." Pratt's voice had gone slightly odd. "I want to be clear that I am counsel to the other party and that nothing I am about to say is advice. But that is a good filing. That is a genuinely good filing. It is not a trick and it is not a technicality, it is simply *correct*, and the reason it will work is that the man who drafted this clause in eighteen seventy-one also read the chapter on poor relief, because in eighteen seventy-one everybody did."

"You could have argued it the other way," June said. "If you'd wanted to."

"I could have argued it the other way for about a week," said Pratt. "And then somebody would have gone and read the chapter on poor relief."

"Would they, though."

Pratt looked at her over the top of his glasses.

"No," he admitted. "Almost certainly not."

"So you'll accept it."

"I will accept it, and I will file it, and I will note the date." He was already reaching for the stamp. "You keep your lease. You keep your door. You will owe the Trust a stated number of nights of record and you will owe them precisely nothing else."

June sat down in the chair she had not been offered.

She had won. That was the thing she could not quite get a hand around. Nine days of everything being removed, and she had taken a single word out of a sentence and taken it apart and won something back, and nobody had helped her do it.

"How many nights," she said.

"That is for the parties. I will propose twelve a quarter and they will counter at twenty and you will settle at sixteen, which is the number I would have advised you to want." The stamp came down. "There. It is a record."

"Sixteen nights a quarter."

"Sixty-four a year. Which is a great deal of your life, and it is also not your whole life, and the difference between those two things is what you bought yesterday afternoon in a library for eleven cents a page."

"Thank you."

"Do not thank me, I have done my job badly on purpose exactly once and today was not it." He blotted the page. "One thing, though, and then I will let you go."

"There's always one thing."

"The counted nights begin tonight."

June stopped halfway out of the chair.

"Tonight."

"At seven. A car, not a summons. You may decline the car and take a bus, and I would, if I were you, purely to establish that you can."

"The clause resumes at nine in the morning and the Trust will not let a day of it run unsatisfied, so the first night of record is tonight, and a car will be sent." Pratt squared the page onto the stack. "Which brings me to the thing that is actually bothering me, and I would rather say it than not."

"Say it."

"I telephoned the house in Sewickley an hour ago to warn them you were coming." He looked up. "They were not surprised."

"Because you'd warned them."

"Because they had made your room up on Thursday," said Pratt. "Which was five days before you found the footnote, and four days before you had any reason to believe you would be keeping your lease."

The clock on the wall behind him went over the hour with an audible click.

"Somebody down there," Pratt said, "expected you to win this."
