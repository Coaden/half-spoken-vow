# Episode Three: No Standing

Episode Three: No Standing

The Hall on Cotter Street smelled like coffee and floor wax and, faintly, under everything, like a building where several hundred people had been eating together for ninety years.

June had been inside it exactly twice in her life, both times for funerals.

"Sit down, sweetheart, you're letting the heat out." The woman behind the counter had a cardigan the color of oatmeal and reading glasses pushed into grey hair, and she was already cutting into something on a tray. "Nut roll. Don't argue with me, it's Tuesday."

"I'm here about a form."

"You can be here about a form with a plate in front of you."

So June sat down at a folding table in an empty hall with two hundred stacked chairs behind her and a plate of nut roll she had not asked for, and said, "I want to renounce."

Yolanda Bright stopped cutting.

"Renounce," she said.

"Whatever the process is. There's a document with my name in it and I'm told I'm named under it by blood, so there must be a way to give the blood back. I'll sign whatever it is. I'd like it witnessed."

"Honey." Yolanda set the knife down. "Let me go get Bernice's binder."

She was gone four minutes. June counted them, because there was a clock, and because counting was what she did instead of the other thing.

On the wall opposite there was a calendar. Not a phone, not a screen. A paper calendar, the kind that came free from a supply company, hung on a nail, and somebody had gone through it with a red marker and circled three days in a row every month, all the way to December.

June knew what the circles meant. She had known since she was sixteen, when the warnings her mother had taught her four years earlier became a locked bedroom, a ruined door, and three days she could not remember in order. Rose had taught her to clear the glass, anchor the door, leave water, and distrust anything said after her hands stopped being hands. June had taught herself the other fifteen years.

She had never seen the dates posted where other people could admit to them.

Yolanda came back without a binder.

"There isn't a form," she said.

"There's always a form."

"There's a form for a member in good standing who wants out. There's a form for a transfer, and a form for a withdrawal, and a form for reinstatement after arrears." Yolanda sat down across from her, and she did it slowly, the way a heavy woman sits when she is buying herself a second. "You can't renounce a thing you were never entered into."

The hall was very quiet.

"I'm in the book," June said. "My mother was in the book."

"Let's look."

The book was a bound ledger the size of a paving stone, and it lived in a metal cabinet, and Yolanda carried it to the table with both arms and opened it flat.

The door banged and Milo came through it with his father's jacket on and his phone already out.

"I looked you up," he said, before hello. "On the members' app. It's doing the thing where it doesn't load, so I —"

"Sit down," said Yolanda.

"It's not doing the thing where it doesn't load, is it."

"Sit down, Milo."

He sat. He kept the phone in his hand and he kept looking at it, and then at June, and then at it, and his face was doing something June did not like, because Milo's face had never once in his life been able to keep a thing off it.

"That's stupid, though," he said. "You're a Havlik. You're standing right there. I can —" He stopped.

"Say it," June said.

"I can *tell*." He gestured at her with the phone, helpless, twenty-four years old. "Everybody can tell. You walked in and I knew before I turned around. So how come the app doesn't."

They went through the ledger by hand, out loud, because that is how the Hall had always done it and because the app had never once been right about anything. Yolanda read the surnames and June listened, and when they got to the H's Yolanda's finger stopped and did not move.

"Havlik, Kenneth Andrew. Havlik, Marta Louise. Havlik, Milo Andrew." A pause. "Havlik, Rose Marie."

"That's my mother."

"That's your mother." Yolanda turned the ledger a little so June could see. There was a line ruled through the entry in blue ballpoint, and beside it a date nineteen years old, and beside that a single word in a small hard hand.

*Closed.*

"And me," June said.

"Baby, there is no you."

June looked at the page for a while. It was a strange thing, being erased in ballpoint by somebody with tidy handwriting. She had expected — she did not know what she had expected. Something with more machinery in it.

"Then I have no standing," she said.

"You have no membership. You have no vote, no benefits, no dues, no burial. You've never paid in, so there's nothing to pay out." Yolanda closed the ledger gently. "And you can't renounce, because there's no entry to close. It's already closed. Somebody closed it for you before you could walk."

"Then what am I."

"You're Rose's girl." Yolanda said it flatly, without kindness, which was the kindest possible way to say it. "Every soul in this building knows exactly what you are, June. Nobody needs the book for that. I knew it when you came through the door. I'd have known it in a parking lot."

"Then how does that work," June said. "Everyone in the building knows, and the book says no, and the book wins."

"The book always wins."

"Why?"

"Because the book is what we hand to *them*." Yolanda's chin went toward the window, toward the river, toward downtown. "When their people come and ask who's ours, we don't take them round the room and let them smell everybody. We hand them the book. That's the whole arrangement. That's been the arrangement since eighteen seventy-one."

Milo said, quietly, "That's not fair."

"No," said Yolanda. "It's just what it is."

June sat with her hands flat on the table.

"Then the book is wrong," she said.

"The book is a book."

The door at the back banged and a big man came in out of the weather, shaking rain off a canvas jacket, and stopped when he saw her.

Kenneth Boyle was sixty-some and built like something you would use to move a piano, and he had been at both funerals. He looked at June, and then at the ledger on the table, and then at Yolanda, and his face did a whole conversation in about two seconds.

"She asked to renounce," Yolanda said.

Kenneth pulled out a chair and sat down on it backwards, and it complained.

"You don't know anything," he said to June. Not unkindly. As a finding of fact.

"I'm learning fast."

"You're not, though. You're learning the paper." He nodded at the ledger. "Ask me what the calendar buys."

June looked at the wall. Three days circled in red, every month, all the way to December. The nearest ring began on the third.

"What does the calendar buy."

"Thirty days," Kenneth said. "Then three nights. Third, fourth, fifth. Same nights for everybody in this book."

"I know what the dates do."

Kenneth looked at her properly then. The room changed with it. Milo stopped moving his phone from hand to hand.

"How long?" Kenneth asked.

"Since I was sixteen."

"Who sits outside?"

"Nobody."

"Who opens the door?"

"I do, when I can use it again."

Kenneth's big hand closed on the back of the chair. June could smell something from him she had spent years calling concern because she had not known there were more exact words available.

"I clear the small room," she said. "I take out the glass. I put an anchor through the door and leave water. I block three days from work. It has held."

"A lock you set from inside isn't containment," Kenneth said. "It's hope with hardware on it."

June thought about her apartment. Third floor, one door, the anchor she had installed herself in her second year because the landlord would not repair the frame.

"Now here's the part that matters," Kenneth said. "The Hall's got a housing roster. Nine rooms in the basement, doors that lock from outside, and a list of who's in which one. There's a fund that covers your wages for the three shifts you lose, because you're going to lose them, every month, forever. There's a man on the second floor whose whole job is calling employers and lying. There's a burial fund because sometimes it goes wrong."

He leaned forward.

"You're not on the roster. You're not on the fund. Nobody's going to call your agency. And in thirty days you're going to use the same room because nobody ever told you there was another way."

The radiator came on under the window and knocked twice.

"That's what the book being wrong costs," Kenneth said. "It doesn't cost you a vote. It cost you fifteen years without a room."

Milo had gone the color of the tablecloth. "She can have mine."

"No," said Yolanda.

"It's *mine*, I'm a member in good standing, I paid —"

"You can't give it," Kenneth said. "It's not a thing you own, son, it's a thing you're on. Rooms go by the roster. You take yourself off, the room doesn't follow you, it goes to the next name." He looked at June. "That's not him being useless. That's how it works."

"I wasn't going to take it," June said.

"I know you weren't."

June found that she had picked up the fork at some point and was holding it like a pen.

"Who closed her line," she said.

Yolanda got up and went to the door and looked out into the corridor, both ways, and shut it.

Then she came back and sat down and put both hands flat on the ledger.

"June," she said quietly, "before you go any further with this, there is something you need to understand about that book. It's got a gap in it. And I'm the one who's been keeping the gap. Nineteen years, I've been keeping it."
