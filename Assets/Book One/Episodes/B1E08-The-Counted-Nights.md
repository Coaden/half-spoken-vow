# Episode Eight: The Counted Nights

Episode Eight: The Counted Nights

She took the bus.

It took an hour and ten minutes and two transfers and she had to walk the last half mile up a road with no sidewalk, in the dark, with an overnight bag, past hedges that had been somebody's whole career.

The house was not a mansion. That was the first thing that surprised her. It was long and low and old and extremely well kept, with deep porches on two sides and lamps already burning at the door, and the gravel had been raked that afternoon, and every window on the west side had its drapes drawn.

The woman waiting on the step had a ring of keys and a cardigan and a face that told her absolutely nothing.

"Miss Havlik. I'm Nadia Kovach. I keep the house." She took the bag before June could stop her. "You walked from Beaver Road."

"I took the bus."

"I know. Mister Pratt telephoned about the car at four and I said you wouldn't take it." Nadia turned and went inside. "Come on. Your feet will be wet."

The hall smelled like wax and cut flowers, and there were flowers, actual ones, in a bowl on a table, in the middle of the week, in a house where nobody appeared to live.

"Rules," said Nadia, going up the stairs. "There aren't many and I'd rather say them now than have you find them by accident."

"Go ahead."

"Ground floor, you have the hall, the front room, the dining room and the kitchen. Kitchen's yours whenever, day or night, and there's food in it, and I'd take it as a personal insult if you went hungry in this house." Landing. Left. "First floor, your room and the bathroom next to it. That's all of the first floor."

"What's the rest of the first floor?"

"Not yours." Nadia opened a door. "This one is."

It was a good room. That was the second surprise, and it was worse than the first. Fresh linen, a lamp lit, a chair by the window, a jug of water and a glass on the nightstand, and somebody had put a small stack of towels on the end of the bed with a folded blanket on top of them.

"Third rule," said Nadia. "This door doesn't lock."

June looked at the door.

There was a keyhole. There was a handle. There was no bolt, and there was no key in the keyhole, and when she looked back Nadia was watching her with that same unreadable face and waiting, patiently, to see what she would do with it.

"Is that for me or for him?"

"That's the first sensible question anybody's asked me in nine years." Nadia set the bag down. "It's for the record. If a door in this house locks, then somebody can say afterward that something happened behind it that couldn't be seen. Nothing in this house happens behind a locked door. That's not a rule about you, it's a rule about the house, and it's older than anyone in it."

"Does his lock?"

"No."

June put her coat on the chair.

"Miss Kovach."

"Nadia."

"Nadia. Whose flowers are those, downstairs?"

Nadia stopped in the doorway.

"Mine," she said. "I do them Mondays. Nobody's asked me that before either." A pause. "My grandmother kept this house, and then my mother, and now me. Ninety-one years between the three of us. I know where every squeak in the floor is and which window sticks in August, and I have never once been asked who does the flowers."

How many named women have you received in this house?” June asked.

Nadia had already started down the stairs.

“Four,” she said, and kept going.

June came down at eleven because she could not sleep, and found the kitchen light on and Elias Marrow sitting at the table in shirtsleeves with a ledger open in front of him and a fountain pen he was not using.

They looked at each other.

"I can go back up," she said.

"It is your kitchen. That was stated to you as a rule."

"It was stated to me as a rule at nine, by a woman who then told me the door doesn't lock, so I'm still working out how the rules run around here."

"They run exactly as she said." He capped the pen. "Nadia does not soften things. It is her single greatest quality and it makes her very difficult to have in a house."

June got a glass down and filled it at the tap and sat at the far end of the table, six feet, the distance she had settled on.

The pull was worse indoors. She had suspected it would be — fewer walls between them, or lower ceilings, or something about the still air. It sat under her sternum like a hand on a doorframe.

She thought about mentioning it and decided not to, and then realized he almost certainly had it too, and worse, since he had been in the building since sundown.

"You're working," she said.

"I am reading. There is a difference and I have been failing to observe it for about two hours." He turned the ledger a quarter turn, which she noticed was toward her, not away. "It is the schedule of my own filings. Everything I have read into a record since nineteen hundred and four. I go back through it when I cannot do anything useful."

"Why?"

"Because I remember all of it," Elias said, "and I would like, occasionally, to find that I am wrong about one."

The refrigerator came on. Somewhere upstairs a radiator ticked.

"Ask me the thing," he said.

"What thing?"

"You have been holding a question since you arrived. You have got it as far as your mouth twice." He put the pen down. "I will tell you now that I can only answer it if it is a question and not an order, so you may as well ask it while we are both still enjoying ourselves."

June turned the glass around on the table.

"The room was made up on Thursday," she said. "Pratt told me. Five days before I found the argument. So somebody in this house knew I'd win, and I want to know if it was you."

Elias was quiet for a moment.

"No," he said.

"Then who?"

"Nadia."

"She knew I'd find the argument?"

"She knew a named party had received mature notice. The house rule is older than I am: when notice matures, a room is prepared whether the person is expected to come or not. Nadia expected the obligation. She did not predict your argument."

"You answered fast."

"I answered honestly. Those are frequently the same speed and people distrust it." He turned the pen over once. "If you would like to test it, you know precisely how, and I would rather you did than sat up all night wondering."

"I'm not doing that again."

"You said that on the bridge."

"I meant it on the bridge."

"I know you did," said Elias. "I am telling you that I would find it a great deal easier to be trusted than to be believed."

"Did you expect me to come?"

"No. I did not know you would find it, I did not expect you to keep your lease, and when Pratt telephoned yesterday morning to tell me what you had filed, I had to sit down."

"You had to sit down."

"I had to sit down," he agreed, "and Nadia saw me do it, and she has not mentioned it once, which is how I know she intends to mention it eventually."

June laughed.

It got out before she could stop it, one short unguarded sound in a kitchen at eleven at night in a house where she had elected to begin performing early, and she saw it land on him — saw him go still with it, the way a man goes still when something unexpected happens near him that he does not want to frighten off.

"Don't," she said.

"I said nothing."

"You did a thing with your face."

"I have been reliably informed," said Elias Marrow, "that I do not."

"Last thing," June said, at the door. "Then I'll go up."

"Ask."

"Three nights." She had the glass in her hand and she had not drunk any of it. "And after the third?"

"Nadia enters the nights in the house record. A trustee reads the disposition before the accepted witnesses. Pratt files it. Clause One is satisfied."

"Even while it's suspended?"

"Suspension stops execution. It does not forbid you to perform an act you chose after notice, and it does not authorize anyone to enlarge that act into custody."

He did not look away from her, which she had come to understand was the thing he did instead of softening an answer.

"Three nights," he said. "Then you leave with your lease and your own door."

She woke at three.

Not gradually. All at once, flat on her back in a strange bed with the lamp off and the house completely still, and she lay there for four or five seconds trying to work out what had done it, because something had.

Then she heard it again.

A voice. Downstairs, or along the passage — somewhere on the wrong side of the wall, low and level and unhurried.

Reading.

Not talking. Not a television and not a telephone. June had spent twelve years listening to people read documents aloud into records and she knew the cadence the way a musician knows a time signature: the small pause at a clause break, the flattening at a proper noun, the drop at the end of a numbered item and the lift going into the next one.

Somebody in this house was reading a document out loud, slowly, at three in the morning, to nobody.

She got up.

The floor was cold. She went to her door — the door that did not lock — and put her hand on it and opened it two inches, and the voice came up the passage more clearly, and it was not Elias.

It was a woman, and she was three items into something, and June stood in the dark in a borrowed room and heard her say, quite distinctly:

"Item the fourth. Havlik."
