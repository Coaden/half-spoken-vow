# Episode Thirty-Five: Nine Days

Episode Thirty-Five: Nine Days

The man who sold June his safe-room slot arrived with his eldest child carrying a Hall-issued first-change kit and asked for the key back.

They stood outside June's building in Polish Hill. The child was sixteen and shivering inside a borrowed coat. A paper bracelet from the Hall clinic circled her wrist, and somebody had written *room six* across the first-change packet in red pencil.

June smelled fear and kinship between them. The clinic had confirmed that the girl's first change would come with the public three-night window beginning in nine days. Her father kept himself between her and the street as if dread were something he could screen with his body.

"Did the Hall nurse examine her?" June asked.

"This morning. First change."

"Does the attendant know?"

"Yes. They put the first-change supplies in six before the roster locked."

Room six. The number June had bought because it sat at the end of the corridor with only one shared wall.

"The calendar says nine days," June said.

"The room was assigned to her before I sold you the key. I thought I could put her in overflow. The roster locked last night, and overflow is full."

The man held out six hundred and fifty dollars in an envelope.

"I don't want the money," June said.

"I want the receipt back."

"Keep the receipt. It proves why the slot changed hands."

"Then the transfer stays in the record."

"Good. It should."

"You may need it in nine days."

June looked at the girl rather than the envelope. "She needs it before I do."

The father smelled ashamed of his relief. June refused to soothe him. Relief did not make him a thief, and gratitude would not make her safer.

She took the brass key marked six from her bag and put it in the girl's palm.

"The first door sticks," June said. "Pull before you turn. The water valve is inside on the right. If you can speak when they ask about the light, answer. If you can't, silence means they leave it as the plan says."

The girl nodded, concentrating as if instructions were a railing.

"Room six. The attendant checks twice. Tell Yolanda I returned it before use."

The girl managed, "Thank you."

"Go."

Her father pushed the envelope at June again. She closed his hand around it.

"Buy the coat you owe whoever lent that one."

They left for Cotter Street. June watched until their car turned the corner.

Nine days. No room. No rent money returned, because accepting repayment would turn the girl's emergency into a debt June could collect later.

June called Yolanda before going upstairs. On speaker, she stated that she returned room six voluntarily, before use, without repayment, assignment, or claim against the child. The father witnessed. Yolanda read it back and transferred the key.

June then told Yolanda to attach the receipt and return declaration to June's counted-night safety file for one narrow purpose: room six was no longer available as containment during the approaching moon window. The filing did not make the sale valid, accept Hall membership, or change June's Clause One residence. Yolanda read those limits back before recording the notice.

"Do you want overflow priority restored?" Yolanda asked.

"Can you guarantee a room?"

"No."

"Then note no replacement was promised."

June ended the call with less than she had bought and a cleaner record of why.

Kenneth met her in the basement that evening. The landlord used it for broken appliances and paint cans. A coal room at the rear had one door, no window, and masonry on three sides.

June had measured it twice. Seven feet by nine. A hot-water tank occupied one corner. Electrical conduit ran low along the south wall. Above it, apartment two kept a crib against the floorboards; June had heard its wheels move.

Her plan placed the anchor farthest from that sound.

"No," Kenneth said.

"You haven't seen the plan."

"Door opens inward. Hinges are residential. Pipe along that wall will come loose."

"I can replace the hinges."

"Floor joists above you. People sleep upstairs."

June laid out chain, two padlocks, a padded restraint, and the receipt for hardware.

"I need you to witness that I tested them."

"I will witness that they won't hold."

"Then say what would."

"A safe room."

"Productive."

"Reinforced masonry on all sides. Outward-opening steel door with two latches. Nothing you can pull loose and use. Drainage. Somebody outside who knows when not to enter."

"I have nine days."

"You have a lease that forbids structural work and neighbors above you."

"I can get permission."

"By explaining what?"

June had drafted three human explanations: panic room, storage security, home-recording booth. None justified a floor drain or a door meant to withstand impact from within.

Kenneth picked up the chain. "This is rated for towing, not impact. The anchor is set in old mortar. You don't know what your body will do."

"Neither do you."

"I know what mine does. That is more than nothing."

He tested the wall bolt once. Mortar powdered onto the floor.

Kenneth pulled harder. The bolt came free with a soft crunch and brought a fist-sized piece of mortar with it. The chain dropped around his boot.

"That was half my strength."

"You're larger than I am."

"Before change."

June picked up the bolt. Dust filled the threads. Its twelve-hundred-pound rating meant nothing without a wall capable of holding it.

"Could we brace across both walls?"

"Then you load the joists."

"Steel cage."

"Becomes loose steel if one weld fails."

Every answer turned equipment into debris.

The basement plan became visibly what it had always been: panic arranged as hardware.

The outer door opened. Elias stopped at the bottom stair, beyond the line June had marked in chalk.

"Kenneth Boyle admitted me to the common basement," he said. "Not to your dwelling."

"Why are you here?"

"Yolanda notified the Trust that you returned the room."

"That entry was bodily safety business. Why did it reach you?"

"Because you directed Yolanda to attach the return to your counted-night safety file, and a material containment condition changed."

"Room six wasn't the residence, and my notice said so."

"It was the containment address associated with it."

June made Kenneth start recording. "Did the notice create authority to intervene?"

"No. It gave the Trust knowledge that a filed safety condition failed. I came to make an offer."

"Fast system when it wants to be."

Elias looked at the failed anchor. "The Sewickley house has a stone service court enclosed on four sides, with no occupied rooms adjoining."

"Trust property."

"Yes."

"Trust staff."

"Nadia and one attendant you select. I will remain outside the inner perimeter."

"How far?"

"The opposite wing until one hour before dawn. Then below ground or off the property."

"If I move toward you?"

"The inner gate stays closed. I move farther away if that will not draw you toward occupied rooms."

"If the pull makes me injure myself against it?"

Elias did not disguise the cost. "Then the attendant follows the plan you wrote. The attachment does not authorize me to enter."

Kenneth said, "It would hold."

June ignored him. "I won't be able to consent once the change starts."

"Consent given before incapacity governs only the safety plan you name. Any act outside it remains prohibited. Any refusal before or during the change ends an optional act immediately."

"I may not be able to speak."

"Then silence refuses anything not already specified."

"And the attachment?"

"A symptom. It authorizes nothing."

Elias set a proposed protocol on the floor between them and stepped back. June made Kenneth pick it up.

It named doors, distances, witnesses, water, the absence of restraints unless June requested them while capable, and Elias's departure time before direct sun.

June sat on the basement step and edited aloud. No blood stored within the court. No trustee inside its walls during the change. Nadia could enter only after announcing herself and receiving an intelligible yes, unless a listed emergency occurred: fire, uncontrolled bleeding, collapse lasting longer than the written interval. Not distress. Not screaming. Not movement toward the gate.

Kenneth added that any restraint had to be fixed before onset and released according to plan. Elias added that June could revoke an optional restraint while capable and nobody could restore it afterward.

"Cameras?" June asked.

"None. An audio recorder outside the court if you request it."

"Continuous. File sealed to me."

"Agreed."

"Drafted. Nothing is accepted until I inspect the place."

"Correct."

"Why offer?"

"Because you need a place."

"What does the Trust get?"

"A living named party. That interest exists whether I help or not."

"What do you get?"

His answer came without performance. "The knowledge that you survive."

Kenneth's breathing changed. He could smell whether Elias meant it. June could smell Kenneth recognizing that he did.

"That isn't nothing," June said.

"No. It is also not a deed you perform."

"Will you claim this as evidence I accepted Trust protection?"

"The record will say you accepted use of a specific court under a specific protocol. Constance may argue consequence. She may not alter the act."

"Will she offer me something afterward?"

"Almost certainly."

"Do you know what?"

"No."

Kenneth said, "He's telling the truth as far as he knows it."

Elias accepted the translation without offense.

June read the protocol again. The house was the one thing that would work. It also required her to enter a trustee jurisdiction on the one night she would be unable to consent to anything new.

"I need enforceable boundaries," she said. "Not your word."

"Inspection tomorrow," June said. "Daylight hours, with Kenneth. Nadia admits us. You stay below ground. We test every gate, confirm who holds every key, and read the final protocol into both records before I enter on the first night."

"Yes."

"Cyd gets a copy."

"If she accepts it."

"If she doesn't, she still gets told where I am in ordinary human language."

Kenneth handed June the failed wall bolt. "Keep this with the protocol."

"Why?"

"So nobody later says the basement would've been fine."

June wrapped it in the hardware receipt. The rejected plan deserved a record too. Asking for help was easier when the alternative could no longer masquerade as self-reliance.

"Then write them," Elias said.
