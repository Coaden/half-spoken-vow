# Episode Forty-Three: Six and Four

Episode Forty-Three: Six and Four

At the first grave, Delisle named the bride, the clause, and the sentence he had witnessed before he let June ask him for anything.

"Lenora Renn. Clause two. I certified that her surety's acceptance had been witnessed."

Wind moved dead grass against the stone. Tess had not come. Delisle carried no parish copy, only a folded list with six names.

He had required June to leave her camera in the car. The graves were evidence, he said, but they were also graves. She could record names and dates aloud. She could not turn stones into exhibits before deciding what question she meant to ask.

The cemetery road curved below them. Beyond bare trees, the city continued with delivery trucks and school traffic. Four women had died inside the Articles without becoming visible to anybody driving past.

"You buried four," June said.

"I witnessed six. Four are here."

"Why not all six?"

"One lived. One disappeared from every record my office can inspect."

"Released or unaccounted for?"

"The first was formally released. The second has no certified ending."

June knew the categories from Nadia's ledger. Hearing them beside stone changed their weight. Release meant a record said the instrument stopped. Unaccounted meant an institution stopped being able to prove where a woman went.

They walked to the second grave. Delisle gave another name, another clause, another statement he had accepted into the record. At each stone he said what he had done before describing what anybody else did.

At the second, he had certified residence after a bride entered a Trust house under protest. She survived that clause and died two clauses later when an executing deed placed her outside shelter before dawn. Delisle had not ordered the deed. His earlier certification kept the sequence moving toward it.

At the third, he had witnessed a surety accept before the consequences were read separately. The Hall called the person's fear proof of sincerity. The Trust called the signature proof of understanding. Delisle accepted both. The bride died attempting to prevent performance against the surety.

"Did she choose that?" June asked.

"She chose the act that killed her. She did not choose the structure that made it appear necessary."

At the fourth, he had witnessed the first half of a vow and later refused to certify the second because the bride was ill. The Articles executed forfeiture anyway. She died in ordinary custody from an ordinary infection nobody treated early enough.

No monster entered any account. Procedure had been sufficient.

"Why keep witnessing?" June asked.

"Because a witness does not decide the terms. He makes sure the people inside them are heard."

"Were they?"

"Not well enough."

"That isn't an answer."

"They were heard accurately at the moment of speech. I treated that as enough. I did not ask whether the room had arranged every consequence so only one answer remained survivable."

"A pressured answer can still be voluntary."

"Yes. And pressure can become compulsion before the witness admits it."

"Where is the line?"

"That is the question I kept believing the next case would teach me."

Six cases later, four stones stood in place of an answer.

At the fourth grave he stopped reading from the list.

He knelt to clear dead grass from the date. His black coat darkened at the knee.

"I performed the burial service here," he said. "The family asked me not to mention the Articles. They told human relatives she died after a long illness. It was true enough to survive ordinary questions."

"Did you object?"

"No. I believed privacy belonged to the dead. I also benefited from nobody asking what I had witnessed."

Delisle did not use confession to ask forgiveness. He put each fact where June could decide what it meant.

"I told myself absence would be worse. That if I refused, both institutions would find somebody obedient and careless. I was correct about the risk and wrong about what correctness excused."

"Were you careful?"

"More careful each time."

"Did it save them?"

"Two. Perhaps."

"Perhaps isn't four graves."

"No."

"If you had refused after the first?"

"Another witness might have certified worse acts. Or the absence of a witness might have stopped the sequence. I cannot know which."

"Then how do you know refusal is right now?"

"I do not yet. That is why I brought you before answering."

June looked across four stones. "Clause three requires a witnessed vow."

"Yes."

"If the deadline pressures me, completion would be invalid."

"If you are compelled, exposed and compellable, unaware of permanence, or under a clause executing against you, yes."

"Clause three is executing against me now."

"The term is active. Whether that alone makes completion void depends on whether your speech answers the attachment or avoids forfeiture."

"Who decides?"

"The witness first. A tribunal later, if challenged."

"Then you could certify a permanent vow and let people litigate my motive afterward."

"Yes."

"There is no afterward for the bond."

Delisle looked toward the four stones. "No."

"Then refuse to witness mine while clause three is active."

"If I refuse only while the clause runs, my refusal can become another pressure. You may believe you must defeat the clause before you are allowed to choose him."

"That is still better than being forced to choose him now."

"Better is not neutral."

"You already admitted neutrality was a story you told yourself."

The words landed harder than June intended. Delisle accepted them without defense.

Delisle folded the list. "You are asking me to remove one coercive route."

"I'm asking you to protect consent."

"And after clause three is defeated?"

"Then I decide again."

"Would you ask me to witness if you freely chose completion?"

"After lawful defeat? After Milo is safe? After nobody's office depends on it?"

"Under whatever conditions you would call free."

June pictured Elias across six feet of rug. Direction under her ribs. His desire stated without request. Her own desire becoming public evidence. She could imagine saying the words. She could not yet imagine living every permanent fact after them.

June could not answer without making a decision the cemetery had no right to extract.

"I don't know."

"That is an honest answer."

"It is not consent to lose the option."

"No."

"Then answer the request I made, not the one your conscience wants. Refuse during clause three. Preserve my later choice."

"A witness is not equipment held ready for a future speaker."

"Your office exists to be ready."

"My office does. My conscience does not transfer with it."

Delisle walked to the two names not buried there. One bride had been released. One remained unaccounted for. He had witnessed both beginnings and neither ending.

The released bride's name appeared on the list beside a parish filing number and the word *alive*. Delisle had verified her survival years afterward but had not witnessed the act that freed her. The unaccounted bride's line ended with an open date.

"Which one made you keep serving?" June asked.

"The living one."

"And which made you bring me here?"

"All the others."

"I believed my office made me neutral," he said. "It made me present. Those are not the same."

"Presence still matters."

"Yes. That is what makes refusal cost something. I do not get to pretend leaving the chair empty is innocence."

"Then don't leave it empty forever."

"If I reserve the right to return, every pressured person after you will be asked to perform well enough to satisfy me. My conscience becomes a tenth clause."

"Will you refuse this vow?"

"Yes."

Relief arrived first.

Then Delisle continued.

"I refuse every future vow under the Articles. I will witness none again, for anyone, under any condition."

"Your office accepted a duty."

"The office must present and preserve. It cannot compel me to certify consent I no longer trust myself to judge."

"The institutions can remove you."

"Yes."

"And appoint someone worse."

"Yes."

He had not solved the risk he once used to justify staying. He had stopped treating the risk as authority over his conscience.

June stared at him. "I asked for one refusal during the clause."

"You asked me to act from my own conscience. I have."

"You are the only accepted witness."

"I know."

"Then a free completion becomes impossible too."

"Yes."

"Take the permanent part back."

"No."

He spoke his refusal into the parish recorder and named the six prior vows, the four burials, and the office he would no longer perform. June heard the institutional door close with every word.

Then he played it back. June listened for a qualification: during the active term, absent new safeguards, unless ordered. There was none.

"Do you accept my witnessing of your refusal?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Do you understand I object to its permanent scope?"

"Yes."

"Enter that too."

Delisle did. Her objection preserved disagreement. It could not restore the chair.

The cemetery bell marked the hour from beyond the trees. Nine days had not paused while Delisle found his conscience.

"Will you still serve as custodial holder?" June asked.

"Until removed. I will present clauses, preserve objections, and certify speech that does not require me to judge completion."

"Will you testify that no accepted witness exists?"

"Yes."

"Will you support emergency relief from forfeiture?"

"I will testify to the altered term and my refusal. Support is a legal conclusion I will leave to the tribunal."

Even now, he would not become June's instrument merely because his choice had harmed her strategy.

They walked back past the four graves. Delisle named each woman once more, not as argument but as custody. June repeated the names so the recorder would carry two voices. At the gate she stopped the file and wrote the time remaining until midnight on the ninth day.

She had tried to protect one choice from pressure.

Delisle returned the recorder to his pocket. "I will not witness again, ever, for anyone."

June realized she had talked her only exit out of existence.
