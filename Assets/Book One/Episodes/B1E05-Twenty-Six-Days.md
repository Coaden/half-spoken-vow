# Episode Five: Twenty-Six Days

Episode Five: Twenty-Six Days

She picked the bridge because it belonged to nobody.

That was Kenneth's word for it, offered in a garage over a carburetor when she had asked where you meet somebody you do not trust. *A span belongs to neither bank.* You stand out in the middle of it where everyone can see you, in person, in front of God and the traffic, and nothing said there can be pretended away afterward.

June had thought that was folklore until she got about a third of the way across.

It started around the second stanchion. Low, behind the sternum, physical and entirely her own. Not a flutter. Nothing that romantic. It was closer to the feeling of a wire tightening somewhere under the floor of a building — a tension arriving through structure rather than through skin, and it got worse with every step, and it was pointing.

She stopped and turned in a slow circle in the rain to check, because she was a person who checked.

Facing downriver: less. Facing the far bank: more. Facing the dark shape standing halfway along the rail sixty feet ahead of her: considerably more.

"Right," she said, to nobody. "Fantastic."

He did not turn around when she came up. He had his hands on the rail and he was looking at the water, and there was rain on the shoulders of his coat and none of it seemed to be bothering him.

"You're early," he said.

"You're earlier." She stopped six feet away and made herself stay there, which took a specific and unpleasant effort, like holding a compass needle off north. "I want to name something first, because I've spent four days trying to work out whether I'm ill."

"Go on."

"There's a pull. It gets stronger the closer I am to you. It started Monday morning at about ten forty-one and it has not stopped since, and I have not slept properly in four nights, and I want to know if that's real or if I'm coming apart."

"It is real."

"Say more than that."

"It is called an attachment." He said the word carefully, the way you hand somebody something sharp. "It is not rare and it is not a disease. It is mutual, so I have it as badly as you do. It is observable — a third party standing close enough can tell it exists, which is why I am not standing closer. And it is involuntary. Neither of us did it and neither of us can stop it."

The rain came sideways off the water.

"Does it make me do anything," June said.

"No."

"Does it make me *want* anything."

"It makes you aware of me." He turned around then, and leaned back against the rail, and folded his arms. "That is the whole of it, Miss Havlik. It is not a leash and it is not an instruction. It confers nothing. It entitles nobody to anything. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something."

"How long does it last."

"I do not know."

"That's not —"

"I am not being evasive. I have been a trustee for a hundred and twenty years and I have seen four of these and no two behaved the same way." He said it evenly. "One faded inside a season. One did not fade at all. The registers record that an attachment existed. They do not record what it felt like, or how long, or what became of the people. That is not an oversight. Nobody has ever thought that part was the important part."

"Somebody should have."

"Yes," said Elias. "Somebody should have."

"But it's evidence."

He went still.

"That's what it is, isn't it," she said. "It's on a register somewhere. It's a fact about me that other people can read."

"Yes."

"And it went on the register on Monday."

"Both registers," he said. "Within the hour."

She let out a breath. It came out unsteady and she was annoyed about that.

"Fine," she said. "Second thing. Abandonment. You said there's a way to take it off the table and it's available to the speaker at any time. So take it off the table."

"No."

"You said ask you the price when I was sitting down. There is nowhere to sit on a bridge, so I'm asking standing up."

He looked at her for a long moment.

"Formal abandonment is excommunication," he said. "My half would end. My exposure would end. My name would be struck from every trustee office, every succession, every institutional protection, and every supernatural claim I might enforce. Permanently. I would keep my civil identity and property held in my own name. The attachment would remain."

He told her without any drama at all, in the same voice he had used to state his name for the record, and when he had finished June found that her hand had closed on the wet rail hard enough to hurt.

For a moment she could not do anything with her face.

She had come out here with a plan, and the plan had been: make him undo it, go home, get on with the eleven days until Marta produced the copy. It had been a clean plan. It had survived about ninety seconds of contact with the actual terms.

"That's not a price," she said. "That's a punishment."

"It is a price. It is simply not a small one."

"And you'd pay it."

"I would have paid it on Monday afternoon," Elias said, "if you had asked me then, and I would have done it in front of Pratt so that it was properly recorded. I did not offer, because you had not asked, and because a man who offers that in the first hour is a man performing something."

"And now?"

"Now I would like you to tell me to."

June stared at him.

"Then why won't you?"

"Because you have not told me to," he said. "You have asked me to. Those are different, and you of all people know they are different, and I am not going to do you the discourtesy of pretending I did not notice."

And there it was.

She stood in the rain on a bridge that belonged to nobody, and she understood, with the clarity that came to her about once a year and never at a convenient time, that she had been outmaneuvered by a man being scrupulously honest with her.

Because there was a way to make him. She had heard it in a stairwell four days ago, in Marta's flat tired voice, and she had been carrying it around ever since like a stone in a coat pocket.

*Anybody with standing can make you do a thing you would not do, and you will do it, and you will be able to watch yourself doing it.*

She thought about the ledger with a blue line through her mother's name. She thought about a basement with nine rooms in it and no room with her name on the door.

She thought: *I have exactly one piece of leverage in the world and it is standing six feet away in the rain telling me the truth about itself.*

"I have standing," June said.

He did not answer.

"Don't I. Named party to the instrument. That's standing."

"That is standing."

"And you're exposed."

"I am."

Her heart was going. She noted it, distantly, professionally, the way she noted everything.

"Then I want to know why you did it," she said. "And I'm not asking you. Mister Marrow, I'm telling you. Say why you spoke on Monday."

It was fast.

That was the thing she was not ready for. She had expected a struggle, a pause, some visible seam. Instead it went through him like current through wire, and his mouth opened before his face had finished changing, and the answer came out of him in his own careful voice with nothing careful left in it at all.

"Because I read clause one that morning."

Silence.

"And?" she said.

"That is the answer to the question you asked."

She let go.

She did not know how she let go, exactly — there was no lever — but something in her that had been leaning forward simply stopped, and he came off the rail and turned away from her and stood facing the water with one hand braced on the metal, and for about four seconds neither of them said anything at all.

"I'm sorry," June said.

"Do not."

"I am, though."

"You are entitled to do it," he said, to the river. "That is what it means. It is not a discourtesy, it is a *fact*, and I told you it was true because I would rather you learned it from me than from Kearse, or from a woman named Odell, or from your aunt in a stairwell." He straightened his cuff. His hands were not quite steady, which she suspected he knew. "Now you know what it does. I would ask that you remember what it looked like."

"How bad was it."

"You do not want that answered."

"I asked."

"You asked," he agreed, "so I will tell you, and then we will not discuss it again. It is not pain. It would be simpler if it were pain." He was still looking at the water. "It is the experience of watching your own mouth make a decision that you were not consulted about, and knowing, the entire time, precisely which words you would have chosen instead."

"I'm never doing that again."

"You will do it again," Elias said. "Not this week. Possibly not this year. But there will be a night when the thing you need is on the other side of my mouth and there will be no other way to get it, and you will do it, and you will be right to." He turned around. "I would simply prefer that you do it knowing."

The rain had gotten into her collar. Twenty-six days until clause one resumed. Twenty-seven until the first red date on the Hall calendar.

"That wasn't the whole answer," she said. "Was it."

"It was a complete and truthful answer to the question you put."

"That is not what I asked you."

Elias Marrow looked at her across six feet of wet decking with the city burning on both banks behind him, and when he spoke he sounded, for the first time since Monday, like a man rather than an office.

"Then do not ask me a question you would rather have the answer to," he said. "There is a second half to that sentence, Miss Havlik. It is yours, and nobody can say it but you, and I will not ask you for it."

"And if I said it."

"Then I would have to tell you the rest."
