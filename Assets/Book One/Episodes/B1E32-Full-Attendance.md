# Episode Thirty-Two: Full Attendance

Episode Thirty-Two: Full Attendance

Marta called the first name on the roll before June had finished distributing her argument.

"Wait," June said.

Three hundred eleven chairs faced the center table. Every one was occupied. Folding legs crowded the wooden floor, and the coffee urn worked against the heat of that many bodies.

Members filled the aisles where the floor plan allowed no chairs. Work boots, hospital shoes, kitchen clogs, one pressed suit beside three reflective jackets. The moon took the same three nights from all of them and charged different prices in wages, marriages, and joints that never healed quite straight.

June's packets moved hand to hand without becoming authoritative. Paper could inform this room. It could not prove her position. Proof stood breathing in front of proof and accepted being known.

Marta held the membership book open. "The meeting began at seven."

"Then give me ten minutes."

"You have five. The moon roster locks at eight."

"The motion can wait ten minutes."

"The roster cannot. Forty-eight room assignments and seventeen transport pairs depend on tonight's vote. If the charter challenge opens, I cannot certify benefits under a structure this meeting has refused to honor."

Marta's answer converted June's extra five minutes into rides, locks, and places bodies would be contained when the three-night window began ten days from now. The roster closed early because employers, drivers, attendants, and forty-eight locked rooms could not be rearranged at moonrise. She had priced the time where everyone could smell June deciding whether to spend theirs.

"Five," June said.

June passed the last copies down the row. She had no vote and no right to address the meeting. Marta had granted five minutes because June was the question being decided.

"The motion is whether Local Nine continues to honor the Articles," Marta said. "If the motion fails, the Trust may petition to void our charter. Benefits, safe rooms, wage support, and burial obligations become unsecured."

Yolanda read the motion from the minute book. Continue did not mean approve every construction. Honor did not mean complete the vow. A yes authorized the Local to perform its obligations while litigating individual clauses. A no repudiated the common instrument and accepted the charter petition that could follow.

June made Marta read one more consequence before June spent her five minutes. Under the Hall bylaws, a business agent who administered a clause later lawfully defeated for violating a member's or kinswoman's bodily authority lost the agency when the defeat entered. Good faith did not preserve the title.

"Does that consequence apply to you?" June asked.

"If a clause I administer is defeated on that ground," Marta said, "yes."

She did not use the risk to ask for sympathy or postpone the vote.

June made her define *may*.

"The Trust has reserved the right to petition," Yolanda said. "It has not promised to do so."

"Has Constance threatened it?"

"No new threat was delivered. The reservation is in the guarantee."

That distinction mattered. June would not win honestly by making Constance crueller than the record showed.

June stood where every member could smell her fear and her kinship.

"A charter exists to protect bodies the ordinary world can't house," she said. "You have three hundred eleven of them in this room. You also have one body you refused to enter on the roster, and the only reason the Articles can reach me is that you kept me outside the protections you now say require my sacrifice."

Nobody looked away. Members could not hide a position by studying paper. They heard her in presence, exactly as their rules required.

"Clause one took my threshold. Clause two took my cousin as surety. The next clause will demand a witnessed vow I remain free to refuse. If you honor every consequence around that refusal, you are using the charter to do the thing the charter exists to prevent."

She held up the Hall's entry from the cutter election.

"Yesterday this Local recorded that no lack of membership diminishes bodily authority to refuse. Tonight you are deciding whether my refusal can coexist with punishment applied to everybody near me. If Milo's standing answers because I say no, then you haven't overridden the word. You've made the price of saying it someone I love."

Fear changed in the room. Not toward agreement. Toward recognition.

"You know what coerced agreement smells like," June said. "You built open roll call because a position should not hide. So do not hide this choice inside the word necessary. Say what yes means. The charter stays funded. Rooms stay open. And you perform the clauses against Milo and me because preserving those things costs less than refusing."

Marta said, "Thirty seconds."

"Then thirty seconds of accuracy."

Marta said, "Time."

"I have one minute."

"Use it."

"A charter preserved by handing over an uninitiated kinswoman has already failed its purpose. Vote against the Articles. Make the Trust argue in public that three hundred eleven people lose shelter because one woman would not be spent."

"And if the Trust does?" Marta asked.

June had used her time. Marta could have denied an answer. She waited.

"Then we learn whether this is peace or leverage," June said.

The room heard that she did not know what happened after the learning.

June sat.

Marta stood. "Three hundred eleven members. Forty-eight safe rooms. One hundred and six wage claims this year. Nine burials. The Trust owns six buildings used by the Local and holds guarantees on the benefit fund. If the charter falls, none of those obligations vanishes. Only the structure paying them does."

She named the next full moon and the number of people assigned rooms. She named the members whose employers had already refused leave. She named nine families who had received burial money.

Then she named failure without ornament. The Hall could keep the rooms one month on reserves. The wage fund could meet sixty-two of the one hundred and six approved claims. Six leases would remain legally valid while their subsidies failed, leaving the Local owing rent it could not cover.

"The Trust does not own our bodies," Marta said. "It owns notes, buildings, and guarantees we accepted because predictable incapacitation costs money. Those ordinary debts remain enforceable if we repudiate every supernatural word tonight."

June saw members look toward the roster board where dates and room numbers were chalked. Their fear was not superstition. It was who would lock which door before sunset.

"June is my niece," Marta said. "Everyone here knows that without paper. She was denied initiation. That was done before this meeting and cannot be repaired by pretending it did not happen. The question tonight is whether three hundred eleven memberships survive the choice that failed her."

Her argument was not better. It was heavier.

June stood. "Point of accuracy. My choice did not fail me. The Local did."

Marta's face tightened. Around them, kinship carried the injury cleanly.

"The minutes will reflect the correction," Marta said.

It was the only point June won.

Yolanda brought the minute book to the table. "Open roll call. Yes means continue honoring the Articles. No means challenge the charter consequence."

The first member said yes.

Then the second.

Yolanda called each name at the same volume. No applause followed an answer. Nobody explained a vote during the roll, because explanation let powerful people turn presence into persuasion one body at a time. Yes or no. The reasons remained physically obvious and procedurally irrelevant.

At twelve votes, June stopped looking at faces and listened to shoes shift against wood. At twenty-three, somebody opened the urn and coffee hissed onto the burner. At thirty-nine, Marta reminded the room that abstention counted as presence but not decision. Nobody abstained.

June's argument had landed. That was the worst part. Members looked at her with grief, anger, even agreement, and still said yes. She could smell no lie and hear no misunderstanding. They believed the charter was failing her. They also believed losing it would fail more people by Friday.

Names moved through the room. Each answer was spoken aloud. Fear, shame, anger, and relief traveled beneath them, but no position could be hidden inside a secret ballot.

June counted until counting became punishment. Fifty yes. Eighty. One hundred. Nobody said no.

Yolanda reached the safe-room stewards. Each said yes. Then members with no current room, people whose wages had never been reimbursed, people who had paid dues for years without filing a claim. Yes did not divide cleanly between selfishness and sacrifice.

June searched for procedural leverage. The motion had been noticed. Quorum was complete. The charter consequence had been read aloud. Marta had disclosed kinship. Every voter heard June directly. Nothing in the room let her turn losing into invalidity.

At two hundred, she stopped counting the total and counted the spaces between answers. None lasted long enough to become refusal.

Milo said yes with surety already sitting inside his standing.

Yolanda asked him to confirm because his office could be affected. Milo said yes again. His fear sharpened; his answer did not.

June wanted to tell him he had voted for the instrument capable of reaching his person. She had already told him. He understood. Respecting his agency felt too much like abandoning him, which did not make it less necessary.

He looked at June afterward. She did not make his choice easier by forgiving it from across the room.

The roll continued. Two hundred sixty. Two hundred ninety. Marta's name came near the end. She said yes without pausing and without pretending it cost less because she chaired the meeting.

Yolanda recorded her own yes, then turned the page.

Three hundred eight answers had been given. Every one was yes. The motion had passed long ago, but the remaining names still mattered because an open roll was a record of bodies, not a shortcut to a result.

June folded her argument once along the staple. The pages had done all they could. She had forced the room to name the bargain, separated Constance's reserved remedy from a threat, and corrected Marta's account of who had failed whom. None of it changed the outcome. Accuracy was not victory. It was the condition that made a real loss impossible to dismiss as misunderstanding.

Yolanda called the next name. Yes.

The next. Yes.

Then she reached the final line.

Yolanda called Kenneth Boyle.

He sat near the back in his welding jacket, hands flat on his knees. The Hall became silent before he answered because everyone smelled his decision.

Kenneth said, "No."

One word, alone, into a silent hall.
