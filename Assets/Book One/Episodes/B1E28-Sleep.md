# Episode Twenty-Eight: Sleep

Episode Twenty-Eight: Sleep

On her fourth sleepless night, June dropped her stenotype case on the Sewickley stairs, and Elias stopped one step below without touching it or her.

The case struck wood and opened. Paper, cables, and the small backup recorder scattered between them.

June reached for the recorder and missed it by three inches. Her hand had begun to shake on the bus from downtown. She had hidden it by carrying the case with both hands. Now the evidence lay across a trustee's staircase.

The house seemed to lean in time with her pulse. Elias remained below her, near enough that the directional ache under her sternum eased and far enough that every stair between them stayed visible.

Elias held the banister. "May I pick those up?"

"The equipment. Not me."

"Understood."

He gathered each piece while June sat on the stair and waited for the house to stop tilting. The attachment pulled harder at this distance. It had become a constant awareness of where he stood, aggravated by exhaustion until proximity felt like thirst.

Nadia appeared at the landing. "Counted night began at eleven. I entered it."

"Thank you," June said.

"You look terrible."

"Also entered?"

"I maintain standards."

Nadia took the case. Elias remained one step below.

"Do you require medical attention?" he asked.

"I require unconsciousness. Preferably the ordinary kind."

"Have you eaten?"

"That is a question."

"Yes. I withdraw it."

June closed her eyes until the banister stopped moving. He did not replace the question with an order. The absence of management felt almost as strange as the pull.

"There is a sitting room off the kitchen," he said. "Two doors. No lock. You may use it alone."

"If you leave, the pull gets worse."

He waited.

June disliked having to say the next part. "Stay in the room. Across from me. No touching. No questions I haven't agreed to answer. Either of us can leave without explanation."

"Yes. I add one condition. If you fall asleep, I do not move closer."

"Agreed."

"If you become ill," Elias said, "I wake you by speaking from where I am. If speech fails, I call Nadia before approaching."

"If there's a fire, you can approach."

"Only far enough to wake you?"

"Far enough to get me out. Emergency permission ends outside."

"Understood."

June almost told Nadia the exception did not need witnessing. Then she remembered how many disasters began with someone deciding an exception was obvious.

Nadia repeated the boundaries from the landing, not as magic, but as a witness who refused ambiguity. Then she carried the case away.

The sitting room held two chairs and a cold fireplace. June chose the chair nearest the door. Elias sat opposite with six feet of rug between them.

Nadia set June's water within reach and a second glass beside Elias. She removed the carafe so neither would need to cross the rug. Before leaving, she read the boundaries back in the order spoken.

"Correct," June said.

"Correct," Elias said.

The latch stayed open when the door settled. June could see a strip of corridor light and hear Nadia's shoes recede across the tile.

For several minutes neither spoke.

The relief was physical. The pull did not vanish, but it stopped dragging at the edge of her attention. June's shoulders lowered without permission.

The skin over her ribs had hurt for days as if she had been bracing for impact. Now the ache loosened one muscle at a time. Elias's breathing changed too, the controlled intervals becoming merely quiet.

"Is this proximity or trust?" June asked.

"Proximity."

"You sound certain."

"My body improved before you agreed to trust me."

"Useful distinction."

"Necessary."

If relief were proof of safety, every locked door would become consent the moment fear eased behind it. June let comfort be a symptom and nothing more.

"Does it do this to you?" she asked.

"Yes."

"How much sleep?"

"Perhaps an hour each day."

"You said trustees don't need much."

"We need more than that."

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"You did not ask."

"That answer is becoming a personality defect."

"It has been one for some time."

"Did anyone tell you?"

"Constance documented it in nineteen ninety-eight."

June opened her eyes. "As a performance concern?"

"As an impediment to efficient stewardship."

"I would like to see that document."

"It contains information about other officeholders."

"Of course it does."

"I can provide the sentence in a certified excerpt."

She laughed, then pressed a hand to her temple because even that hurt. The corner of his mouth changed just enough to show he had intended the effect.

The laugh cost her less than expected.

From another room came Nadia's voice reading the counted-night entry. A second steward answered the date and June's presence. Written record met spoken verification, then the house went quiet.

"One true thing," June said. "Volunteered. No compulsion."

"Rules. It can't be a fact already in a filing. It can't be something either institution is entitled to demand. Either person can decline without a substitute."

"Agreed. May the listener ask one question?"

"One. The speaker may decline that too."

"Agreed."

They had built a procedure for honesty because neither trusted honesty without exits. June found that more reassuring than spontaneity.

"You first."

"That's not volunteering. That's negotiation."

"I am out of practice."

June looked at his scarred right hand, resting open on the chair arm. "I declined the cut because the price belonged to Cyd. Not because I chose you."

"I know."

"You didn't know until I said it."

"No. Thank you for correcting me."

He gave no visible injury for her to manage.

"My question," Elias said. "If Vera had named a deed belonging only to you, would you have accepted?"

June watched the dark seam between two floorboards. "I don't know."

"That is an answer."

"It's not a useful one."

"It prevents me from inventing a different one."

She had expected either hope or relief. His refusal to manufacture either made the room quieter.

"Your turn."

Elias looked toward the dark window. "When Kearse compelled me, the answer about your ordinary life was true before he asked. I had already identified it as the thing I was most afraid the Articles would take from you."

June's allowed question became six possible questions and then one. "Why?"

"Because it is the place where your skill has value without anyone first assigning you standing."

The answer struck closer than desire would have. Her work was invoices, wrist pain, hostile counsel, and other people's words preserved cleanly. Elias had understood that losing it would force her further into a world where every useful act required somebody else's permission.

June asked him to describe the compulsion without making her absolve the bargain that enabled it. He did. His mouth had remained his body and ceased to be his decision. He had heard each answer before he spoke it and been unable to substitute a safer word.

"I did that to you on the bridge," she said.

"Yes."

"You warned me I might do it again."

"You have not."

"I sold the chance to somebody else."

"Yes."

"Your turn to ask one question."

He considered. "Why did you make Kearse promise not to use what you supplied against my body or office?"

"Because I was angry at you, not entitled to you."

Elias's fingers tightened once against the chair arm.

"Is that helpful?" June asked.

"Yes."

"Does it change what happened?"

"No."

The symmetry was imperfect and therefore honest.

No comfort arrived from him. Neither did punishment.

"I am sorry," June said.

"I believe you."

"Does that help?"

"It changes what happens next. It does not change what happened."

That was the first apology she had made in days that nobody tried to convert into permission.

Elias told her Kearse had asked whether he would accept the cost of completion. June's spine went rigid.

"What did you say?"

"Yes."

"That answer is about your cost."

"Entirely."

"It does not create a request to me."

"No."

She made him repeat it, not because she doubted the grammar, but because hearing a boundary twice made the room safer.

"That isn't why you spoke the vow."

"No."

"Still protected."

"Still mine to tell."

June let that boundary stand.

"Did you know I gave Kearse your schedule?"

"When he served me."

"Do you hate me?"

"No."

"Are you angry?"

"Yes."

"Those can both be true."

"Frequently."

She closed her eyes. The chair was not comfortable, but comfort had become less important than a room where nobody demanded a decision from her body.

"If I fall asleep, don't read anything over me."

"I will not."

"Don't answer the phone in here."

"Agreed."

"If I say your name, wake me before you assume I meant you."

"Yes."

June folded her coat beneath her head. Elias asked permission before lowering the lamp. She allowed it halfway, enough that his outline remained distinct from the chair.

"One more boundary," she said.

"Yes."

"If the attachment does anything while I'm asleep, that isn't an answer to either of us."

"Agreed."

"Say the whole thing."

"No symptom you experience while asleep supplies consent, invitation, or information you chose to give me."

Only then did June close her eyes.

June slept for forty minutes. She woke because the house clock struck two, not because Elias had moved. He remained exactly where he had been, eyes closed, hands visible.

The first thing she knew was his direction. The second was the clock. The third was that her jaw had unclenched.

She sat up too quickly. Elias opened his eyes but stayed still.

"Did I say anything?"

"You said no once. I did not answer."

"Anything else?"

"No."

"Did the pull change?"

"It eased when you slept and sharpened when you woke. I assign no meaning to that."

June drank water. "You're learning."

"Slowly."

"Were you asleep?"

"Briefly."

The knowledge that her presence had given him the same small relief was more intimate than touch would have been. June stood before the thought could ask anything of her.

At the door, Elias said, "Constance is going to spend me."

June turned.

"On what?"

"I do not know. She has reserved my exposure for a use she considers necessary. When she makes it, she will offer you something that looks like mercy."

"What should I do?"

"Read the price."

"What do you want in return for the warning?"

"Nothing," he said. "That is the one thing I can still offer freely."
