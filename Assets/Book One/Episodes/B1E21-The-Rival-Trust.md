# Episode Twenty-One: The Rival Trust

Episode Twenty-One: The Rival Trust

Elias reached Kearse's house knowing the invitation was a compulsion and found every door left ceremonially open for him.

The front door stood wide to the November night. The library doors had been fixed back with brass hooks. Even the cabinet holding the recorder was open, its red light visible from the hall.

Hospitality arranged as humiliation.

June sat beside Pratt with Kearse's concession in her hand. She had not known what her fact purchased until Kearse thanked her for delivering him. Elias saw that in her before she spoke.

"I didn't authorize a compulsion."

"You authorized nothing beyond information," Kearse said. "My trust had already filed a rival petition for one recorded examination of his exposed office. Your interval let me serve it. The petition created the claim; your bargain did not."

"And after tonight?"

"Its access to his exposed word remains until the petition is disposed. Any new injury still requires its own command and consequence."

"It does neither."

Elias said, "Miss Havlik, you should leave."

Kearse turned to him. "Remain where you are."

The command took hold between one step and the next. Elias's body stopped. His intention continued forward without it, an absence shaped like motion.

June rose. "Stop."

"You have no authority over my use of standing in my own office," Kearse said. "You may stay, or Mr. Pratt will escort you home."

"I will stay."

"Then witness what you bought."

Kearse approached Elias with no haste. "Give me the clause file."

Elias's right hand opened the case. He selected the file and placed it on the table. He would have chosen instead to throw it into the grate, although there was no fire.

"Remove your gloves."

He did.

Kearse told him to place his burned hand palm-down on the table. The old mill scar touched polished wood. Nothing in the command harmed him. That was the demonstration: standing did not require urgency, justice, or a useful purpose. It required only a lawful party and a named act within reach.

"Lift it."

Elias lifted his hand.

"State whether either command was necessary."

"No."

"Did you perform them voluntarily?"

"No."

"Did your body resist?"

"My intention resisted. My body performed."

June said, "Enough."

"Not yet," Kearse answered. "You used this once and believed revulsion taught you the whole mechanism. It did not."

"I know what I did."

Elias looked at her. "You know what it looked like."

The distinction cost him to offer because it also gave Kearse permission to continue. June heard it and stayed.

Kearse commanded Elias to state the answer he would have chosen to the glove instruction. He said he would have declined and asked the purpose. The forced act and the unavailable response entered the recording together.

"That is the part official compulsion records omit," Elias said. "They preserve performance and call the matter complete."

June glanced at his bare hand. "Like a satisfied clause."

"Yes."

"State your exposure."

"I spoke the first half of an incomplete vow before an accepted witness. Until it is answered or formally abandoned, a party with standing may compel my performance."

"Do you wish to answer me?"

"No."

"Will you?"

"Yes."

Kearse's charm did not fail. That was what made him more dangerous than a man who enjoyed pain. He performed each indignity as a necessary demonstration to people he hoped would learn.

"Sit."

Elias sat.

June had gone white around the mouth. He wanted her to look away. He wanted her to remember the bridge, when she had compelled him once and understood enough to release him. He wanted several things unavailable to him.

Kearse took the opposite chair. "The Marrow Trust is selling property."

"Yes."

Kearse required Elias to name the first sale. An office building with no lineage function. The second housed a daylight archive. The third carried a lease guaranteeing two Local Nine safe rooms.

"Would Constance sell the third?"

"Only if failure left no alternative."

"What does failure mean?"

"Inability to meet human debts as they mature."

"Not extinction."

"No."

June heard the practical scale. The Trust's supernatural crisis would arrive as rent, payroll, insurance, and buildings sold one by one. Institutions died through ordinary bills before anyone named death.

"Does completion erase those debts?" she asked.

"No. It gives permanent standing from which to restructure. It does not manufacture money."

Kearse looked mildly disappointed that the answer diminished the mythology of what he hoped to acquire.

"Then why pursue the Trust?" June asked.

"Standing compounds," Kearse said. "Property merely pays for patience."

Elias answered before compulsion reached him. "And another house's obligations become leverage over everyone named in them."

Kearse acknowledged the unwelcome correction with a small, perfectly courteous bow.

"Its human successors are refusing appointment."

"Yes."

"It requires June Havlik's completed attachment to continue beyond this century."

"Constance believes so."

"Do you?"

Elias tried silence. His lungs waited. His mouth opened.

"I believe no institution is entitled to a person's permanent choice merely because it failed to prepare for its own ending."

June looked at him then.

Kearse leaned back. "That answer would cost you office."

"You did not ask whether I would file it."

"No. I prefer knowing where the unfiled belief lives."

He poured tea for June, who did not touch it.

"You think I intend to court her," Kearse said. "I do not. I need her alive because a living adverse party can force a trust to litigate. I need your trust to fail because it owns obligations my house can administer better. Romance would complicate an otherwise elegant acquisition."

"How reassuring," June said.

"I promised honesty, not comfort."

Kearse asked Elias for the Marrow properties under review, the three rival petitions, the names of human successors. Elias answered only what the compulsion reached, but it reached every literal question.

Elias used precision where refusal was impossible. A property *under review* was not one merely discussed. A successor's name did not include an address. A petition's existence did not disclose its strategy. He narrowed every answer to the smallest truth the grammar required.

Kearse noticed.

"You think literal compliance protects the Trust."

"It limits your question to the question you asked."

"Then I will ask better ones."

He did. Which property sale would damage Local Nine's safe-room capacity. Which successor had access to June's lease. Which rival petition could attach to the half-vow before abandonment.

June interrupted with objections even though she had no authority over the interrogation. She made Kearse name relevance. She made Pratt note when a question touched her employment and invoked the concession each time.

Kearse withdrew two questions rather than create a written breach.

Elias could not stop the answers, but June could make some of them more expensive to ask. The alliance began in the same room as her betrayal, without forgiving it.

Between questions, Kearse required smaller acts. Move the recorder closer. Pour June's untouched tea into the waste bowl. Repeat a sentence after him and identify which words created obligation. Each command proved control while pretending to prepare the record.

"Why this theater?" June asked.

"Because you sold a vulnerable interval without understanding vulnerability. Understanding should be delivered before the next bargain."

"At his expense."

"All lessons here are."

Elias said, "Ask what you summoned me to answer."

"You are impatient."

"I am compelled, not improved."

Kearse smiled. "There you are."

He asked whether Elias could refuse June a direct command. Elias explained that her standing could compel him while exposed, but her refusal to use it remained absolute. Kearse asked whether completing the vow would protect him. It would end exposure and cost him every oath-bearing office forever.

"Would you accept that price?"

The question reached. "Yes."

June's chair moved against the floor.

Kearse lifted a hand. "I did not ask whether she should pay hers."

"You arranged the answer where she had to hear it."

"Sound-native evidence, Miss Havlik. Your profession should approve."

Elias wanted to strike him. The compulsion did not require stillness, but violence against a host with standing would become another instrument aimed at June. He remained seated by choice, which was the only choice available worth preserving.

June crossed to the recorder and read Kearse's own concession into it. The promise protected her employment from information she had supplied. Kearse agreed that the coming answer could not lawfully be used against the agency by his trust.

"Your rivals are not bound by my promise," he said.

"Then name every party receiving the recording."

He named only himself and his counsel. June made him repeat it. She could not release Elias, but she could narrow the damage while witnessing it.

"Require your counsel to acknowledge," she said.

Kearse called the attorney into the doorway. The human counsel repeated the limits and agreed to hold no copy after the stated use. Pratt read the acknowledgment back.

"Now you have ordinary remedies if I breach," Kearse said.

"Now I have evidence that you knew the boundary."

Elias watched June turn the only available tool on the room. She could not overpower compulsion. She could build a record around it until every additional injury acquired an author.

Then Kearse said, "Stand and face Miss Havlik."

Elias obeyed.

"Don't," June said.

"That is a refusal directed to me," Kearse said. "I will honor it as to any act upon you. My question acts upon him."

"You know that's false."

"Morally, perhaps. Procedurally, no."

Elias faced her. The attachment drew tight. Her anger carried no scent he could read, but he knew its shape from the stillness of her hands.

Kearse turned on the recorder.

"Elias Marrow, name the one thing presently most capable of hurting June Havlik."

Elias held his jaw closed. The compulsion moved beneath muscle, patient and absolute. He experienced every word he would have chosen instead. Her mother. Milo. The approaching moon. Himself.

The command required one thing, not the worst truth in all time. Presently. Capable of hurting.

His mouth made the answer.

"Her ordinary life," he said. "Her work. Cyd Toomey's agency. The lease she cannot afford to lose. Make her trouble expensive to the people she loves, and she will surrender before she lets them keep paying."
