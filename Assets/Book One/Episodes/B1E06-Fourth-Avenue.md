# Episode Six: Fourth Avenue

Episode Six: Fourth Avenue

She booked it for two in the afternoon on purpose.

At two in the afternoon the only people in a trust office are the ones who can be sued, and June had had a week to think about that, and it had become the most useful thought she owned.

The Marrow Trust occupied one floor of an older building on Fourth Avenue, behind frosted glass with nothing on it but a number. The reception desk was empty and had the particular dust-free emptiness of a desk nobody sat at. Every interior door stood open. Every window on the sun side had heavy blinds pulled all the way down and taped at the edges, and the afternoon came around them in a thin white line that stopped a foot short of the carpet.

Aloysius Pratt came out of the third door along with his sleeves rolled and a look of open delight.

"Miss Havlik. Sit down, sit down. Nobody comes here."

"I'd have thought a trust had clients."

"A trust has instruments," said Pratt. "Instruments do not require chairs."

He put her in a conference room with a long table and no windows, and brought a jug of water she had not asked for, and sat down across from her and folded his hands, and June recognized the posture, because it was the posture of a man about to be asked about his life's work by somebody who had never asked.

She had thought about how to run this the whole way over on the bus.

"I'd like to be straight with you," she said. "I'm the named party and I'm trying to get the shape of the thing before something else happens to me. I'm not here to trap you and I'm not recording."

"You may record if you wish."

"I don't have standing to compel you, so I'd rather ask nicely."

"That," said Pratt, "is the first correct sentence anybody has said to me in this building in a year." He got up and went to the sideboard and came back with a folder. "You have no standing to compel me. I am not a party. I am counsel of record, which is a considerably smaller thing that people constantly mistake for a considerably larger one."

"Then why help me?"

"Because you asked about the instrument," he said, "rather than about the people. Everybody asks about the people."

He opened the folder.

"Nine clauses," Pratt said. "They run in sequence. That is the first thing, and it is the thing everyone gets wrong, so I will say it slowly. The sequence is fixed. Clause one, then two, then three, and so on, in that order, forever. It has never been reordered and cannot be."

"Naming isn't one of the nine."

"Correct. Naming made you a party with standing. It did not make residence due, and it did not speak your half of Mister Marrow's vow. His half interrupted the reading, suspended the first clause, and bound only him. Those are three separate entries. People blur them because they happened in the same minute. The instrument does not."

"Why fixed?"

"Because a sequence that can be reordered is a sequence that can be *aimed*," said Pratt. "Fix the order and nobody can reach past clause two to get at clause seven because it happens to suit them this year. It is, I think, the single most humane thing in the document, and it was put there deliberately by somebody who had thought about what he was building."

"That's an odd word for it."

"It is the correct word. Whoever drafted this was not a cruel person." He said it without emphasis. "That has been my professional opinion for twenty-two years and I have never once been able to persuade anybody of it."

"So I know what's coming."

"You know the *order*. You do not know the *timing*, and the timing is not disclosed to anybody, including me. A clause becomes due when it becomes due." He turned a page toward her. "Clause one, residence. Clause two, surety. Clause three, a term certain. Four is a question of issue. Five and six concern the disposition of property. Seven I will not summarize because you would ask me a follow-up and I would have to decline it. Eight and nine have never been reached."

June copied it down in block capitals, and then went back and numbered them, and then drew a line under three.

"You said four is a question of issue."

"I said that, yes."

"Issue means children."

"Issue means children," Pratt agreed, and did not elaborate, and poured himself some of the water she had not asked for.

"Never reached in a hundred and fifty-five years?"

"Never reached in seven brides," said Pratt.

She stopped writing.

"Tell me about the seven."

Pratt's hands went still on the folder, and for the first time since she had walked in he stopped being delighted.

"Four of them died," he said. "Under numbered clauses. It is recorded that way, which I have always found —" He stopped. "The word the instrument uses is *satisfied*. Clause four was satisfied. Clause two was satisfied. That is the entry. Two were released, which is a different entry. And one is unaccounted for, which is not an entry at all, it is simply an absence where an entry should be."

"Do you know their names?"

"I know all seven," said Pratt. "I could not tell you a single thing about any of them beyond a date, which I have always considered a failure of the filing system rather than of the women."

"Which clauses."

"That I genuinely cannot tell you, because the schedule is held in the sealed original and I have never been permitted to read it." He said it lightly and it did not land lightly. "Twenty-two years. I have drafted around that document, filed under it, defended it twice, and I have never once been shown the sealed text."

"Then who holds it?"

"The Trust holds the sealed original in a vault. Local Nine holds a certified copy, which is the one you have requested from your aunt, and I will tell you now that she will take eleven days." He almost smiled. "She always takes eleven days."

June wrote: *sealed original — Trust. certified copy — Local Nine.*

"That's two."

"Yes."

"Two copies, three parties. Somebody at that table on Monday didn't have one."

Pratt looked at her for a moment.

Then he got up, went to a card index on the sideboard — an actual card index, oak, the kind that had been ordered when the building was new — and pulled a drawer and walked his fingers back through it.

"There is a third holder listed," he said.

"Listed as who?"

"Listed as *holder*." He drew out a card and brought it to the table and set it in front of her, and June saw a line of typescript and, under it, a blank. "There is a place on this card for a name. In twenty-two years nobody has told me what goes in it, and I have asked four times."

"What did they say the four times?"

"The first time I was told it was not relevant to my work. The second time I was told it was a historical artefact and the holder was very likely long dead. The third time I was told that the card was probably a clerical error carried forward." He shut the drawer with great care. "The fourth time, which was in two thousand and nineteen, nobody said anything at all. Mrs. Marrow looked at me for a while and then asked after my mother."

"Is your mother well?"

"My mother has been dead since two thousand and eleven," said Pratt, "and Mrs. Marrow sent flowers."

"You've asked four times."

"Once every five or six years," said Pratt, "which is the interval at which a man can ask a question in an institution like this one without it becoming a personality."

June looked at the blank space on the card for a while.

"Mister Pratt," she said. "Can I ask you something that isn't about the instrument."

"You may certainly ask."

"Twenty-two years. Do they ever make you a trustee?"

The line of white light along the blinds had crept about an inch while they had been sitting there. Pratt put the card back in the drawer, and closed it, and did not turn around for slightly too long.

"That is not a thing I have been told either way," he said.

"One more," June said, at the door. "And this one you can decline."

"I shall enjoy that."

"When a clause is satisfied — when it's done, and the entry gets made — who writes the entry?"

"A trustee reads the disposition into a record before an accepted witness. Then I file it." He said it evenly. "I have filed four of them."

"Four."

"Four," said Pratt. "I have very good handwriting, Miss Havlik. It has occurred to me more than once that the last official act performed for four women was my handwriting."

She went down in the elevator with her notes on her knee and the whole nine-clause spine written out in block capitals, and stopped in the lobby to put her coat on, and Pratt came out of the stairwell door behind her, moving faster than she had seen him move.

"One housekeeping matter," he said. "It slipped my mind entirely and it is the only thing I have said today that has a date on it."

"Go on."

"A suspended clause resumes on the thirtieth day. Not thirty days from the reading — thirty days from the interruption." He was slightly out of breath. "Monday was the second. By my arithmetic you have twenty-three days, and clause one will resume at nine in the morning, whether or not anybody appears."

"Twenty-three."

"Twenty-three." He hesitated. "And Miss Havlik. Forgive me. I assume you have somewhere to be on the third."

June stopped with one arm in her coat.

"Why would you assume that?"

"Because everybody on your side of this always does," said Pratt, and looked immediately as though he wished he had let her get to the door.
