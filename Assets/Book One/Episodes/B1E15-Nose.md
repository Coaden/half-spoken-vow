# Episode Fifteen: Nose

Episode Fifteen: Nose

The client said the price was too high, and June smelled that he was afraid.

Not of the number. His fear sharpened whenever Cyd said turnaround and eased whenever Sondra said coverage. He sat across the lobby table with Cyd's bid open in front of him and kept one thumb pressed over the delivery schedule.

"We appreciate the proposal," he said. "But the monthly figure is outside what we budgeted."

Cyd drew breath for the answer they had rehearsed in her kitchen.

June spoke first.

"If the price matched your budget, would you sign today?"

The man's thumb stopped moving.

Sondra turned her head by one exact degree.

"Price is the issue," he said.

Fear strengthened.

They were in the lobby because the client's conference room had flooded overnight. Four chairs, a low table, and a receptionist close enough to hear every promise. Cyd had complained about it on the drive over. June now understood it as an advantage. Nobody could revise what was said here without doing it in front of witnesses.

"That wasn't my question," June said. "If we changed the price, would you sign today?"

"I'd have to review the service terms."

"Which term?"

Cyd set down her pen. "June."

The warning was light enough for the client to mistake it for teamwork.

He tapped the schedule. "Your guarantee has exceptions."

"Every guarantee does," Sondra said. "Ours names them."

"We had a late transcript last quarter."

There it was. Fear, then anger laid over it because anger was easier to negotiate in public.

June said, "Not from us."

"No. From our current vendor."

"How late?"

"Three days."

"What did it cost?"

He looked toward the receptionist. "A filing deadline."

Cyd leaned forward. "Okay. That's not price. That's redundancy. We can solve redundancy."

She was off at once, fast and practical. Two reporters held in reserve for multi-day matters. Same-day notice when a job threatened to run long. Sondra named the assignments she could cover. June laid out how the agency handled expedited copy and read-back. The client kept objecting to money, but his fear dropped each time somebody named what happened after a missed deadline.

June followed that instead of his words.

"You don't need a lower monthly rate," she said. "You need the contract to state who calls you before a delay becomes your problem."

Silence reached the reception desk.

The man looked down at the bid. "Can you put that in writing?"

"Before close of business," Cyd said.

"And identify the backup reporter by name on each assignment?"

Sondra said, "Yes."

"Then send the revision. If legal approves it, we'll restore the contract."

June did not let him close the folder. "Restore means the same volume and priority as before termination?"

Fear flickered.

"Subject to need."

"That isn't restoration. That's permission to call us."

Cyd's shoe touched hers beneath the table.

The client looked down. "Same volume for six months. Priority shared for thirty days, then reviewed."

"Put the review date in the revision," Cyd said. "Then we have a deal."

His fear eased. Not gone. Answered. June stored the distinction.

Cyd did not celebrate. She closed the folder and said, "You'll have it by four," as though contracts returned every day to women who had nearly lost them before breakfast.

In the elevator, Sondra waited until the doors closed.

"What the hell was that?"

Only Cyd was allowed to swear with real authority, but Sondra had earned the attempt.

"A client with a missed deadline," June said.

"He said price six times."

"He touched the schedule every time he said it."

That was true. It was also not how June knew.

Sondra considered it. "I didn't see that."

"You were watching his face."

"Where were you watching?"

"Everything he did when the deadline came up."

"That's not an answer either."

"It's the answer I have."

Sondra did not push. She would wait for evidence and remember the evasion.

The elevator opened. Cyd walked out ahead of them without answering for June, which was worse than an interruption.

They drove back to Cyd's house because the revised clause had to be drafted before four and the agency printer was being serviced. At the kitchen table, Cyd dictated while Sondra worked on her laptop and June checked each sentence for what it actually promised.

"Named backup on assignment," June said. "Not named backup reporter. If you name a person and she gets sick, you've breached before the job starts."

Sondra changed it.

"Notice of anticipated delay," Cyd said. "No, wait, anticipated by whom? They will use that to say we should have known a witness would talk for nine hours about a loading dock. Reasonably anticipated. Put reasonably in. Lawyers adore reasonably because it means six months of invoices later."

The work settled June. Human terms. Human remedies. A contract could still hurt someone, but it did not wake up inside their blood.

Sondra sent the draft at three twenty-eight. Approval came at three fifty-one.

"Restored," she said. "Six-month term."

June made her open the approval and read the operative paragraph rather than trust the subject line. The volume guarantee matched. The thirty-day priority review was dated. The client's right to cancel for missed deadlines now required written notice and a chance to cure.

"It says restored," Sondra said.

"Now it does."

Cyd took the laptop and forwarded the approval to the agency archive, herself, and a folder labeled *People Who Will Swear They Never Said That.*

"Business classification," she said when Sondra looked at her.

June could smell no hidden threat in the email. That was the limit of the new literacy: paper carried only what people put into it. She still needed the ordinary habits that had kept Cyd's agency alive.

They rebuilt the next month's assignments around the returned contract. Sondra kept the difficult Wednesday session. June took the expedited copy. Cyd refused June's attempt to give away two additional days.

"You won work," Cyd said. "You don't apologize by becoming unavailable for it."

"I'm not apologizing."

"You moved your name off the board while speaking. That's an apology with office supplies."

June put her name back.

The restored contract changed more than the client meeting. It put billable hours beneath all three women and made June's inexplicable judgment valuable enough that Cyd could no longer dismiss it as stress.

"We should write a checklist for client fear," Cyd said.

"No," June answered too quickly.

Sondra looked up. "Why not?"

No checklist could teach a human nose to separate deadline fear from price. Writing the method would expose that June's evidence had not come from posture alone.

"Because it worked once," June said. "We write process after repetition, not after luck."

That satisfied Sondra. Cyd continued watching.

June added one legitimate question to the intake notes: *If price changed, would you sign today?* The question could expose a false objection without supernatural evidence. She preserved the tool everyone could use and concealed why she had known when to use it.

Cyd opened a bottle of wine and poured three glasses without asking whether the workday was over.

"To fear," she said. "Apparently our new sales strategy."

Sondra raised her glass but kept looking at June.

"You really got all that from his thumb?"

"And the wording. He never said the bid was unaffordable. He said it was outside the budget. Those aren't the same."

"They usually are."

"Not today."

Sondra's phone rang. She took the call into the front room, already talking about the added deposition from the warehouse case.

Cyd waited until the door swung shut.

"Don't give me the thumb."

June took one drink. "It was a very informative thumb."

"You've known me nine years. I know when you're building an answer while you're saying it."

"Then why ask?"

"Because I want to give you one more chance to stop."

The kitchen smelled of tomato sauce from lunch, Cyd's wine, and the rain drying on all three coats by the back door. Cyd herself remained unreadable in the new way. Human. No fear June could separate from ordinary evidence. No scent of illness or violence or kinship. Only the face of her closest friend, which required history instead of instinct.

June had promised her an explanation twice.

"I noticed his attention," she said. "I've been hyperaware since the Hall. It's probably stress."

"Probably."

"And I got lucky."

"Twice. The warehouse witness and today."

"Two things can happen."

"Three can too. Want to wait?"

June looked at the contract approval on the laptop. Her new literacy had just restored six months of work. It had also let her answer a question the client had refused to state. Useful things became instruments quickly in this world. Tess had proved that with nine index cards. The Trust had proved it with nine clauses.

Cyd said, "I am not asking whether you're stressed. I'm asking what is happening to you."

In the front room, Sondra laughed once into the phone. The ordinary sound made the kitchen feel smaller.

June could tell Cyd about the Hall, the Articles, Elias, the moon, and the brass key marked six in her bag. She could also picture Cyd doing exactly what Cyd did with every practical emergency: making calls, moving staff, putting her own name between June and whoever came next. Cyd had nine employees and a lease. June's trouble had already reached the client list.

"Nothing is happening to me," June said. "I'm tired, I'm watching people too closely, and today it happened to work. That's all."

The lie came out clean. Better than the promise on the phone. Better because June had selected facts that were true and arranged them around the missing thing.

Cyd studied her without the advantage of blood, scent, or a room full of members who could not conceal a position.

At last she nodded.

June understood then that Cyd was the only person left she could lie to.

So she did.
