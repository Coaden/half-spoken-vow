# Episode Twenty-Four: Bound

Episode Twenty-Four: Bound

Elias began clause two at half his normal pace while Milo stood in the center of the Hall and June was held outside the speaking circle.

The circle held one folding chair, the Articles on a music stand, and the minute book at Marta's elbow. A room full of bodies willing to bear what they heard made the boundary real.

Elias had required the chair, water, and an unobstructed path to the door. Marta had agreed. Constance's instruction lay inside his file: *Obtain acceptance sufficient to survive a later claim of confusion.*

She wanted an enforceable record. Elias intended to give her one. He also intended to make refusal possible enough that no honest witness could mistake endurance for assent.

"Upon satisfaction of residence," Elias read, then stopped.

The pause was not required.

Milo knew it. June knew it. Marta, seated beside the minute book, knew it. Nobody objected.

"The bride shall provide surety for faithful performance of the Articles."

Elias lowered the page. "Milo Havlik, you may withdraw your volunteered acceptance now. No cause is required."

Milo's hands shook once and became still. "I don't withdraw."

June stood beyond the chalked circle with Kenneth beside her. Her lack of standing had become a physical distance of eight feet.

"He doesn't understand the consequences," she said.

Marta answered, "He will hear each one."

Elias continued.

"The surety shall answer for any default by the named bride until completion, release, or lawful defeat of the sequence. A default may be performed against the surety's standing, liberty, property, or person, to the extent named in the executing clause."

He paused.

"Tell me what standing means here," Elias said.

Milo glanced toward Marta. "My membership. My vote. Benefits. Whether I'm in good standing."

"It may include all of those. Liberty?"

"Being made to stay somewhere. Or go somewhere."

"Property?"

"Anything I can legally surrender."

"Person?"

For the first time, Milo looked toward the empty chair. "Me."

The Hall remained quiet. Elias let the answer remain audible in it.

"Do you withdraw?"

"No."

"Milo," June said.

He did not look at her.

Elias remembered Lenora Renn's surety attempting to object after the office had already been declared attached. Constance's marginal instruction corrected the procedure. It did not correct the clause.

"The surety's obligation is independent of affection, intent, and later disagreement with the bride. It remains until one of the stated endings occurs. Do you withdraw?"

Milo swallowed. "No."

"You can," June said. "Nobody gets to call it cowardice."

"June Havlik is not recognized in the circle," Marta said.

Elias looked at her. "Her speech need not be recognized to constitute a refusal or advice."

"It cannot alter his answer."

"No."

He gave Milo the next consequence. If June failed a clause, enforcement could fall on him before notice reached her. He might be required to appear, remain, surrender an office, or perform a named deed. No payment could discharge it.

"If you are ordered to perform a deed," Elias said, "you cannot substitute money, property, or another person unless the executing clause permits substitution. If the deed costs you employment, housing, or safety, hardship does not discharge it. State your understanding."

Milo's jaw worked before he spoke. "If they name something I'm supposed to do, I have to do that thing. I can't buy my way out."

"And if performance injures you?"

"It still counts."

"That is incomplete. Injury does not make an unnamed deed valid. You are bound only to what a clause lawfully names."

June's voice came from beyond the chalk. "Make him repeat that part."

Marta started to object.

"It is a request concerning accuracy," Elias said. "Accuracy is useful to the record."

He looked at Milo. "What are you bound to perform?"

"Only what a clause lawfully names. Not whatever somebody decides would punish me enough."

Elias inclined his head. June had acted through the record even when the circle denied her a role inside it.

"Do you withdraw?"

"No."

Milo finally looked at June. His fear was plain to every member in the Hall. So was the thing beneath it: decision, borne in presence where none of them could call it confusion.

"I leaked what she was doing," Milo said. "I helped bring this clause sooner."

"Guilt is not consent," Elias told him.

"It isn't only guilt."

"Then state what remains without it."

Milo began, stopped, and began again. "She has nobody in the room who can answer as family. I can. I want to."

"That explains why you came," Elias said. "It does not prove why you stay."

Milo's attention settled on him. "What's the difference?"

"You came believing you could repair an injury. You now know the office may create another. If repair is impossible, what choice are you making?"

The coffee urn clicked as its heater engaged. Somewhere near the rear wall, a folding chair shifted under a member's weight.

Milo said, "I'm making sure they can't say she failed because nobody from her line would stand up."

"They may say she failed for another reason."

"Then it won't be that one."

The answer was smaller than heroism and more durable. Elias wrote it in his private notes.

"Wanting to protect her does not mean you understand default," Elias said.

"Then ask me."

Elias departed from the prepared text. "If she refuses a clause and enforcement falls on you, will you blame her?"

"I don't know."

"If she could end your obligation by answering the vow and refuses to do so, will you ask her to answer?"

Milo looked at June. "Probably."

"If she still refuses?"

"Then she refuses."

"You may hate her and remain bound. You may regret this and remain bound. You may believe the clause unjust and remain bound. Do you understand?"

"Yes."

June said, "He understands the words. That isn't the same as living them."

"No person can demonstrate future regret in advance," Marta said. "That cannot be the test."

Elias knew she was right. Consent did not require prophecy. It required knowledge of permanent facts and freedom at the moment of choice. Surety was not permanent, but its uncertainty demanded at least as much honesty.

He read Milo the available endings again: completion, release, lawful defeat. Not forgiveness. Not changed feeling. Not family asking nicely.

"Who may release you?" Elias asked.

"Whoever has authority under the Articles when release is requested."

"Does June possess that authority?"

"No."

June made a sound that stopped before it became a word.

"Can I promise you release?" Elias asked.

"No."

"Can Marta?"

Milo looked at her again. This time he answered without waiting for help. "Not by herself."

"If all of us tell you informally that we will protect you, what effect does that have on the office?"

"None."

"Do you rely on any such promise?"

"No."

Marta opened both hands on the table, showing the Hall she had offered no concealed bargain. A trustee would have wanted an affidavit. The Hall wanted her resolve carried plainly in the room.

Milo repeated each one in his own words. His voice steadied as the price became less abstract and more terrible.

Elias asked once more whether anyone had threatened his membership, housing, wages, or family if he refused. Milo said no. Marta confirmed it before the Hall. June could not disprove freedom merely because the free choice hurt her.

"Has anyone promised you advancement, repayment, forgiveness, or improved standing?" Elias asked.

"No."

"Did anyone tell you that kinship required acceptance?"

Milo hesitated. "Nobody had to."

"That was not my question."

"No. Nobody told me it was required."

"Do you believe June's blood gives her authority to accept for you?"

"No."

"Do you believe her refusal can prevent your acceptance?"

His eyes went to June. "No."

That answer injured her more visibly than any before it. Elias did not soften the question. Freedom that existed only while nobody used it was decoration.

The Hall heard him.

June's face did not forgive the choice. Elias respected her for that. Love did not turn an injury into permission merely because both people accepted the wound.

He read the final condition.

"Acceptance must be spoken in your own words. Silence does not accept. Presence does not accept. Kinship does not accept. You may refuse now or at any time before your complete statement enters the witnessed record."

He waited longer than the instruction required.

Marta did not hurry him.

Milo said, "I am Milo Havlik, a member in good standing. I accept the office of surety for June Havlik under clause two, knowing I can be made to answer for her default until the sequence is completed, released, or lawfully defeated. I accept now."

The obligation entered the room.

Elias felt it as a trustee felt a filing settle: a pressure becoming fixed. Around him, members reacted through bodies rather than paper. Chairs creaked. Breath changed. Kinship did not alter, but Milo's standing acquired weight.

Yolanda read his statement back.

She did it without emphasis. When she finished, Elias asked her to repeat the three endings, then whether any other ending had entered the record. It had not.

"Is that what you said?" Elias asked.

"Yes."

"Is it what you meant?"

"Yes."

Elias completed the disposition. Clause two was active. Milo stood bound as surety. The clock of default now ran through another person's life.

Marta called the witnesses. Each answered aloud that Milo had been informed, free to refuse, and had accepted in his own words. Elias signed for the Trust.

Then the minute book closed.

Milo gripped the unused chair. His body remained ordinary while the consequences learned its name.

June entered the circle after Marta erased one chalk mark with her shoe.

"You had no right," she told Milo.

"I had standing."

"That's not what I said."

"I know."

Milo turned to Elias. "Thank you."

The words were so unexpected Elias answered the literal question inside them. "For what?"

"Giving me room. The pauses."

"They should not be unusual."

"They were."

Elias closed the clause file.

Outside, the alley air was cold enough to cut through his coat. He reached the brick wall before his body rejected the blood he had taken earlier. Nothing in a trustee required nausea. Memory supplied it.

Lenora's surety had thanked him too. Not for pauses—there had been none—but for explaining the order clearly after the office attached. Elias had accepted the gratitude as evidence of competent administration.

Four days later, the executing clause had named a deed the surety could not perform without losing his home. Elias had read that one at normal speed.

He was sick into the gutter with one hand braced against the wall.

When he straightened, Marta stood at the alley door.

She had watched him do it.
