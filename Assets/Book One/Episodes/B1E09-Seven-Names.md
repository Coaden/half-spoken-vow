# Episode Nine: Seven Names

Episode Nine: Seven Names

She came back the next counted night with her shoes in her hand.

She had thought about it for two days, on buses and between sessions and once in the middle of a deposition about a boundary dispute, and she had arrived at one conclusion, which was that the voice had not been hiding.

A person hiding does not read at that speed. A person hiding does not do the small courteous pause at a clause break. Whoever had been reading at three in the morning had been reading the way you read when the reading is the point, and June had spent twelve years around exactly one activity that sounded like that.

Somebody in this house had been checking a record.

The passage on the first floor ran back past three closed doors to a fourth that was open two inches with light behind it, and June went down it in stocking feet with her heart going, and the voice got clearer, and it did not stop.

"Item the seventh. Renn."

She put her hand on the door.

It was a linen room. Shelves to the ceiling, sheets in stacks, a smell of soap and cedar, a bare bulb, and a small table under it with two chairs.

Nadia Kovach sat in one of them with a ledger open in front of her, reading aloud to the room.

In the other chair, listening with her eyes shut, sat nobody at all.

"You may as well come in," Nadia said, without looking up. "You've been on that board eleven seconds and it's the loud one."

June came in.

"Sit down. Not that chair."

She sat in the third one, a stool by the shelves.

"Who's the other chair for?"

"My mother." Nadia turned a page. "Then it was for my grandmother, and my mother sat where I'm sitting. You read it out and somebody listens, and if the two don't match you find out that night instead of in forty years."

June stood in the doorway with her shoes in her hand and understood that she was looking at the thing she had been trying to describe to herself for nine days without the words for it.

Two records. One written and one carried. And in this house, at three in the morning, somebody had been checking them against each other for ninety-one years.

"Match what?"

"What's written against what's remembered." She said it the way you would explain a light switch. "Paper's a good servant and a poor witness. It'll say whatever the last hand wrote and it won't blink. So we read it out. And when there's nobody left to listen —" she nodded at the empty chair "— you read it out anyway, because the day you stop is the day it starts being whatever somebody needs it to be."

"Does he know you do this?"

"Mister Marrow knows I keep a house." Nadia turned a page. "He has never asked me what's in the linen room, and I have never volunteered, and both of those are decisions he and I have made every day for eleven years."

"That's not a no."

"It's the most accurate answer I've got."

June looked at the ledger. It was smaller than the Hall's, bound in cloth, and very old.

"The Trust doesn't know this exists," she said.

"The Trust has a vault."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Nadia agreed, "it isn't."

"Whose ledger is it?"

"The house's."

"That's not a person."

"No," said Nadia. "It isn't. That's rather the point of it." She smoothed the page flat with the side of her hand. "A person can be persuaded, or dismissed, or buried. My grandmother worked that out in nineteen thirty-four and she made the book belong to the building instead of to her, and she wrote that on the first page, and every one of us since has signed under it."

She turned the ledger around.

The entries were in six or seven different hands, going back, and the ink changed color as it went, and June's eyes went to the top of the page and started down.

It was a list of names.

"Read it," Nadia said. "Out loud. I'll listen."

So June read.

"Item the first. Sorley. Eighteen seventy-three. Released."

"Go on."

"Item the second. Baird. Eighteen ninety-one." She stopped. There was a second column she had not registered, narrow, and in it a single numeral. "Two."

"Go on."

"Item the third. Danko. Nineteen fourteen. Two." Her voice had changed and she could hear it changing and could not do anything about it. "Item the fourth. Havlik. Nineteen forty-six. Four."

The bulb hummed.

"Item the fifth. Ostrowski. Nineteen sixty-eight. Released. Item the sixth. Kovach, A. Nineteen ninety-two."

She stopped.

"Item the seventh," said Nadia.

"There isn't a disposition beside six."

"No. There isn't. Now read seven."

June looked.

Below it, in newer ink, in a hand she had watched writing a laundry list on Tuesday, there was a line that said:

*Item the seventh. Renn. Two thousand and fifteen. Two.*

The second column beside Kovach was blank.

June sat back.

"Four of them have a clause number," she said. "Baird, Danko, Havlik, Renn. Two, two, four, two."

"Yes."

"And a clause number means —"

"It means that's how it was satisfied," said Nadia.

June sat with that for a while. There was a stack of pillowcases at her elbow, ironed and squared, and she found she had put one hand flat on it.

"Havlik," she said. "Nineteen forty-six."

"Your great-aunt. Your grandfather's sister." Nadia's voice did not change at all. "Clause the fourth. She was twenty-two."

"Nobody told me she existed."

"Did anybody tell you your mother had a sister?"

"Marta. Yes. Obviously."

"Then somebody chose which parts of that family you got." Nadia turned the page back. "I'd think about who."

"Nobody told me she existed."

"No. They wouldn't have."

June looked at the page for a long moment, at six generations of handwriting, at four numerals in a narrow column, at a hundred and fifty years of women who had been *satisfied*.

"You said Trust doesn't know," she said. "Say the rest of it."

"The Trust keeps a record of what the instrument did." Nadia closed the ledger, carefully, with one finger inside it. "The Trust's record is accurate and complete and I have no quarrel with it. It says: named, date, disposition. That's all a vault needs."

"And this one?"

"This one says what they were like."

She opened it again, further back, and turned it around, and June saw that below each entry there was a paragraph. Small, cramped, in ink and later in ballpoint, and different every time.

*Would not eat what was put in front of her. Learned all our names in a week and used them.*

*Frightened of the stairs and would not say why.*

*Sang in the kitchen. Filthy language.*

*Made her own tea and would not be waited on. Left three pounds on the dresser for my mother and my mother never spent it.*

*Called the room the guest room, every day, for nine months, and never once called it hers.*

"That's not a record," June said, and her voice came out wrong.

"It is, though. It's just not one anybody's ever asked us for." Nadia set her hand flat on the page. "Four women died under this roof and the only official sentence about any of them is a number in a column. Somebody has to be the other record. My grandmother decided that was going to be us, in nineteen thirty-four, and nobody's rescinded it because nobody's ever known to."

June read the last one twice.

*Called the room the guest room, every day, for nine months, and never once called it hers.*

"Who was that?"

"Renn," said Nadia. "Two thousand and fifteen."

"Clause two."

"Clause two."

The house was very quiet.

"How many people have read this?"

"Living? Three." Nadia counted them without hesitation. "Me. Father Delisle, once, in two thousand and sixteen, and he asked to and I let him and he was sick afterward. And you."

"Not Mrs. Marrow."

"Not Mrs. Marrow."

"Why are you showing me?"

"Because you came back," said Nadia. "Nobody comes back. They hear it once, they lie there till morning, and then they never come down that passage again, and I've always understood it and it's always been a shame."

June looked at the sixth item.

"Kovach, A.," she read. "Nineteen ninety-two. No clause."

"No."

"So she's —"

"There's no number and no release," June said. "So either it's not finished, or —"

"That's a different question," Nadia said, "and it's the one you should be asking, and I'll answer it, but not tonight, because it takes an hour and you've got a session at nine."

She shut the ledger and stood, and put it back on the shelf between two stacks of sheets, which June understood, suddenly and completely, was the safest place in the house. No lock, no box, no floorboard. A book in a linen cupboard, in plain sight, in a room nobody with any authority in this building had ever once had a reason to enter.

"Ask me something else instead," Nadia said. "A small one. You'll sleep better with a small one."

June stood too. Her feet were freezing.

"Four of them," she said. "You said you keep this because somebody has to. But you said four died and there are six with numbers or releases before yours. So you knew some of them."

"I knew two of them well enough to say so."

"Which two?"

Nadia Kovach turned the bulb off, and in the dark, on the landing, she said:

"That's not a small one either. But I'll tell you which two I *liked*, and I'll tell you that one of the two is still alive, and then I'm putting you to bed."
