# Episode Ten: Which Two

Episode Ten: Which Two

Nadia made coffee at half past four in the morning without being asked and without asking, and the first thing she said about the one who got out was not what June expected.

"She wasn't clever."

"You said you liked her."

"I did like her. She was not clever and I want that said first, because you're clever, and clever is going to be the thing that kills you if you're not careful." Nadia set the pot down. "Ostrowski. Nineteen sixty-eight. Twenty-six years old, worked at a bakery on Butler Street, could not have told you what a clause was."

"And she got released."

"She got released in fourteen months, whole, on her own feet, with her own name."

June wrapped both hands around the cup.

"How."

"She made herself necessary to somebody who wasn't a party."

The kitchen clock ticked. Outside the window it was still completely black, the specific black of four in the morning in November when the sun has not even begun to think about it.

"Say more."

"There's the Trust, and there's the Local, and those two are bound to the instrument and they will grind along until the end of the world doing what it says, because that's what they are." Nadia sat down. "But there are other houses in this county. Other trusts, other locals, people with money and standing who are not parties to your Articles and never signed a thing. And they all want something, and none of them are obliged to want what the instrument wants."

"And she found one."

"She found a man across the river who needed somebody who could read a room and keep her mouth shut, and she was very good at both, and she made herself worth more to him alive and free than she was worth to anybody as a satisfied clause." Nadia turned her cup a quarter turn. "By the time clause three came due there were three parties with an interest in her staying exactly where she was. So it got waived."

"Waived by all parties."

"That's the third way out and it's the only one anybody's ever managed." Nadia looked up. "Satisfied, waived, or defeated. Four women got satisfied. Nobody in a hundred and fifty-five years has ever defeated one. Ostrowski got waived, and she got waived because she was *useful*, and I have thought about that woman every single day for fifty-eight years."

"Did she know she was doing it?"

"She knew exactly what she was doing. That's the part people get wrong about her." Nadia refilled both cups without asking. "Everybody who's heard that story since decides she stumbled into it, because she worked in a bakery and she said *youse*. She didn't stumble into anything. She sat at this table in nineteen sixty-eight and she said to my mother, I can't beat this, but somebody out there wants something and I'm going to find out what."

June wrote *waived* on the back of a receipt from her coat pocket.

"That's a strategy," she said.

"That's the only strategy."

"It's also a way to end up owned by somebody else."

"Yes," said Nadia. "It is exactly that. She spent fourteen months making herself indispensable to a man who would have spent her the second she stopped being indispensable, and she knew it, and she did it anyway, because the alternative had a number in a column."

The coffee was very strong and very bad and June drank it.

"What happened to her after?"

"She went back to the bakery," said Nadia. "For thirty-one years. Married a man who drove a delivery route and never told him a word of it, and buried him, and outlived him by a decade."

"That's it?"

"That's it. That's the entire prize." Nadia looked at her over the cup. "You want it to be bigger. It isn't bigger. The prize is that nothing else happens to you, and you get to be bored, and you get to be old."

"Tell me about the other one."

Nadia was quiet for a moment.

"Renn," she said. "Two thousand and fifteen. Clause the second."

"She's the one who called it the guest room."

"Every day. Nine months." Nadia's hands were flat on the table. "She was clever. That's why I said what I said. She was clever the way you are — she read everything, she asked good questions, she caught two things in that instrument that nobody had ever caught. She sat where you're sitting and she told me she'd found a way through and I believed her, because she was right."

"What happened?"

"She was right and she was alone." Nadia said it without any inflection at all, which was the worst possible way to hear it. "She found the argument, and she filed it herself, and she filed it late — not by much. Four days. Because she'd spent the eleven days before that arguing with her sister about whether she was allowed to accept help, and the sister wanted to bring somebody in, and Renn said no, because if she brought somebody in she'd owe them."

June had stopped moving.

"She was so careful not to owe anybody," Nadia said, "that she ran out of days."

June sat with that.

"That's not a lesson," she said. "That's just a bad outcome. She was right and she lost. That happens."

"It does happen. It happened in this house on a Thursday." Nadia's voice stayed level. "And I'm not telling it to you as a lesson, because I don't know what the lesson is. I've had eleven years and I still don't. I'm telling it to you because you're clever and alone in exactly the same shape, and I would rather you heard it from somebody who liked her than found it in a file."

The refrigerator hummed.

"Her sister's still alive," June said.

"Her sister has spent eleven years on this and she is the single most frightening person I have ever met, and she will find you inside a month, and when she does I want you to remember that I told you about her here, at this table, before she got to you." Nadia stood and took the pot back to the stove. "Because she's going to be right about nearly everything. That's what makes her dangerous."

June's phone went at six.

She had it face down on the table and it buzzed against the wood and Cyd's name came up, and June looked at it for two rings, sitting in a stranger's kitchen in Sewickley in yesterday's clothes at six in the morning.

She answered.

"Where are you?"

"Working."

"You're not working, I have the schedule, you're not on anything till nine." A pause, and traffic behind Cyd's voice, and the particular tightness that meant she'd been up a while. "Lyle Frank's office called yesterday at five and pulled you off the whole December calendar. By name. Not the agency. You."

June closed her eyes.

"Did they say why?"

"They said a scheduling matter and then the man on the phone told me — and I want you to hear this exactly, because I wrote it down — he said, *we'd rather not have a reporter who's a party to something*." Cyd's voice cracked on the last word and came back harder. "So somebody's ringing round my clients about you, June. Somebody who knows what a party is."

Nadia had gone very still by the stove.

"How much was December," June said.

"Don't do that."

"How much."

"About four thousand," Cyd said. "And I don't care."

"You do care, you've got nine people —"

"I *have* nine people and a lease and I am telling you I don't care, and what I want, right now, at six in the morning, is for you to tell me what is going on, because I have known you nine years and you have never once lied to me and I have been sitting in my car since five deciding whether you started."

The kitchen was completely silent.

June looked at the receipt on the table with *waived* written on the back of it. She thought about a woman in nineteen sixty-eight who had gotten out by making herself useful to somebody who would have spent her. She thought about a clever woman in two thousand and fifteen who had been so careful not to owe anybody that she had run out of days.

"Cyd," she said.

"Yeah."

And she could not do it. Not on a phone, not in somebody else's house, not with a steward four feet away pretending to be busy with a stove.

"I'm going to tell you what I can," June said. "Not on the phone. Tomorrow, at your place. The work part first."

There was a long pause.

"Okay," Cyd said. "Okay. That's — okay. That's better than what I thought."

When she'd rung off, Nadia turned around from the stove.

"That was well done," she said.

"That was cowardice."

"It was part of the truth instead of another lie. In this house that's not the same thing." Nadia dried her hands. "Now. Before you go, and before her sister finds you, and before you decide you're going to do all of this on your own like Renn did — there's something in the book I didn't show you last night."

"Which."

"Ostrowski left a forwarding address."

June looked up.

"In nineteen sixty-nine," Nadia said, "when she was released, she wrote it in the back of that ledger in her own hand, and underneath it she wrote *if another one comes, she may write to me*, and in fifty-seven years not one person has asked me for it."

She put the coffee pot down.

"So that's the question, and it's the only one that matters this morning. Are you going to be the one who writes to her?"

"Or?"

"Or you'll be a number in a column," said Nadia Kovach, "and I'll write a nice paragraph underneath it, and I'll mean every word."
