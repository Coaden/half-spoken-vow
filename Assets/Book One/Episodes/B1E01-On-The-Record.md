# Episode One: On the Record

Episode One: On the Record

Nine minutes into the deposition, June Havlik had already worked out that she was not going to make rent.

The math was simple and she had done it twice on the bus. Two hundred and eighty dollars for the morning session. Three hundred and ten for the afternoon, if the afternoon happened, which it would not, because opposing counsel had the flu and everybody in the room knew it. That left her nine hundred dollars short by Friday and no session booked until Tuesday.

So she did what she always did, which was put the number in a drawer in the back of her head and shut it, and keep her hands moving.

She had sworn the witness at nine oh two. She had set her machine at nine oh five, checked her audio backup, checked it again, and written the case caption across the top of her notes in block capitals, because in twelve years she had learned that the one thing you could not reconstruct afterward was what you had assumed you would remember.

"Mister Pratt," the attorney across the table said. "You said the transfer occurred in March of two thousand nineteen."

"I said it was recorded in March," said Pratt. "It occurred the previous November."

June's fingers did not stop. On the page behind her eyes she saw the earlier answer, twenty minutes old. Pratt had said the transfer occurred in March. Not that it was recorded in March. Occurred. Now he was saying November.

She marked it. Not because anybody had asked her to. Because a transcript in which an event happened in March on page forty-three and November on page fifty-eight was a transcript that would come back to her in six weeks with a lawyer's note attached, and she had never once had a transcript come back.

Aloysius Pratt was a small, dry, immaculate man who wore a pocket square to a records deposition, and he was the happiest person in the room. He liked documents. Not the way a lawyer liked documents, as a place to hide things. He liked them the way a man likes a well-made chair.

At the far end of the table sat a heavy woman in a windbreaker.

She had not been introduced. She had not spoken. She had a spiral notebook in front of her that she had not opened, and reading glasses on a beaded cord, and hands that had done forty years of something that was not this. She was not a lawyer. June had recorded eleven hundred depositions and she knew a lawyer the way she knew a misspoken date.

The woman had been watching her since the swearing-in. Not the way a stranger watches a court reporter, which is to say not at all. The way you watch a thing you have already made a decision about.

"Let's go to Exhibit Four," Pratt said.

Paper moved. June's hands moved with it.

"For the record," Pratt said, "Exhibit Four is a certified copy of articles of agreement executed on the eleventh of June, eighteen seventy-one, between the parties identified therein. I'll read the operative portion."

He read it the way he did everything, evenly, without decoration, and June typed it the way she did everything, half a second behind his voice and not listening to it at all. That was the trick of the job. You did not listen. If you listened you fell behind, and then you were reconstructing instead of recording, and reconstruction was how transcripts got sued over.

So the recitals and the first two constitutional sections went through her hands and into the machine without touching her.

"Under the article of naming," Pratt said, "the obligation shall be borne in each generation by the naming of a bride. Naming makes that person a party to the instrument. It does not activate a numbered clause."

Her hands kept going.

"There are nine operative clauses," Pratt said, "fixed in sequence. Since the execution of the agreement, seven brides have been named."

Somewhere on the other side of the drawer in the back of her head, something opened.

"For completeness," Pratt said, and turned a page, "the eighth named is June Elizabeth Havlik, of Polish Hill, in this county."

Her hands kept going.

That was the part she would think about later, and for a long time. Not the sentence. Her hands. They took her own name off his voice and put it into the machine and did not falter, because eleven hundred depositions had taught them that stopping was worse than anything that could possibly be said.

Nobody in the room looked up.

The woman in the windbreaker did not look up either. With her it was a different kind of not looking — the kind you do when somebody reads you a page you have already read.

The attorney with the flu said, "I'm sorry, is that a living person?"

"It is," said Pratt.

"Do we need her?"

"She is present," said Pratt.

And that was when the door at the back of the room opened.

It opened from the interior corridor, the one with no windows, which ran along the core of the building past the freight elevator and the mechanical room. June knew that corridor. Everybody who worked in the building knew it, because it was where you went in July when you could not face the lobby.

A man came out of it.

He was tall and narrow and wore a dark three-piece suit of no particular decade, and June's chest turned toward him.

That was the only way she could put it afterward, and she was a person who was paid to put things exactly. Not her head. Not her attention. Something behind her sternum, low, physical, and entirely her own, leaned toward a stranger the way a compass needle leans, and she felt it happen with the specific horror of a woman feeling her own body do something without asking her.

She kept typing. Her hands, again. Her hands were the only part of her still doing its job.

"Father," Pratt said, pleasantly, to the far corner. "Would you note the time."

June had not registered the priest. He had been sitting against the wall behind the deponent's chair since before she set up, a tired man in a black shirt with his hands folded, and she had taken him for a family member, because probate was full of them.

"Ten forty-one," the priest said.

"And you are present as an accepted witness."

"I am."

"Thank you." Pratt turned to the man in the doorway with something that was almost warmth. "Whenever you're ready."

The man came to the end of the table. He did not sit.

"For the record," he said. "Elias Marrow. Trustee."

"Noted," said Pratt.

He opened a folder.

June's hands were ready for a clause. Her hands, at this point, wanted a clause. A clause was a thing with a shape, and shapes could be typed.

Elias Marrow looked at her instead.

It was not a long look and there was nothing kind in it. It was the look of a man checking the level of water in a glass before he picks it up.

Then he said, "Let it reflect that I am not reading."

Pratt's pen stopped.

"I speak the first half," said Elias Marrow, "before an accepted witness. I offer myself, by name, to the woman named as the eighth. Elias Marrow. That is the whole of my half, and it is given freely, and it is given now."

Nobody moved.

June did not understand it. She wanted that on some record of her own, later, that she had not understood one thing about it, not what he was doing or why or what it was going to cost anybody. She had never heard the word trustee used the way this room used it.

But she understood the grammar.

She understood the grammar the way she understood a misspoken date, instantly, without deciding to, because grammar was the water she swam in. That was a complete statement. It was closed at both ends. It was not a request and it was not an offer waiting on a condition. It was a whole sentence that had a hole in it exactly the size of a second sentence, and until somebody said the second one, it was going to sit there being half of something.

The heavy woman in the windbreaker was on her feet.

Pratt had gone the color of paper.

And in the silence, June Havlik did the only thing that a person in her chair is permitted to do. She lifted her hands off the keys, which is the signal, and she said:

"Clarification for the record."

Four words. Her voice came out flat and professional and entirely steady, and it was the strangest thing she had ever done, because she was asking on behalf of a document about a sentence that had her name in it.

Pratt said, "Yes. Yes, of course."

"The statement wasn't directed to counsel," June said. "It was directed to a party. I need the record to show what it was."

She was not asking what it meant. She would think about that later too. She had asked what it was, because what a thing was could go in a transcript and what a thing meant could not, and even then, even with her chest still leaning, she had not stopped being good at her job.

The priest unfolded his hands.

"Let the record reflect," he said, and he said it slowly, the way a man says a thing he has said before and hoped not to say again. "The first half of a binding vow has been given by Elias Marrow, of the Marrow Trust, before me, and accepted into the record."

He looked at June.

"It binds only him," he said. "Not her."

Nobody explained it. Nobody was going to.

The attorney across the table was looking between the three of them like a woman who had come to a records deposition and found herself in weather. Pratt was writing, fast, in a hand June could not read upside down. The heavy woman in the windbreaker had not sat back down, and she was not looking at Elias Marrow at all. She was looking at June, and her face had nothing in it but arithmetic.

June put her hands back on the keys, because there was a sentence waiting and it was hers to take down, and she typed it exactly as spoken, both halves of the exchange, in order, the way she had typed eleven hundred depositions and every one of them clean.

Because Elias Marrow was still looking at her, and what he said was:

"I know."
