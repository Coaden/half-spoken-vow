# Episode Twenty-Three: Her Own Surety

Episode Twenty-Three: Her Own Surety

With four hours left, June dictated faster than Cyd could format and refused Milo's third call.

"The named bride is the sole continuous person present in every definition of her line," June said. "Therefore—no, strike therefore. Put: Under either record, the bride cannot be outside her own line."

Cyd typed at the deposition-suite desk with her glasses halfway down her nose.

June had told her only what the filing required: an inherited private agreement had named June, the next provision threatened to make Milo a guarantor, and two institutions used incompatible definitions. Cyd knew nothing about changing bodies, sunlight, attachments, or vows. She was here because a seven-page argument still needed verbs that agreed with their subjects.

"You know computers have a speed limit imposed by spite."

"Read it back."

Cyd read the paragraph. June paced between the stenotype case and a stack of exhibits left for tomorrow's session.

The argument was simple enough to frighten her. Clause two required one member of the bride's line. June was the bride. If line meant body, she could not be excluded from herself. If it meant legal succession, the Articles already recognized her as the named party. In either system, she stood inside the required category.

What the argument did not answer was why anyone in eighteen seventy-one would have written the requirement that way. June pulled the facsimile closer and traced the sentence with one finger. The member-side hand had written *line*. A trustee clerk had inserted *surety* above it in darker ink. Two ideas occupying the same sentence without agreeing what a person was.

"Could they have meant any relative?" Cyd asked.

"The family institution doesn't use relative that loosely. Its record treats line as bodies that carry kinship."

"And to the Trust?"

"A line can be succession. An office after an office after an office."

Cyd stopped typing. "I understand those as definitions because you gave them to me. I do not understand why one side keeps a family in a room and the other keeps one in a filing."

"You don't need to. Tell me whether the sentence works under both meanings."

"So your strongest argument is that both sides accidentally included you for opposite reasons."

June looked at the clause again. "Not accidentally. They each assumed their meaning would win."

That was the weakness running through the Articles. It was also the only space they ever gave her.

"Next," June said. "Surety exists to answer for another person's default. The clause does not expressly prohibit identity between principal and surety."

"Normal surety law does," Cyd said.

"Normal surety law doesn't use a family meeting as service."

"Fair. Deeply upsetting, but fair."

The phone lit with Milo's name again. June silenced it.

"You should answer him," Cyd said.

"If he helps prepare this, Pratt will say his volunteer statement became performance."

"Did Pratt say that?"

"He might."

"So you are protecting your argument from a conversation nobody has had."

"I'm protecting Milo."

"You are making me help you alone. Different arrangement, same disease."

June stopped pacing. "Do you want me to leave?"

"I want you to cite the right case. You dictated volume eighty-four. The objection is in volume eighty-seven."

June checked. Cyd was right.

The error would have pointed Pratt to a property dispute with no relevance except proving June had worked past the point of competence.

"Fix it," she said.

"Already did. Try gratitude. It has fewer filing requirements."

June took the seat beside her. They worked through the remaining pages together. Cyd found a date inconsistency. June removed a sentence that promised more than the precedent held. At nine twenty, they had a seven-page filing with three exhibits and one proposition: June Havlik could stand as her own surety because neither record could place her outside her own line.

The first exhibit was the Hall's own kinship acknowledgment from the residence hearing. The second was the Trust's notice naming June as bride. The third was a disposition from nineteen thirty-two in which a trustee had served as both officeholder and guarantor because the instrument had failed to separate the capacities.

Cyd tapped that one. "This person already had an office."

"The point is identity isn't automatically fatal."

"The point against you is that she had two hats before she put them on at once."

June read the disposition again. The phrase *independent capacity* appeared in the fourth paragraph, but it addressed the guarantor's authority to convey property, not her eligibility to serve. June had cited the holding narrowly and accurately.

She hated how much relief accuracy could provide when it did not provide safety.

"Put the phrase in the adverse-authority section," she said.

"You think Pratt won't find it?"

"I think he'll find it faster if we look like we're hiding it."

Cyd added the paragraph. June dictated the distinction: the Articles created the bride's status before they demanded surety; therefore the capacity arose independently in time, even if both statuses arose under one instrument.

"That's clever," Cyd said.

"That's not a compliment in a filing."

"It is when I say it. When Pratt says it, start worrying."

June read the whole filing aloud. Cyd followed on screen and stopped her twice. The second read was clean.

Milo called again. This time Cyd answered.

"She's working," Cyd said. "No, you cannot help. No, I do not understand why either. Yes, I will tell her."

She ended the call.

"He says the Hall has already prepared his acceptance statement."

June deleted a sentence and restored it. "That doesn't make it valid."

"He says he wants it valid."

"He wants to repair what he leaked."

"You don't get to decide his motive because you dislike the result."

"I can smell his motive."

Cyd's hands left the keyboard.

The admission sat between them.

June had meant it literally. Cyd could not know that, but she knew it was not a figure of speech.

"We're finishing this filing," Cyd said. "Afterward, you are going to explain that sentence."

"Afterward."

"I am writing down that you said afterward, because tomorrow has lost all value."

She wrote it on a yellow note and stuck it to June's recorder. The ordinary promise carried no supernatural force. It still made June less able to leave.

June initialed the note. Cyd looked surprised.

"That doesn't make it magic," June said.

"I know. It makes it harder for you to pretend you forgot."

That was what most useful records did.

They returned to the argument. Cyd forced her to include Milo's volunteer statement as an adverse fact rather than hoping Pratt would overlook it. June framed it as unperfected intent, insufficient until final acceptance. The filing improved because somebody else had made her acknowledge the part that hurt.

June tried one final route before they sent it. If the Trust found self-surety defective, she asked for leave to cure instead of immediate activation of Milo's offer. Cyd read the request twice.

"Cure with what?"

"A qualified person I choose."

"You don't have one."

"Not yet."

"Then you're asking for time."

"Time is a remedy."

"Only if you say what it remedies."

June added that continued review would preserve both sides' interests while avoiding an unnecessary burden on a volunteered surety. It was the kind of sentence people used when the truth—*please do not put my brother in the place meant for punishment*—would not fit inside a caption.

"Send it," Cyd said.

"It needs service in both systems."

They drove to Fourth Avenue. The building's public counter closed at ten, and Pratt was waiting behind the frosted glass with his coat on.

June handed him the filing at nine fifty-two. Cyd witnessed receipt. June stated the request aloud. Pratt repeated it exactly and stamped the first page.

He stamped all three copies, wrote the time beneath his initials, and slid one back through the opening. June checked the impression while Cyd stated that she had witnessed delivery of seven pages and three exhibits. Pratt entered the same description in the Trust's intake ledger.

Writing, speech, witness. Neither lineage could later pretend the packet had arrived in the wrong kind of world.

"This is good," he said.

"Is it sufficient?"

"I cannot tell you before review."

"Can the surety and principal be the same person?"

"Under human law, generally no. Under this instrument, the question has never been presented."

"It is now."

His smile was tired and genuine. "Yes, Miss Havlik. It is."

"Will you hold Milo's statement unperfected during review?"

"Until the filing is disposed."

"And if you deny it?"

Pratt's eyes moved to the stamped packet. "The Hall may proceed on the statement already offered."

"May or will?"

"That is not my office's decision."

The answer was exact. It also told her Marta had refused to promise restraint.

Cyd waited until they reached the sidewalk. "That's it?"

"That's filing. You put good work through a slot and wait for somebody else to discover what you missed."

"Comforting profession."

At eleven, June was back in the empty deposition suite cleaning the paper tray when Pratt called.

She put him on speaker. Cyd sat on the table with her coat still on.

"I am sorry," Pratt said.

"Read the disposition."

"The argument is sound as to identity of line. It is denied as to capacity."

"Why?"

"I will read the operative sentence. The office of surety may be held only by a person possessing standing independent of the principal obligation. June Havlik's standing arises solely from her status as named bride and is not independent thereof. She therefore lacks capacity to serve."

"The nineteen thirty-two disposition treated capacities created by the same instrument as separable."

"Because the trustee possessed an existing office from which she could answer. Your status as bride creates the duty you propose to guarantee. It creates no separate fund of standing against which default can execute."

"The clause says a person."

"The clause says surety. A surety must leave the obligee something to reach that is not already inside the principal obligation."

June heard the logic close. If clause two took from her as bride, it could not also claim to have collected from her surety. The office existed because the Articles wanted a second life available for enforcement.

Not protection. Collateral.

"What about leave to cure?"

"Denied as moot. A qualified volunteer has already entered an offer before the Hall. The Articles do not authorize me to suspend a sufficient offer while you search for one you prefer."

Cyd shut her eyes.

June did not. "Has the Hall been notified?"

"The disposition was served on Marta Havlik before I called you."

The choice of sequence was not cruelty. The Hall had standing in the disposition. June had only interest.

June stared at the speaker.

No membership. No vote. No benefits. No standing outside the paper trying to spend her.

The fact had been established before she knew there were nine clauses. She had built seven pages around it and never asked whether the foundation belonged to her.

"The filing was otherwise persuasive," Pratt said. "If you possessed independent standing, I believe it would prevail."

"But I don't."

"No."

Cyd took June's hand off the back of the chair before she could tighten it further.

Pratt's voice softened. "You of all people should have seen it coming."
