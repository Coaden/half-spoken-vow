# Episode Thirty-Nine: The Contract

Episode Thirty-Nine: The Contract

Cyd read nine reduced schedules aloud, and June heard her own name in every missing hour.

"Monday, six hours cut. Tuesday, four. Wednesday, the whole standing session. Thursday, expedited copy moved in-house. Friday, nothing, which is a bold new interpretation of a schedule."

The agency office was not quiet. It was performing quiet around the open door.

The printer ran without anyone collecting its pages. Two contractors edited at desks with headphones on and no audio playing. In the break room, somebody had labeled a yogurt with yesterday's date and today's threat: *Touch this and become discoverable.* Under ordinary circumstances Cyd would have photographed it for morale.

Nobody laughed when she read Friday.

Cyd turned the scheduling board so June could see it. Nine contractors. Nine columns. Blue assignments had been erased and rewritten until the board carried grey shadows behind every surviving hour.

"Total," June said.

"One hundred and eighty-three billable hours through the end of the month. Fourteen thousand six hundred and forty dollars before copy."

"Payroll?"

"Covered. My draw isn't. Equipment replacement is delayed. Nobody gets a bonus unless I find a client under a rock."

"Health stipends?"

"Funded through next month. Software licenses renew Tuesday. I can delay the server or ask everyone to accept slower remote access."

"Don't ask them until they see the numbers."

"That is why the door is open."

Cyd had put the loss where the agency could hear it without turning June into a public confession. The staff knew a client had acted on a security report and Cyd refused to remove June from the contractor list. They did not know why sunlight mattered or what June did on full moons.

Sondra stood by the printer with a sealed envelope.

"There's another number," she said. "The client offered me twenty-four of those hours directly."

She placed the envelope beside the schedule rather than handing it over. The paper remained sealed because opening it in front of Cyd might look like acceptance and leaving it at home might look like concealment.

"When did it arrive?" Cyd asked.

"Courier at nine twelve. I called you at nine fourteen."

"Who else knows?"

"The receptionist signed for it. Nobody here knows what's inside."

Sondra had made a clean custody record before asking for help. June recognized the habit and could not resent it without resenting herself.

June looked at her.

"Not through Cyd. As an independent assignment. Same rooms you lost."

"Are you taking it?"

Sondra's anger sharpened. "I have rent too. I am not disloyal because somebody offered me work you can no longer enter a building to perform."

"I didn't say you were."

"You looked it."

"Then I looked wrong."

"I covered your chair yesterday," Sondra said. "Today the client offers it to me without the agency fee. If I accept, I make money because they hurt you. If I refuse, I lose money to prove a loyalty nobody agreed to pay for. Both choices make me useful to somebody else's argument."

"What do you want?" June asked.

"For this not to be a test of whether I love you."

"It isn't."

"Your face remains unconvinced."

June did not promise a feeling she had not achieved. "Then my face needs time. Your rent doesn't."

Cyd took the envelope. "The problem is not Sondra taking work. The problem is a client bypassing the agency after a consultant damaged our access."

She read the offer aloud. It guaranteed Sondra work only if she accepted directly and agreed not to subcontract through Cyd.

The rate was twelve percent higher than Sondra's agency rate and still cheaper for the client after removing Cyd's share. Payment was sixty days instead of thirty. The client could terminate if its security consultant objected to anyone Sondra used for support.

"They aren't only buying your hours," June said. "They're buying the right to isolate your workflow."

"And transferring collection risk to me," Sondra said. "Two months is a long time to finance a law firm."

Cyd circled the clause. "If they want continuity, they pay a retainer or keep the agency between you and accounts payable."

"That's not replacement," June said. "It's separation."

"Correct," Sondra said. "Which is why I haven't signed."

"What do you need?"

"An answer that doesn't turn my groceries into a loyalty test."

June considered the board. "Take the work if Cyd can negotiate a referral fee and the client states the arrangement does not reflect on agency quality."

Cyd shook her head. "You don't give permission. Sondra decides. I negotiate. You stop managing our feelings because the numbers make you guilty."

New terms. Already enforced.

June started again. "What information would help you decide?"

Sondra named it: payment security, whether declining would cost future agency assignments, whether Cyd would treat direct work as competition, and whether the security clause could bar anyone who spoke to June.

Cyd answered what belonged to her. She would not punish refusal or acceptance. She would waive noncompetition for this contract. She would not let the client use Sondra to poach other contractors.

The remaining answers had to come from the client.

They went to the client's lobby at noon. Cyd brought the reduced schedules. Sondra brought the unsigned offer. June attended only as the person whose removal had started the damage.

Security issued June a visitor badge that opened the lobby restroom and no interior door. She photographed its number and returned it visibly at the end. Admission far enough to negotiate did not restore access to work.

Cyd requested a conference room. The representative offered three lobby chairs beside a fountain.

"Then your lobby will hear the numbers," Cyd said.

A conference room became available.

The client representative read a prepared statement. Security concerns required restricted access. Business continuity justified direct contracting. No judgment had been made about transcript quality.

"Who recommended direct assignments?" Cyd asked.

"Our property advisers."

"Name them."

The representative checked the page. "The Marrow Trust."

Cyd did not look at June. "In what capacity?"

"Property adviser and guarantor on the building's continuity plan."

"Did the Trust threaten title, financing, insurance, or access if you retained her?"

"It advised that continuity coverage could be reviewed if we ignored a documented risk."

Ordinary leverage. A guarantor protecting a financial position through contract. The conduct did not become imaginary because the information beneath it had been compelled.

Nobody spoke for a moment.

June had expected Kearse. Elias's compelled answer had opened the route. Pratt's ordinary transmission and the Trust's ordinary indemnity rule had carried the resulting instability into the client's decision without any trustee needing to order retaliation.

"Did they recommend Sondra by name?" June asked.

"They provided a list of acceptable reporters."

"Was I on it?"

"No."

"Who signed the recommendation?"

The representative read the name of a Trust office, not a person. Sunlight-safe authority operating through a page delivered by a human hand.

"What caused that office to exclude me?"

The representative checked the incorporated notices. "A risk classification transmitted by Charles Pratt after the access dispute was entered in the custodial record. The guarantor rule excludes any named person whose presence is part of an unresolved premises dispute until review."

"Did Constance Marrow or Elias Marrow direct you to use that rule against me?"

"The notice says the classification is administrative. No trustee instruction and no condition concerning your vow or the Articles appears in the file."

June wrote the distinction down. Kearse had forced Elias to identify the pressure point. Pratt had transmitted the resulting dispute because his office transmitted records. The Trust's property office had then applied an ordinary indemnity rule to a supernatural mess without asking whether the result would look like punishment. Harm did not require a mastermind, and accuracy did not make it harmless.

"Did you ask why I was excluded?"

"The security report was incorporated by reference."

"Did you receive it?"

"No."

"Then you removed me based on a document you never read."

"We relied on our adviser."

"Put that in the written statement."

Cyd read the nine reduced schedules, one after another. She named the hours, the pay, and which contractor lost each assignment. The representative began to say the matter was outside his authority.

"Then bring in whoever has it," Cyd said. "We are not discussing a feeling. We are discussing one hundred and eighty-three hours."

The representative left twice. Each time Cyd stopped June from filling the silence with a concession. Sondra calculated the financing cost of sixty-day payment. June listed every assertion that lacked a source.

When the representative returned with authority, Cyd separated the issues. Sondra's decision first. Agency damage second. June's access review third. No settlement of one waived another.

The ordinary negotiation continued for forty minutes. No magic. No standing. Cyd secured a referral fee, a written statement preserving agency quality, and a review date for June's access.

She also obtained fifteen-day payment for Sondra, removal of the blanket support-person veto, and acknowledgment that nobody reviewed June's performance before acting. The review date was not reinstatement. Cyd made them write that too.

"Do you accept the direct assignment under these terms?" she asked.

Sondra read every page. "Yes."

No one called the choice disloyal.

Sondra accepted the direct work after all three were written down.

At the elevator, Cyd calculated the referral fee. It replaced less than one-tenth of the agency's loss.

"This is a tourniquet," she said. "Do not call it recovery."

June added the amount to her photograph of the board. The victory deserved its correct size.

Back at the agency, Cyd called everyone into the open doorway. She read the revised numbers without naming Sondra's choice as a rescue. The server would wait. Health stipends remained. Bonuses were uncertain, not canceled. Contractors whose hours changed could decline the new schedule without losing future priority.

June stood beside the board while nine people absorbed consequences that bore her outline and did not belong to her to manage. One asked whether the client had criticized quality. Cyd read the written statement. Another asked whether more contracts were at risk. Cyd said yes, because accuracy did not improve when offered to employees.

"What can we do?" somebody asked.

"Preserve every client communication," Cyd said. "Do good work. Do not speculate in writing. Bring direct offers to me before signing so I can protect the agency relationship."

No speech about family. No demand for loyalty. A workplace responding to damage with procedure.

Sondra pinned her signed assignment beside the written agency waiver. The documents showed exactly where her private work began and Cyd's claim ended.

Outside, she handed June a copy.

"I am not the villain here," Sondra said.

"I know."

"Then hear the rest without making me one. They offered me your slot. And the client mentioned the Marrow Trust by name."
