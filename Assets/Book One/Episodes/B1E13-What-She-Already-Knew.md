# Episode Thirteen: What She Already Knew

Episode Thirteen: What She Already Knew

The witness gave a flawless answer, and every nerve in June's body insisted it was false.

"No," he said. "I did not enter the warehouse after October seventh."

His voice was level. His hands stayed loose on the table. Counsel did not look up. Nothing in the sentence offered June a reason to distrust it, and distrust was not her job anyway. Her job was to take the words down exactly and keep her opinions out of the transcript.

Under the smell of coffee and copier heat, the man smelled afraid.

Not nervous. June knew nervous witnesses. They dried their mouths with paper cups and answered questions that had not been asked. This was colder and sharper, with something metallic under it that reminded her of the Hall after two men had carried in a broken folding table.

Recent violence.

Her hands missed a stroke.

"Correction," June said. "The witness said October seventh."

The examining attorney glanced at her. "That's what I have."

"Then the correction is mine. Proceed."

She found her place. Across the table, the witness's fear changed because she had spoken. It did not lessen. It aimed.

June kept her eyes on the paper feed.

The questioner moved to invoices. Three exhibits, two objections, a dispute about pallet counts. June took all of it cleanly while the other information sat in the room without a name anyone human would use. She had known things like this before. Which witness had been awake all night. Which lawyer was ill before the cough started. Which deponent had gone home and hit somebody after the last session.

She had called it instinct because instinct was a word people accepted from a woman who did not want to explain herself.

At the break, Cyd followed her into the equipment room.

"You missed a stroke."

"I corrected it."

"You don't miss strokes. You correct other people's strokes in your sleep."

Cyd had a coffee in one hand and her glasses on top of her head. She smelled like coffee, winter air, and nothing June could separate from ordinary human worry.

"Bad night," June said.

"You've had eleven bad nights. This one has a witness staring at you like you found a body in his trunk."

"Maybe he doesn't like court reporters."

"Nobody likes court reporters. That's how you know civilization is functioning."

June checked the backup recorder. The red light held.

"I need five minutes."

"You have four. Counsel wants to finish before lunch and the witness wants to become vapor."

Cyd went out. June stood alone among spare cables and boxed toner and tried to identify what had happened without improving it into something dramatic.

Fear was real. Recent violence was real. Members read both because bodies carried them. Every person at the Hall had known June was a Havlik before anyone opened the roster. She had grown up outside that room, but she had not grown up outside her own body.

The ability had not arrived with Elias. It had been present when she was twelve and knew her mother was sick before the fever. At twenty-two, when she changed subway cars because the man beside the door carried violence on him like hot pennies. In eleven hundred depositions, every time she marked an answer for review before the contradiction appeared.

The difference was that someone had finally given the evidence a name.

She returned to the conference room with a plan small enough not to ruin anybody's record.

"Back on the record," counsel said. "Before the break you testified that you did not enter the warehouse after October seventh. Is that accurate?"

"Yes."

The fear came back at once.

June lifted her hands. "Clarification for the record."

Both lawyers turned.

"The prior question used the word enter. The exhibit references access. If counsel intends those words to mean the same thing, the transcript should reflect that."

The examining attorney looked at the invoice, then at the witness.

"Fair point. Did you access the warehouse after October seventh by any means, whether or not you personally entered?"

"No."

The metallic note strengthened.

"Did you direct anyone else to enter?"

"I asked the night supervisor to retrieve a file on October ninth. I didn't consider that access."

"Who was the night supervisor?"

The witness swallowed. "The night supervisor."

Opposing counsel sat forward. "We need a break."

"We just had one."

"We need another."

June lifted her hands and stopped the machine. The witness got up too quickly and struck the table with his thigh. Fear rolled off him. The trace beneath it was unmistakable now.

She had been right once.

Once was not evidence.

They resumed twenty minutes later after a conference in the hall. The witness corrected the answer. He had sent the supervisor inside, and the supervisor had been injured when a shelving unit fell. The incident report was not among the produced exhibits.

Counsel asked whether he had known the shelving was unstable. He said no. The fear changed, but the trace of violence did not. June could not tell whether that made the answer false. Borne evidence was not intent. It was not testimony waiting inside somebody's skin.

The questioning reached an earlier maintenance inspection. That report was missing too. The witness said he did not know where it was, and this time his fear did not change.

June marked the answer for the ordinary reason that missing reports returned later with lawyers attached. What she smelled could guide attention. It could not testify.

June took down every word. She did not look pleased. A missing injury report could cost Cyd's agency the assignment if the transcript appeared unreliable, and a court reporter who inserted herself into testimony could lose more than one client.

At the next pause, the examining attorney pushed an exhibit toward June.

"Mark this as twelve."

His cuff shifted. Illness came off him, faint and sour beneath starch and soap.

June marked the exhibit, then said, "Counsel, the room is booked until four. If you need a short recess before the next section, this would be the clean place."

He frowned. "Why would I?"

"You asked for water twice and haven't touched it."

He stared at the glass. Then he put a hand flat against his upper abdomen.

"Ten minutes," he said. "Off the record."

In the hall, he was sick into a wastebasket.

Twice.

She tested the limit while counsel washed his face. The witness remained with his lawyer. June could smell fear through the open door, but not whose. Two bodies, one signal, no attribution she could prove.

She wrote three lines in the notebook she used for equipment failures. Proximity mattered. Crowds blurred. Words still had to do the separating.

It was less like acquiring a weapon than discovering she had spent years operating a machine without knowing one of its keys worked. That made it useful. It also meant she had pressed it before.

A new name did not make her infallible. It made her responsible for the difference between a fact she read and an assumption she supplied.

Before they resumed, June asked the witness whether he needed more distance from his attorney for the next answer. It was an ordinary scheduling question. His fear eased when the attorney stepped into the corridor.

"I want to correct something else," he said.

June put her hands on the keys.

He admitted the earlier inspection had recommended taking the shelving out of service. He had delayed because the warehouse was behind schedule. The violence June smelled came from the injury; the fear came from the decision before it. Scent had not supplied that distinction. Procedure had.

She read the correction back. The witness approved it. Words completed what the body could only point toward.

June marked the sequence in her private notebook: perception, question, correction. Never accusation without the middle step. A literacy required grammar before it could become evidence.

June stayed in her chair and rewound the disputed answer through her headphones. Words first. Body second. Neither replaced the other. One told her what could be proved. The other told her where to ask.

Cyd came in and closed the door.

"What did you do?"

"I clarified a term."

"You cracked the case open with a vocabulary question."

"That's what vocabulary questions are for."

"And you knew Larkin was going to throw up."

"He looked pale."

"He always looks pale. He bills by the hour in a room with no windows."

June packed the paper notes into her case. "Did we keep the assignment?"

"We kept it. They're adding the supervisor tomorrow. Sondra's covering because you were supposed to be at Hanley."

"Good."

"It is not good. It is billable, which is different." Cyd lowered her voice. "You promised me an explanation."

"Tonight."

"You said tomorrow yesterday."

"Then I'm improving the deadline."

Cyd's mouth tightened. She wanted to believe her. June could read that only because she had known her nine years, which suddenly felt cleaner than scent and much more dangerous.

There was a knock.

Cyd opened the door before June could answer. Elias stood in the corridor in a dark suit, far enough from the window at the end that the weak winter sun did not reach him.

"Miss Havlik," he said. "I need to speak with you about Tess Renn."

The pull under June's sternum found him immediately. She let it register and then, deliberately, attended to everything else.

No illness. No recent violence. Fear, held so tightly it had become nearly still.

And desire.

It was not vague. It was not the attachment pointing. It was his body answering the sight of her while his face remained composed and his hands stayed at his sides.

Elias said, "The matter is entirely professional."

June looked at him with the attention she had used on the witness.

What she read was not what he had been saying.

"Professional first," she said. "Tess used my standing to request three records. I authorized those three and nothing else."

The fear in him eased by a degree. The desire did not.

"Pratt received the requests with your recorded limits attached," Elias said. "The Trust will honor them. I required a further condition: no petition settled with my cooperation may act upon you without your knowledge."

"Knowledge isn't consent."

"No. Your refusal needs no assistance from me. Knowledge merely gives you time to use it."

Cyd remained in the equipment-room doorway. "Does anybody want to explain why an archive request requires a man dressed for a funeral to visit my office?"

"A private contract dispute," June said. It was true and nowhere near enough. "He came to confirm that nobody used my name beyond what I authorized."

Elias looked at her. He could not contradict the sentence. He also could not make it complete without her permission.

"That is why I came," he said.

June turned back to him. "Anything else about Tess?"

"Her evidence begins with her sister and eleven years of research. Her conclusions are not evidence. Keep those separate."

"I already do."

"I know."

His gaze dropped to the case at her feet, then returned to her face. "Clause One has been entered satisfied. The interval before Clause Two has begun. And the first red date is nineteen days away."

There it was: the next thing that could reach her body while the institutions argued about paper.

June picked up the rejected safe-room forms she had brought to work and held them against her chest.

"Professional enough," she said. "You can go."

He did. The pull followed him down the corridor. The fear stayed with her.
