# Episode Twenty: Useful

Episode Twenty: Useful

Kearse placed an unsigned concession before June and said it became real only after she supplied one fact about Elias.

"One scheduling fact," June said. "Identified before disclosure. Those were the terms."

"These are the terms."

They sat in a rival trust's library across the river, after sunset, with two human witnesses at a desk beyond the open doors. The room was smaller than Sewickley and warmer by several degrees. Nothing looked inherited by accident.

Aloysius Pratt occupied the chair beside June with a legal pad held against his knees. June had asked him to authenticate Kearse's filings, not advise the bargain. He remained the Marrow Trust's attorney of record, a conflict nobody in the room could mistake for neutrality.

"Did the Trust send you?" June asked.

"No. It knows I was asked to inspect rival filings. It did not instruct me to attend."

"Read the concession," June said.

Kearse did. His trust would grant her access to three filed objections, notify her before asserting standing under the Articles, and refrain from using information she provided to petition against her residence or employment.

"Employment was not on the bridge draft," June said.

"I improved it."

"Why?"

"Because somebody is calling your clients and I am not. I prefer my innocence documented before you become influential."

"That's almost considerate."

"Consideration is the foundation of contract."

Pratt said, "Human contract. We should preserve the distinction."

Kearse inclined his head. "Thank you, Mr. Pratt."

June read the requested fact: the location and interval during which Elias Marrow may be served without the presence of another Marrow trustee.

"That's not scheduling," she said. "That's access."

"Access governed by a schedule."

"No."

Kearse poured tea for three people and drank none himself.

"Then we have reached the educational portion. You need my objection to surety before clause two settles on your cousin. I need an opportunity to ask Mr. Marrow a question he would otherwise decline. Neither need is shameful."

"What question?"

"Not part of the identified fact."

"Then no bargain."

June struck the requested sentence. "If you want a location, warrant that the information is not already available through reasonable search."

"Reasonable to whom?"

"Counsel with access to public service records."

Pratt said, "That definition excludes private movement reports and house schedules."

"Good."

Kearse accepted the warranty. June added that the fact could be used for one service only, disclosed to no assignee, and retained under confidential treatment if the concession proved unusable.

"Knowledge cannot be returned," Pratt said.

"Treatment can."

"Then say that."

She did.

Kearse watched without impatience. "You are building human contract around lineage injury."

"Human contract is the part where I can sue you without asking what the judge's clerk believes a record is."

"A practical preference."

"The best kind."

Pratt found one contradiction between advance notice and confidential service. June made notice simultaneous with service. It gave her no time to prevent the act, but it preserved proof if the information traveled beyond the one use.

"You could walk away," Pratt said.

"And clause two reaches Milo without your objection in my hand."

"That is pressure."

"Yes. It is not compulsion. I can still say no."

She stated that for the witnesses and for herself.

Pratt cleared his throat. "A location does not disclose the question or authorize the compulsion. It is information about ordinary presence."

June turned to him. "Would you give it?"

"I am not the party asked."

"That wasn't my question."

He considered. "No. I would not. But I have wanted access to Mr. Kearse's objections for twenty-two years and understand why someone might."

Kearse looked pleased with both answers.

June narrowed the sentence. One location. One forty-minute interval. No information about route, transport, daylight shelter, or any person accompanying Elias. Kearse accepted each cut.

The remaining fact appeared harmless: Elias would retrieve the clause file from Fourth Avenue alone between eleven and eleven forty that night.

It cost June nothing. The schedule appeared only in Pratt's private working service file, which he had shown her while identifying papers already served. It was unavailable through public search. If Kearse wanted it badly enough to trade three objections, he might know less than he claimed.

That was how she justified the signature.

Before signing, June called Elias. The call went to voicemail.

"Kearse wants information concerning your exposure," she said. "I narrowed it to one procedural scheduling fact. I have not agreed. Call me before eleven."

The warning satisfied no term and arrived late. It still prevented June from pretending there had been no route to ask.

Kearse said, "If he advises against the trade, will you decline?"

"I will decide after hearing him."

"And if he does not answer?"

June looked at the clock. Waiting could lose the objection and leave Milo exposed.

"Then I decide with the evidence I have."

At ten fifty-seven, no call had come. June signed.

Kearse signed the concession. Both witnesses stated that they had seen it. Pratt read the operative promises aloud and handed June the first objection.

It concerned the definition of line.

The rival trust had argued, decades earlier, that written appointment could not create borne kinship and borne kinship could not by itself create an office. The petition failed, but the denial admitted the two were separate acts.

June made Pratt read the denial into the witnesses' hearing. Then she read Kearse's concession again and added a receipt listing every page she had been given. Kearse signed that too.

"You expect me to remove something after delivery," he said.

"I expect people to remember a generous stack and disagree about what was in it."

"That is nearly insulting."

"Nearly is doing useful work."

The second objection concerned notice. The third challenged a trustee's attempt to treat a member's presence as agreement. Both had failed, but each forced the deciding office to say what the Articles did not mean. Losing arguments had built a fence around the clauses. June could use the fence.

"Why give me all three?" she asked.

"Because a rival who cannot survive is merely a vacancy. I need you capable of making choices."

"Choices that weaken the Marrows."

"Ideally. But genuine choice includes the tiresome possibility that you disappoint me."

Pratt finished comparing seals. "All three are authentic filings. None has been superseded in the public register."

June stored them in separate sleeves. She had become useful, just as Nadia advised. The practical result felt like relief. The moral result had not arrived yet.

She tested the first objection against clause two while Kearse watched. If line came from the Hall's borne record and office from the Trust's written one, a surety required two distinct acts. Kinship could identify Milo. It could not appoint him. A written appointment could create office. It could not manufacture kinship.

"Forty-eight hours," Pratt said. "At minimum. A dispute between accepted definitions stays execution until both records answer."

"Can it keep him out entirely?"

"If no valid appointment occurs."

"Milo volunteered."

"Volunteer language may become appointment if repeated after consequences are stated."

June wrote the danger beside the argument. The concession had bought a real defense, not archival curiosity.

Kearse said, "That is what usefulness looks like. Someone else knows a thing you need. You make their need for you larger than the value of withholding it."

"Nadia's bride survived that way."

"Ostrowski. She worked for my predecessor."

June stopped writing. "You knew her."

"I met her twice. She was excellent at hearing which person in a room had authority and which merely occupied the largest chair."

"Did your trust spend her?"

"It would have, had her usefulness ended before the waiver. She understood that."

The strategy was not freedom. It was controlled dependence with a favorable exit date.

"Why tell me?"

"Because admiration is dangerous when it edits precedent."

Kearse had just given June a truth against his own charm. That increased her trust by exactly enough to make him more dangerous.

June asked Pratt to certify the passage about Ostrowski if any public filing supported it. None did. Kearse offered a house ledger, then withdrew the offer when June required the woman's consent to inspect it.

"You do learn," she said.

"I calculate," he answered.

"Today those produce the same result."

She added the unavailable ledger to her notes without treating it as evidence. A true story from an interested trustee remained a lead, not precedent.

Pratt asked whether she wanted certified copies made before leaving. June did. Kearse charged no fee, which meant the concession covered paper as well as access. She made him say so. A small term named after the large one was already settled gave the witnesses a second chance to hear that the bargain had limits.

June opened the first objection again. Its failed argument had already given her forty-eight hours. Its denial might also keep Milo out entirely.

She read the denial's admission twice.

"Useful?" Kearse asked.

"Potentially."

"Then we are both satisfied."

Footsteps crossed the open library doorway behind her.

Elias entered without invitation because the building was not a dwelling. It belonged to the trust, not to a resident, a distinction June understood only when nobody stopped him at the threshold.

The attachment tightened under her sternum. His gaze found her, then the signed paper.

"Miss Havlik."

"Mr. Marrow."

Kearse rose with expansive pleasure. "Right on time."

Elias looked at the clock. Eleven twelve.

June's mouth went dry.

"Why is he here?" she asked.

"Because your fact let me serve a separately filed rival demand while he was outside the protection of another Marrow trustee. Your bargain created no claim. My trust's pending petition supplied the standing; service perfected it."

"You said one fact."

"You gave one. I used it."

Elias did not ask how Kearse had known. He was looking at June, and what she could read on him made explanation worse: fear, no surprise.

He had expected her to become useful to someone. He had warned her what usefulness cost.

Kearse invited the witnesses closer. "Before we begin, the consideration must be acknowledged."

"The agreement is complete," June said. "You don't need to name it again."

"Not for validity. For courtesy."

He addressed the room.

"Let it be witnessed that June Havlik supplied the location and vulnerable interval of Elias Marrow, enabling service that could not otherwise have been made. In return, my trust granted the concessions already read."

The same scheduling sentence sounded different with Elias standing inside it.

Kearse smiled at June.

"Thank you for delivering him."
