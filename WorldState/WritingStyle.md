# The Half-Spoken Vow — Writing Style Guide

**Status:** Locked, v1.2.0 — 2026-08-19
**Derived from:** the genre research synthesis (`docs/genre-research-notes.md`), the production bible's
voice signatures, and the drafted pilot `Assets/Book One/Episodes/B1E01-On-The-Record.md`.
**Authority:** subordinate to `WorldState/BookCodex.json` and `AGENTS.md`. Where this file and canon
disagree, canon wins. This governs *how* it is written, never *what* is true.

**v1.1.0 revision note:** four rules that had been written as timetables were restated as effects.
Interiority, countable anchors, and verbatim read-backs are all genuine strengths of this series and
all three were phrased in a way that would have become audible as a metronome across hundreds of
episodes. The underlying discipline is unchanged and in places is stated more strictly; what was
removed is the schedule. The guiding rule for this document: **state the required effect strongly, and
do not mandate a particular instrument on a predictable cadence unless canon or continuity requires
it.** A long serial should have a recognizable grammar without exposing its metronome.

---

## 1. The register in one paragraph

Procedural, dry, materially specific. This is a legal-gothic serial about a woman who is paid to
listen, and the prose should sound like it was written by someone who notices wording for a living.
Sentences are plain and load-bearing. The supernatural is delivered in the same flat, competent voice
as the paperwork, and is more frightening for it. Nothing swoons. Nothing is purple. The horror and
the romance both arrive as *facts*, stated at the same temperature as a filing deadline.

**The single most useful instruction:** write with the evidentiary discipline June brings to a
transcript. Exact wording matters. Observable fact precedes interpretation. Implication is allowed to
stay implication. The prose does not tell the listener what the evidence has already demonstrated, and
it lets them feel what the narration is refusing to say.

This is a discipline, not a voice. It governs every viewpoint in the series; it does not make every
viewpoint sound like June. Elias's narration applies the same rigor to precedent, institutions, and
the weight of long memory, and reaches quite different conclusions in quite different words. Each POV
keeps the perceptual and verbal character defined in sections two and seven.

## 2. POV and tense

- Third person limited, past tense, always.
- One POV per episode wherever practical. Where it changes, change only at a clean scene break, never
  mid-scene, never twice in one episode.
- Establish a new viewpoint naturally in the first sentence or two — through what that character
  notices, wants, or is doing. **Never** a name-header, a location slug, or any mechanical announcement.
- One narrator performs everything. Never write an effect that needs two performers to land.
- June's narration knows procedure, wording, money, and bodies. It does not know politics, history, or
  what anyone else is feeling. Elias's narration knows institutions, precedent, and the weight of long
  memory, and refuses to examine its own motives.

## 3. Sentence craft

- **One breath per sentence.** If a narrator needs a second breath, split it. Semicolon chains fail here.
- Prefer the plain verb. "He said" over "he intoned." Attribution is invisible; adverbs on attribution
  are a smell.
- Vary length deliberately: a run of medium sentences, then a short one that lands. The short one is
  the beat. Do not use three in a row or the effect dies.
- Concrete nouns over abstractions. A number, a price, a material, a piece of furniture.
- No sentence exists only to be beautiful. Every image does narrative work — the room's temperature
  and the enemy's weakness in the same clause.

## 4. Interiority budget

**The typical profile:** interiority runs somewhere near nine percent of an episode, usually in three
or four short hits of roughly fifteen to forty words.

Read that as a description of what this series normally sounds like, and as a diagnostic for catching
drift — not as a quota. **The governing principle is dramatic function, never arithmetic.** An episode
whose work genuinely requires more will run higher; an episode that is all pressure and procedure will
run lower, and both are correct. Never add a line of interiority to reach a percentage, and never cut
one that is doing real work because an episode is running rich.

What does not flex is the shape:

- Every interior beat is *triggered by something happening in the room*. She notices what his hands are
  doing, then one line about what that costs. Never the reverse, and never unprompted rumination.
- **No standalone description paragraphs and no interior essays.** Setting arrives as a character's
  grievance, threat, advantage, or expense. Action and external stimulus stay primary.
- One striking physical detail per character per scene, tied to a feeling, or none at all.
- The far end of the range is real and worth protecting: roughly once per ten-episode arc, in an episode
  that is otherwise a pure two-hander, a "what these years did to me" passage can carry a much larger
  share. Earn it with the scene, not with a schedule.

## 5. Openings

- **Concrete pressure inside the first forty spoken words.** The pilot spends its first sentence on a
  woman calculating that she will not make rent.
- No prologue, no weather, no cosmology, no room description, no recap.
- Open on the danger, never on longing. Emotion arrives because the danger moved.
- Name the stake and the injury before any proper noun the listener does not already know.

## 6. Endings

Every episode ends on a **state change** — a status, standing, obligation, or fact that is different
than it was. Tonal variety is retained; the floor does not move.

Distribution across any twelve episodes: about four hard event cliffs, five decision buttons, two
dread-deferrals, one warmth beat. Place the warmth beat immediately before an arc's worst turn.

- Escalate through the final two hundred to three hundred words toward the mapped hook and stop sharply.
- End on a line that can be *said*. The best buttons in this series are two or three words of dialogue.
- Never end on interiority. End on a decision, a knock, an obligation, a name, or a sentence somebody
  says out loud.

## 7. Dialogue

- **Asymmetric turn length is the voice tag.** Give the powerful party four words and the pressured
  party forty. The ear identifies a speaker by rhythm before timbre.
- Each principal has a fixed verbal signature; see `AudioProductionBible.json` → `voice_distinction_guide`.
  June quotes people back at themselves. Elias uses no contractions and answers only the literal
  question. Marta says the hard thing first and puts a number in it. Constance asks permission she does
  not need. Cyd interrupts herself. Tess states her own motives unprompted. Milo starts over mid-sentence.
- Name the *tone* of an interruption rather than relying on a dash. In audio a bare cut-off is confusing.
- Alternate a line of dialogue with a physical beat, so the narrator has somewhere to change voice and
  the listener has somewhere to re-anchor.
- Humor is structural oxygen: mock the staging, never the threat. Comedy between twenty and seventy
  percent of an episode, never at ninety-five. A laugh releases tension and an episode must end holding it.

## 8. Audio-native reveals

Every essential reveal reaches the listener as **sound**. Rotate the channel; never repeat one twice
running.

The house channels: a document read into a record and then read back verbatim; an overheard argument
through a door or in a stairwell; a voicemail or speakerphone call in company; a bound ledger read
aloud with the crucial fact arriving as an *absence* somebody must say out loud; a vote called name by
name; a price quoted in full before any work is done; a sound at a threshold that forces a decision.

**The read-back is the series' signature.** A passage spoken once and then repeated exactly, in a
different voice or the same one. Use it for menace, for proof, and for intimacy.

Never let a silent visual carry a beat alone.

## 9. Worldbuilding discipline

- **At most one new coined term per episode**, and never in the same episode as a new named character.
- Deliver every rule as somebody's **bill or grievance** — a price, a shipment, a rumor, a deadline, a
  person she dislikes. Never as a lecture, never as history.
- Institutions are shown as pressure before they are named. In the pilot, Marta is "a heavy woman in a
  windbreaker" for the entire episode and no lineage is named once.
- **Countable anchors are a signature strength — use them wherever the material supplies them.** Nine
  clauses. Nineteen days. Three hundred and eleven members. Nine hundred dollars. This world runs on
  clause numbers, money, dates, deadlines, votes, memberships and obligations, so most episodes will
  offer the listener something concrete and repeatable without being asked. Take it when it arises.
  **Never invent a number, price, deadline, or count solely to satisfy the pattern.** The purpose is
  listener orientation and retention, not numerical decoration, and a figure with no dramatic business
  in the scene is worse than none.
- **Keep standing threats and obligations alive, and never through expository summary.** Refresh them
  by whatever mechanism the scene actually wants: a read-back, a line of dialogue, an action taken
  because of them, a consequence landing, a character misremembering the wording, or a document being
  invoked against someone. What is prohibited is the clumsy catch-up paragraph, not the absence of a
  quotation.
- **Reserve verbatim repetition for when the repetition itself means something.** Exact wording carries
  unusual force in a world built on records, testimony and witnessed language, and that force is spent
  if it becomes a scheduled tic. Quote exactly when the exactness is the point — when a phrase is being
  held against someone, proved, weaponized, or discovered to have been altered. Do not quote exactly
  because an episode is due for one.

## 10. The two lineages must sound different

Apply the swap test to prose, not just plot. Member scenes run on presence, bodies, and what cannot be
concealed — people are in a room together and lying is not available. Trustee scenes run on documents,
filings, and what is on the page — nobody trusts a voice. If a scene would read the same with the roles
exchanged, or with the wolf side replaced by an ordinary union, rewrite it.

## 11. Intimacy

`docs/sex-sensuallity-policy.md` is authoritative. In prose terms:

- Live in charged non-contact. Peak at act-present-with-anatomy-displaced. **Transition through** the
  act rather than narrating it.
- Attention goes to temperature, breath, the sound the other person makes, the specific place a touch
  lands. Never geometry.
- Apply the **read-it-aloud test**: if a phrase becomes awkwardly anatomical when spoken by one
  narrator performing both parties, rewrite or elide it.
- Consent is affirmative, audible, and revocable, every time. The bond intensifies feeling; it never
  supplies permission, and where it pulls hardest the characters say so out loud.
- Heat escalates across seasons. Do not spend the strongest card in the runway.

## 12. Cruelty

Darkness must carry a bill. Cruelty costs the person committing it; the relationship is built before it
is broken; it is a choice between goods rather than a display of appetite; aftermath is small and
specific rather than escalating; the victim keeps agency inside it; and **the narration never enjoys it
more than the character does**. Move fast and flinching through the act, slow afterward.

## 13. TTS normalization — write it normalized the first time

The upload master is read aloud verbatim, so draft in a form that can be pasted without a cleanup pass.

- Spell out all numerals, ordinals, decimals, clock times, dates, measurements and units.
- Replace symbols and abbreviations with words.
- No all-caps display text; render it as speech or as narrated description.
- No Markdown in the body, no speaker labels, no production notes, no bracketed sound cues.
- File convention: `Assets/Book One/Episodes/B1E##-Title-In-Kebab-Case.md`, opening with the heading
  `# Episode One: The Title`, then the title repeated as the first plain-text line of the body, one
  paragraph break, and straight into the cold open with no recap.
- This series is dense with citations, dates, dollar amounts, headcounts and clause numbers, and those
  countables are a deliberate device. A mis-spoken number damages the one thing the listener is tracking.

## 14. Length

1,650 to 1,800 spoken words per episode; hard floor 1,000. One scene, three to four beats, one
location, one time of day, at most one location change, no more than six foreground voices and
preferably two or three when the scene's work is between two people.

## 15. Standing prohibitions

No prologues. No dream sequences. No retrospective narrator who guarantees survival. No mid-scene
head-hopping. No character whose identity depends on typography. No mood chapters where the plot
pauses. No lecture, no infodump, no encyclopedia. No cliffhanger that is not paid. No episode in which
June is only acted upon. And nothing from `docs/originality-blacklist.md`, ever.

## 16. Prose integrity — the editor's pass

Run this before any episode is called done. These are the failure modes that survive a first draft
because they *sound* like prose, and every one of them has been caught in this project at least once.

- **Self-canceling sentences.** A clause that promises a contrast and then delivers none. The real
  example: *"nobody in the room even looked up. Except the woman in the windbreaker, who did not look up
  either."* The word "except" sets up a turn that never arrives, and the reader stalls hunting for it. If
  a sentence contains *except*, *but*, *although*, or *however*, confirm the thing after it actually
  differs from the thing before it.
- **Fragment stacking.** One or two deliberate fragments land. Three in a row is a tic, and after a long
  sentence it reads as a mannerism rather than emphasis.
- **Restatement disguised as emphasis.** Saying the same thing a second time in different words is not a
  beat. Cut the weaker one.
- **Characters explaining what they already know.** If two people in a room both know a fact, they do not
  recite it to each other for the audience's benefit. Find someone who does not know, or let the audience
  infer.
- **Repetitive dramatic beats.** Two episodes where the same character learns the same kind of thing the
  same way. Vary the channel and vary who is surprised.
- **Cute identifiers.** A descriptive tag used once is dry; used three or four times it becomes authored.
  Watch anything of the form "the [adjective] [role]."
- **Accidental echoes.** The same distinctive number, image, or phrase doing unrelated work twice in one
  episode dilutes whichever use matters. If a figure is a deliberate refrain, protect it by changing the
  other one.
- **Prose written to reach a word count.** The most detectable flaw in the entire list. If a passage was
  added for length it will be the passage where nothing changes. Cut it and find a real beat instead.
- **AI-shaped rhetoric.** Tricolons in the abstract ("not X, not Y, but Z"), sentences that open by
  announcing their own significance, and the reflexive "it wasn't just A, it was B." All of these read as
  filler because they contain no information.
- **Unearned interiority.** A character having a realization the scene has not set up. If the insight
  arrives without a trigger in the room, it belongs to the author, not the character.

**The test:** every sentence should survive a human editor asking *what does this one do that the last
one didn't?* If there is no answer, it goes.
