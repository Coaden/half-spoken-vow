# Genre Research Notes — *The Half-Spoken Vow*

**Status:** working document, not a deliverable. Assembled from a structured read of 18 novels and
3 sourcebooks, 2026-08-18. Full per-reader notes are in the session scratchpad; the collision
census is consolidated in `docs/originality-blacklist.md`; approved decisions are in
`docs/locked-decisions.md`.

**Purpose:** a decision substrate. Nearly every choice in the codex's `story_engines` and
`canon_rules` should trace back to a line in this file.

Reading method: openings (15–20 pages), one mid-book section, and the ending where reachable —
enough to extract hook mechanics, POV and tense, chapter length, escalation cadence, and how
romantic tension is sustained rather than resolved. Not read end to end.

---

## Tier A — load-bearing

### Dark Lover — J.R. Ward
- **KEEP:** The roster tableau — one scene marches six warriors past the audience, each given a single unhealed wound plus one unexercised appetite, none addressed in this book. Plus the communal-house ending that converts loners into a permanent ensemble with a dinner table.
- **AVOID:** ~2,160-word chapters with hooks only at chapter ends; tension discharged in graphic sex by the 40% mark; the onomastic gimmick (decorative silent letters), which reads as a wink on the page and a stutter in the ear.
- **ADAPT:** The dying man's bequest that binds the hero to the heroine before he wants her — promote it from a one-time device into a standing institution: a lineage-obligation registry any episode can draw a writ from.
- **WHY:** Book one's job in a long series is not to close a story but to leave visible, itemized wounds on secondary characters and put them all in one building.

### The Warlord Wants Forever — Kresley Cole
- **KEEP:** The structural centerpiece of the corpus. Combinatorial faction ecology — 5–8 factions each holding a specific historical grievance, plus an internal schism in the largest, so *n* factions yield *n(n−1)/2* hostilities nobody had to author. Plus vow-law (an oath in a specified form physically compels, so dialogue becomes plot) and the offstage countdown (an imprisoned absolute power, never on stage, invocable as pressure forever).
- **AVOID:** Compulsion-as-eroticism as the load-bearing intimacy mechanic; anachronistic snark that collapses the mythic frame when read aloud at length; 13 chapters for 37k words — too few pivots.
- **ADAPT:** The liability heirloom — an object that is inherited punishment, fate-detector, hostage, *and* transferable, so the succession dispute is a free arc. Change its effect from sexual command to compelled truth or compelled proximity. Also her blood-memory download: a diegetic flashback delivery system with a built-in per-episode cliffhanger.
- **WHY:** Conflict becomes combinatorial rather than authored — any two characters in a room already have a reason to fight and a reason not to marry, which is what a 300-episode serial needs at episode 180.

### Dark Prince — Christine Feehan
- **KEEP:** The telepathic voice-first meeting — two minds talking in the dark before either has a body, the most audio-native hook in the corpus. Plus the species-level crisis that makes the romance load-bearing for the plot, and sensory-somatic prose over visual detail.
- **AVOID:** Compulsion as a flirting mechanic (a mental push to knock her out, a remote assault on her sleeping body, a command that inflicts pain on resistance — all framed as courtship); memory-wiping, which destroys stakes; a hero who cannot be refused; and resolving the bond in chapter four, leaving three quarters of the book on external plot.
- **ADAPT:** The ritual words — magnificent spoken liturgy, and also the book's worst coercion because his speech binds her without her knowledge. **Make it a duet:** his half binds only him. This became our central mechanic.
- **WHY:** A single-volume fantasy where his omnipotence and her helplessness *are* the fantasy; we need a bride who can walk away and keeps choosing not to, so every mechanic that removes her exit must be replaced by one that raises the cost of leaving.

### Cry Wolf — Patricia Briggs
- **KEEP:** Our ethical baseline. A supernatural bond explicitly **incomplete without consent** — the bodies bonded on sight, the people have not, and it will not finish until they do. Plus a hero who actively offers her the exit against his own interest; micro-consent staged as physical blocking; and dominance framed as *who protects*, so hierarchy generates constant friction with zero sanctioned harm.
- **AVOID:** A prologue in a stranger's head; dense wilderness procedural a listener cannot map; quiet chapter ends (only two of fifteen carry a real hook); deep dependency on a companion series; and a climax so understated the bond completing gets one clause.
- **ADAPT:** The two-tier bond — a body-layer arriving uninvited, a person-layer that must be spoken. Also her rank that sits *outside* the hierarchy and therefore cannot be commanded: keep the function, discard the name and the framing entirely.
- **WHY:** Proof you can keep the animal certainty, the possessive growl, and fated recognition while the heroine still holds the veto — and the veto *is* the plot, because it is the only thing that permits indefinite renegotiation.

### Halfway to the Grave — Jeaniene Frost
- **KEEP:** Banter that survives being read aloud. Asymmetric turn length as the voice tag (four words against forty), one contested nickname per pair, insult-as-affection where the target *laughs* rather than retaliating, and interruptions named by tone rather than marked by an em-dash.
- **AVOID:** ~4,000-word chapters; resolving the central romantic obstacle mid-book.
- **ADAPT:** Her charged non-contact register — heat from threat, scent, proximity, and a deliberate refusal to touch — as our default, since it is fully performable by one narrator.
- **WHY:** With no face to watch and one voice performing everyone, dialogue rhythm is how a listener tells characters apart.

### Court of the Vampire Queen — Katee Robert
- **KEEP:** The stated-transaction spine — obligations itemized up front; the bond both parties resent; a political clock; debt-for-victory; short chapters ending on a quotable line.
- **AVOID:** **Its consent model, entirely.** The heroine is handed over under threat, hesitates, is continued past, and her arousal is a property of the bite rather than her own desire; the front matter self-labels as dubious consent. Also its explicitness frequency, which a single narrator cannot carry without the serial becoming erotica.
- **ADAPT:** Invert the one useful piece — a spoken formula recurring as an audio device — into something requiring real, refusable consent to function.
- **WHY:** Closest book in the corpus to our premise, and therefore the clearest map of the specific ditch to avoid.

### Vampire's Bride — Gena Showalter
- **KEEP:** Intimate scenes carried by very short imperative dialogue, two to four words a turn, alternating — performable as rhythm and breath rather than narrated anatomy.
- **AVOID:** An opening atrocity so total the origin wound has nowhere left to escalate, and which a lone narrator would have to perform in character.
- **ADAPT:** The dead bride who still occupies the office, so the living woman is an interloper — predecessors' fates as running dread, their survivors as a faction. Withhold the predecessor's fate and reveal it through unreliable reporting: a wound that worsens as it is understood beats one detonated in episode one.
- **WHY:** Nearest thing in the corpus to the literal bride premise, and it shows how to make "bride" an office rather than an event.

### The Vampire Chronicles — Anne Rice
- **KEEP:** What immortality *feels* like. Grief that goes cold and stays in the room; memory that corrupts itself; "forever" as a unit of measurement rather than a promise; boredom presenting as exquisite courtesy; condescension that is actuarial rather than contemptuous; and the deepest lever — the very old starve for a *witness*, not for blood.
- **AVOID:** Her pacing, completely. The *Interview* opening spends ~300 words on a room before any want is stated — two minutes of an eleven-minute episode. Scenes whose whole content is a mood shift; digression as principle; nested narrators; sentences no narrator can say in one breath; a protagonist whose defining act is declining to act.
- **ADAPT:** The confessional frame, with inverted economics — she uses it to license digression; ours would need a countdown, one question answered per episode, each answer worsening the speaker's position.
- **WHY:** The only writer here who knows what four centuries of memory does to the capacity to love, and the one whose scene rhythm would kill an eleven-minute episode fastest. Quarantined to the interior layer, kept away from structure.

## Tier B

### Glass Houses — Rachel Caine
- **KEEP:** Strategically the most important entry. Her *scene* — 820–2,200 words, three beats, one location, two to four speakers — is almost exactly our episode. First sentence names the arc and delivers a concrete injury in fourteen words, with no vampire anywhere in chapter one. Dread kept continuous by a standing rule with a clock, a countable price, threats that persist as re-quoted refrains, and sanctuary with a loophole.
- **AVOID:** Ending the book mid-stab with a brand-new antagonist introduced in the last 948 words; resolving the whole political conflict in one long sit-down with the sovereign.
- **ADAPT:** Her chapter-end distribution — only about a third are literal cliffhangers, the rest decision buttons, dread-deferrals, and one deliberate warmth beat placed just before the worst turn.
- **WHY:** The only book in the library whose native unit is already under 2,000 words.

### Full Moon Rising — Keri Arthur
- **KEEP:** Dual heritage built on *partial* inheritance rather than doubled power — each half fails at the worst moment, and her mixed blood is simultaneously her deepest shame and her only job qualification.
- **AVOID:** 6,107-word chapters, eight of them a single unbroken scene; venting the biological compulsion that is supposed to drive the serial; six unfamiliar coined nouns inside the first 1,400 words.
- **ADAPT:** The recurring involuntary clock — but pointed at a prohibition, so it generates a crisis every arc instead of being spent.
- **WHY:** The only author here who engineered wolf rules and vampire rules to conflict *inside one body*, which is literally our premise. Source of the lineage-swap test now in canon.

### Some Girls Bite — Chloe Neill
- **KEEP:** The outsider-entry rig: the inciting act is already done before page one; rules are delivered by a functionary *with an agenda* and every rule costs her something inside the same scene; and she contests each rule as it lands, so etiquette and tyranny arrive together in two lines of dialogue.
- **AVOID:** Fifteen coined institutional nouns plus a twelve-house roster recited as a list; a retrospective narrator, which pre-defuses every peril by guaranteeing survival; 6,646-word chapters split just once.
- **ADAPT:** Her induction ceremony, which works because it is entirely voice and gesture — a template for our wedding-treaty rite. And convert her physical rulebook into an *oral* instrument: a formula that must be said correctly or the contract fails.
- **WHY:** Our bride enters someone else's court, so the audience must learn the politics only as fast as she does.

### Dead Until Dark — Charlaine Harris
- **KEEP:** Mythology delivered *only* as gossip, grievance, and shop-talk — every rule of the world attached to a price, a shipment, a rumor, or a person she dislikes. Plus a gift and a social wound that are the same object, so ordinary life is already supernatural pressure.
- **AVOID:** Chapter ends on quiet emotional beats and closed doors; 7,700-word chapters; a single-POV lock that forces offstage plot to arrive as report.
- **ADAPT:** Her murder spine as a *rolling* one — a crime that indicts the romance itself (victims who consorted across the lineage line), reset every ~25 episodes.
- **WHY:** A listener holds a polity in the same faculty used for real gossip about real coworkers. A nine-rank hierarchy is unrecoverable at 1.5× on a commute; "the aunt who still hasn't returned the wedding silver" is unforgettable.

### Undead and Unwed — MaryJanice Davidson
- **KEEP:** Humor as structural oxygen, and the only chapter length in the corpus already at episode scale (1,000–1,900 words through the first half). The governing rule: **deflate the props, never the threat** — mock the staging, fear the involuntary. Plus the reluctant-title engine, where the *refusal itself* is the plot and every faction must keep courting and coercing.
- **AVOID:** A consort acquired by fine print — funny on the page, and over 300 intimate audio episodes it plays as the hero tricking her into marriage. Also mid-book chapter bloat, and a villain who is only ever ridiculous and can therefore never frighten anyone again.
- **ADAPT:** Two-layer naming — public honorific spoken, private demotion in narration. Costs nothing, works only in first person, and the narrator's tone does the joke.
- **WHY:** A series this long suffocates without laughs; and her reluctant queen is the mechanism that lets a bride say no for hundreds of episodes without the story stalling.

### Touch of Evil — C.T. Adams & Cathy Clamp
- **KEEP:** Faction opposition that cannot be settled by a duel because both sides are demographically dependent on the same scarce women, and neither can pay in the other's currency. Plus rules-with-loopholes: a negotiable code generates far more scenes than a war does.
- **AVOID:** Clinical parasitology delivered as a remembered lecture, which makes the vampires disgusting rather than desirable; ten near-interchangeable male protectors; a mass-casualty climax that clears the local board in one night.
- **ADAPT:** A polity where the mating unit is a *separate governed institution* from the war-band — so the marriage is a public office with a payroll and a rival claimant. Take the shape; invent every word.
- **WHY:** Our premise is a woman treated as a treaty clause, and this is the only version of the antagonism that structurally cannot resolve.

## Tier C

### The Hamilton paired study — *Guilty Pleasures* (book 1) vs *Slay* (book 30)
- **KEEP:** From book one, a protagonist defined by a profession with a price list and a rule she states aloud, so the world's physics arrive as operating procedure; every chapter has a job in it; the villain dies on-page by her own hand. From book thirty, one thing only: domestic humiliation scales forever and costs nothing — its best material is a woman in the wrong shoes at an airport dreading her father.
- **AVOID:** The measured decay. Distinct names in chapter one **5 → 15**; mean per chapter **6.2 → 10.4**, peaking at 25 in under 3,000 words; top-five share of name-mentions **55% → 38%**. The protagonist becomes a spectator at her own climax. Chapters become status reports assigning conditions to twenty characters in turn. The series' load-bearing physical law is deleted as a reward. The central romantic promise is deferred thirty books past the point where payment could feel like a reward.
- **ADAPT:** Book thirty's one good instinct — letting the protagonist be *weakened for a whole book* — implemented properly: power failures on a schedule the audience knows, so vulnerability is a recurring appointment rather than a plot emergency.
- **WHY:** A photograph of exactly where a growing court lands in year twenty if nobody enforces caps. Source of the durability rules in `docs/locked-decisions.md` §5.

### Inheritance — Nora Roberts
- **KEEP:** Payoff metabolism. An 800-word prologue establishes premise, murder, curse, engine, and hook. Chapter one plants doubt and pays it off *inside the same chapter*, with the planted object becoming the weapon. A *middle* chapter closes three promises and opens three more. Grief is converted into physical business — packing an ex's things into bags — never into interiority. Plus a per-episode micro-payoff machine that lands every time and costs nothing.
- **AVOID:** Top five characters absorbing 69% of name-mentions — correct for a trilogy, too tight for a court with two lineages; ~4,000-word chapters; chapters that are a quiet day.
- **ADAPT:** The bounded countable collection, where each item is its own self-contained tragedy with its own victim and era. **Announce the number in episode one and never change it.** Ours must not be tokens taken from murdered brides.
- **WHY:** Proof that a bride-curse premise can be industrialized without escalation — tension from counting *down* a known quantity, never from making the monster bigger.

### Empire of the Vampire — Jay Kristoff
- **KEEP:** A cold open with the ending already implied — one sentence carrying era, stakes, protagonist, doom, and question. Antagonism in every exchange, so even "may I come in?" is a power move. Description doing double duty (the room's temperature *and* the enemy's weakness in one clause). Repeatable liturgy the audience learns and anticipates. An addiction spine that makes the protagonist *do things*. And prophecy treated with contempt by the person living inside it — the inoculation a mate-bond story badly needs.
- **AVOID:** Profanity density; ten coined nouns in one chapter; nested flashback inside a frame; a character whose identity is a font; grimdark mood chapters where the plot pauses; a seven-character POV sprawl.
- **ADAPT:** Cruelty with a bill attached. The rules: it costs the perpetrator; the relationship is built before it is broken; it is a choice between goods, not a display of appetite; aftermath is small and specific; the victim keeps agency inside it; and the narration must not enjoy it more than the character does.
- **WHY:** Kristoff, not Rice, is the prose template — immediate opposition, sensory economy, and repeatable liturgy are exactly what one voice needs to hold a listener for eleven minutes.

## Tier D — sourcebooks

*Read at structural depth: organizing chapters only, mechanics and catalogs skipped. All three PDFs
were scanned images with no text layer and had to be OCR'd first. Extraction target was the
organizing principle, stated at an altitude containing none of the source's proprietary vocabulary.*

### Core rulebook
- **KEEP:** The multi-axis character grid, and specifically that each axis pays out in a *different* currency — upbringing sets otherworld access, birth-timing sets volatility, belonging sets self-mastery. No axis dominates, so no two characters are comparable on one scale and every pairing has an asymmetry to dramatize. Plus a shared law code published *with* a per-clause note of who ignores it, and a standing ledger that records negative entries.
- **AVOID:** Thirteen factions crossed with five roles and three origins — 195 valid character types before rank. A tabletop affordance for player choice; unlistenable as an audio cast. Also its thesis that success means merely surviving.
- **ADAPT:** Keep categories small, closed, and named — closure makes politics computable and consequences predictable — but collapse the counts hard, and let a listener infer a role from behavior rather than from a glossary.
- **WHY:** Audio has no character sheet, so every category must be re-derivable from dialogue — yet an arranged marriage needs exactly this cross-cutting grid, because a match that satisfies one axis must violate another.

### Faction supplement
- **KEEP:** Faction construction from four separable pieces — a founding grievance, a virtue carried to the point of blindness, a defensible *misreading* of the shared law, and a distinct procedure for judging its own. The grievance supplies motive, the blind spot supplies how they can be manipulated, the misreading supplies the argument, and the procedure supplies a set piece. Plus the double ledger: deeds that earn standing inside a faction and would destroy it outside.
- **AVOID:** The internal fractal — five method-camps, three secret societies, rumored alliances with four outside bodies. In audio the listener cannot track who is betraying whom, and the betrayal lands as noise.
- **ADAPT:** The cell-structured conspiracy as an *information* device rather than an org chart: one convener knows the whole plot, everyone else knows exactly one link. A single narrator can voice the gap between what a character knows and what the listener knows, giving indefinite partial-knowledge suspense with nothing to memorize.
- **WHY:** Our bride is inside a lineage she did not choose, so we need a faction whose members are sympathetic and sincerely villainous at once.

### Cosmology supplement
- **KEEP:** Two things. The standardized entity record — every otherworld being documented on identical fields, the last being **its price** — which makes an unbounded population navigable and every encounter pre-negotiable. And the most valuable item in the corpus: **every boon is priced, and the price is a deed, not a resource.** Ask, be told what you owe, go do it.
- **AVOID:** The hundred-page bestiary and the gazetteer of named regions that exist only to be looked at. Every named place must be somewhere a scene happens.
- **ADAPT:** The permeability gradient — the barrier between worlds is a property of *place*, thickest where manufacture and order dominate, thinnest where the old persists — converted into a **sensory** gradient, so the listener learns where they are from how the crossing sounds and what it costs.
- **WHY:** Our canon requires a cost on every escalation, and this solved it more cleanly than we would have: the price is named up front, it is a task, and paying it is a scene.

## Synthesis

**Hook mechanics.** Concrete pressure inside the first forty spoken words, roughly fifteen seconds — Caine spends fourteen. Name the stake and the injury in sentence one, before any name the listener doesn't already know. No prologues in a stranger's head, no weather, no cosmology, no room description. Kristoff's shape is the target: one sentence carrying era, stakes, protagonist, and question. Open on the bride, in dialogue, with the marriage already at issue.

**Pacing and episode length.** The usable unit across the whole corpus is Caine's scene, not anyone's chapter: **1,450–1,650 words, three beats, one location, two to four speaking voices, no more than one location change.** Novel chapters here run 2,100–7,700 words, i.e. 2–5 episodes; treat a novel chapter's single emotional turn as the payload of a whole **mini-arc**, while each episode still carries its own micro-turn. Cap coined nouns at one new term per episode, and never introduce a new term in the same episode as a new named character.

**POV and tense.** Third person limited past, dual and bride-weighted, one POV per scene and preferably per episode. Rejected: the retrospective narrator (guarantees survival), mid-scene head-hopping (unmarked in audio), and wide ensemble rotation (how cast bloat begins).

**Heat.** Live in the charged non-contact register, peak at act-present-anatomy-displaced, and treat the fullest expression as an event affordable perhaps eight to twelve times across 300 episodes. The binding constraint is not squeamishness but the single narrator: explicit content forces one performer into three registers at once. Highest-value technique — **feeding as displaced intimacy**, which reads as intimacy, performs as breath, follows the same escalation curve, and can recur weekly without spending the consummation. Also: cut away on the line rather than the act; no intimate scene in the first forty episodes concludes inside its own episode; heat reported through a third party doubles as court plot.

**Tropes that actually pay off in serialized audio.** Obligation and vow-law, because a sentence spoken in episode 40 can detonate in episode 300, and talk is the cheapest thing to produce. Status conferred by witness, which makes a private act permanent and socially catastrophic. Debt-for-victory, so nothing is ever won free. A bounded countable collection with the number announced up front. An office with a docket, so strangers can bring problems weekly. Honorific friction, which re-teaches rank in every line of dialogue for free. A rite performed aloud. Ceremony generally — hierarchy enacted in cadence and address rather than described.

**Observed failure modes, and the rule each produced.** Cast bloat and the greeting tax → cap the roster and bench most of it. Stakes inflation → escalate proximity, betrayal-intimacy, publicity, irreversibility, and moral compromise instead. A protagonist who outgrows every threat → fixed power slots, scheduled failures, and jeopardy aimed at people she cannot replace. Arcs that stop closing → close three for every one opened. Physical law revoked as a reward → three inviolable laws, never excepted. Consent laundering → symptoms are never consent, and the bond is the problem the characters fight, never the mechanism that excuses a scene. Tension discharged early → consummate when it satisfies, but keep completion a separate, spoken, still-unmade decision. Comedy landing on the out-line → humor between 20% and 70% of an episode, never at 95%. Ambient misery with no forward motion → no mood episodes; interiority capped near 9%, in three or four hits of 15–40 words, each triggered by something happening in the room.

**The sentence for above the door:** *a listener who quits after any episode should feel they got something; a listener who quits after any mini-arc should feel they got a story.*

## Archetype self-checks

Required before the codex is finalized. One entry per principal and recurring character, naming the
shared archetype in use, the nearest source character they could be mistaken for, and **at least
three concrete departures**. Where no nearest source character exists, the archetype is still
recorded. Screened against `docs/originality-blacklist.md`; ordinary given names are not audited.

### Principals

**June Havlik** — archetype: the reluctant bride / outsider entering a supernatural political system.
- Nearest source character: Merit (Neill, *Some Girls Bite*), with a secondary echo of Kate Reilly (Adams & Clamp).
- Departures: (1) she is thirty-one with a precarious trade rather than an interrupted doctorate, and is never turned or transformed at any point; (2) her lineage is inherited but administratively erased, so she is powerless by *paperwork* rather than by newness — the opposite of Merit, who is granted a rank and a sword in week one; (3) she acquires no supernatural capability of any kind across the whole series, and her instruments are wording, records, and witnesses; (4) her defining skill is occupational listening, and her arc turns on choosing an obligation rather than on learning to accept a title.

**Elias Marrow** — archetype: the brooding immortal bound to serve a ruling house; the enforcer who falls for his assignment.
- Nearest source character: Charles Cornick (Briggs, *Cry Wolf*), with echoes of Bill Compton's officer-of-a-jurisdiction role.
- Departures: (1) turned in 1868 at thirty-one and one hundred fifty-eight years old, against Charles's two hundred years and status as the first born rather than made; (2) he is a *document officer*, not an assassin — his violence is procedural, performed by reading clauses aloud, and he has never been physically formidable; (3) his origin wound is a mill burn and a career of correct filings, not a mother's death in childbirth; (4) his inciting act is a legal self-exposure that strips his own protections, which has no analogue in the source; (5) he is deliberately never the physical protector — she is not carried, rescued by force, or shielded in combat.

**Marta Havlik** — archetype: the scheming matriarch / the leader who sacrifices one for many.
- Nearest source character: Mary Connolly, the pack leader (Adams & Clamp), with a note that the blacklist flags "Mary plus pack authority" specifically.
- Departures: (1) her authority is *elected and revocable* — she is a business agent with a constituency, not an alpha with dominance; (2) she is sixty-four, human-scaled, and can be voted out, which happens on the page; (3) her grievance is a burial fund and three hundred eleven memberships, not territory or breeding rights; (4) she loses her office as part of the season's price, which no source matriarch does.

**Constance Marrow** — archetype: the ancient sovereign of a vampire house.
- Nearest source character: Amelie the Founder (Caine, *Glass Houses*), with echoes of Celina (Neill) and structurally of Rice's custodial ancients.
- Departures: (1) she *deliberately presents as sixty* because authority reads better on grey, inverting the genre's universal youth-presentation; (2) she is not a monarch but a **trustee** — her power is fiduciary, exercised through instruments and filings, and she can be sued; (3) turned in 1631 at forty-four, avoiding every age and era in the corpus; (4) she is explicitly sincere and not cruel, and the canon forbids revealing her as a hidden villain — the source sovereigns are all ultimately revealed as predators managing livestock; (5) she is the *drafter* of the antagonist document rather than its enforcer.

**Cyd Toomey** — archetype: the loyal human best friend / comic confidant.
- Nearest source character: Jessica (Davidson, *Undead and Unwed*) and Mallory (Neill) — the blacklist flags Mallory-as-blue-haired-sorceress.
- Departures: (1) she has no supernatural nature, latent or revealed, ever — a hard canon commitment against the genre's default; (2) she is June's *employer*, so the friendship carries a payroll and a material conflict rather than pure emotional support; (3) she is forty-one and running a nine-person small business, not a wealthy twenty-something with unlimited resources — she absorbs a countable financial loss instead of writing a cheque; (4) her comic register is administrative competence rather than zaniness.

**Tess Renn** — archetype: the bereaved avenger who recruits the protagonist.
- Nearest source character: no close match in the corpus. Distantly Asil (Briggs) as a mate-bereaved figure, and structurally the "hostile tutor" function Helen performs for Neill.
- Departures from Asil: (1) she is human, thirty-eight, and mortal, against a thirteen-hundred-year-old wolf; (2) her instrument is an eleven-year legal case requiring a living plaintiff, not vengeance by violence; (3) she is honest about wanting to use June and states the risk out loud, where the archetype conventionally conceals its intentions; (4) her loss is a sister killed by a clause, not a mate killed by a witch.

**Milo Havlik** — archetype: the eager young kinsman; the loyal-but-dangerous relative.
- Nearest source character: no close match. Closest is the indiscreet-informant function Willie McCoy performs for Hamilton, and the younger-sibling energy of Neill's Jeff Christopher.
- Departures: (1) he is family, initiated, and *higher-standing* than the protagonist, which reverses the usual mentor-ward gradient; (2) his indiscretion causes real, traced, irreversible damage rather than serving as comic relief; (3) he becomes collateral — bound as legal surety — making him a plot object without ever losing agency; (4) twenty-four, in a job he is bad at, with no supernatural competence to compensate.

### Recurring characters

**Aloysius Pratt** — archetype: the officious functionary who administers the rules. Nearest source character: Helen, the House liaison (Neill). Departures: (1) he is human and permanently barred from elevation, which is his tragedy rather than his function; (2) he genuinely loves *drafting* and can be exploited through professional vanity; (3) he helps the protagonist repeatedly for craft reasons, not agenda; (4) twenty-two years of being passed over gives him a want of his own that Helen entirely lacks.

**Yolanda Bright** — archetype: the keeper of records who knows everything. Nearest source character: loosely Yolanda's function overlaps Neill's institutional-explainer role and Harris's small-town gossip network. Departures: (1) her knowledge is *documentary* and she is the one falsifying the document; (2) she has already refused an order once and has no leverage left, which makes her compromised rather than wise; (3) she is a warm, food-pushing lodge secretary rather than a mystic or an archivist.

**Dr. Owen Shaw** — archetype: the doctor who treats supernatural injuries. Nearest source character: Samuel Cornick (Briggs) and Havers (Ward). Departures: (1) he is human and has never been told what he treats; (2) he has constructed an elaborate personal fiction to preserve his own deniability — the opposite of an initiate; (3) his jeopardy is a medical license, not a lineage duty; (4) he is not attached to the protagonist romantically or by blood in any way.

**Father Bram Delisle** — archetype: the priest as moral counterweight to immortals. Nearest source character: Father Edgar Hummer (Feehan, *Dark Prince*). Departures: (1) he is procedurally load-bearing, not merely a confessor — his signature is legally required, which makes him a *mechanism* rather than a conscience; (2) he has buried four of the six brides he witnessed and considers himself an accomplice; (3) he refuses his office on the page and thereby causes the dark night, actively harming the protagonist while trying to help her; (4) no vampire confides in him and he offers no absolution to anyone.

**Vera Odell** — archetype: the witch who trades in bonds. Nearest source character: Mariposa (Briggs, *Cry Wolf*).
